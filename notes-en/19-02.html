19-02 Improper integrals with a parameter: uniform convergence returns

Improper integrals with a parameter: uniform convergence returns

1 · Motivation

Improper integrals with a parameter, and uniform convergence comes back. Last episode, a closed rectangle bought us three swaps. Now let one side run off forever. This new object is an improper integral with a parameter.

Our star example: a fading sine wave, one for each x. For each positive x, one substitution turns every wave into the same wave. So the answer is pi over two times the sign of x, a value we earn at the end. The integrand is continuous, and still the answer jumps; last episode's promise breaks.

Every x converges on its own, each at its own pace. Think of booking one study room slot that suits the whole group. Each x has its own cutoff, and past it the tail stays small. Uniform convergence asks for one cutoff serving every x at once. Near zero there is always one roommate who arrives later, the later the meeting starts.

Keep any small distance from zero, and the convergence is uniform. All the way down to zero it fails, and the trouble keeps marching toward zero. So what exactly has to be uniform, and how do we check it?

3 · Method

The Weierstrass M test: trap everything under one integrable ceiling. Its series face: each term under a number, and those numbers sum. Example: this time the letters swap roles, and y does the sliding. The ceiling integrates to pi over two, so it is uniform for every real y.

The Dirichlet and Abel tests, built for oscillating integrands. Away from zero, the partial integrals of the sine share one bound. The other factor slides down to zero and ignores x, so it is uniform. Near zero the bound is lost; the partial integrals grow as large as you like. A failed condition proves nothing alone; the window did the real proving.

Abel's example: sine over x, times a fading exponential. The sine integral converges, and with no y inside, it is automatically uniform. The fading factor only goes down and stays between zero and one, so Abel says uniform. Keep this one in mind; the final payoff runs on it. The proofs copy their chapter thirteen twins, so the course text skips them, and so do we.

Back to our example: any closed piece inside keeps a distance from zero. So it converges uniformly on every closed subinterval of the open half line. Closed pieces carry the guarantee, because continuity and derivatives only look nearby. Every positive point fits inside a closed piece, so the function is continuous there.

Theorems nineteen point ten and eleven: continuity, then derivatives. Read the conditions in two columns: what only converges, what must be uniform. The original integral only has to converge, nothing more. What must be uniform is the integral of the derivative. The classic accident: checking the original integral, then stopping. In our example the original is uniform, but the differentiated integral diverges. Yet the true derivative is zero; the careless swap gives nothing at all.

4 · Outlook

Payoff time: a fading difference of two sines, integrated out to infinity. The key move: write that difference as an integral of cosine in y. The M test gives uniformity, so we may exchange the order of integration. The inner integral is a simple fraction; one more integration gives two arctangents.

Drop the second sine, then let the fading slowly disappear. We may slide all the way to zero, because Abel gives uniform convergence there. Take the limit, and the Dirichlet integral arrives: pi over two times the sign of b. Rename the letter, and it is our opening example, jump and all. The jump itself is the proof: starting from zero, it cannot be uniform.

Last episode's three swaps are back, now paid for with uniform convergence. In nineteen oh three, Gamma and Beta are tamed by exactly these tests. And nineteen oh four ends with exactly such a differentiation under the integral sign.

Later on, the Laplace and Fourier transforms are integrals of exactly this kind. Every swap made there runs on today's guarantee. In one line: uniform means one cutoff serving every x at once. When differentiating, check the derivative's integral, not the original.
