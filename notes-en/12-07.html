12-07 Rearrangement: change the order of adding, pick any answer

Rearrangement: change the order of adding, pick any answer

1 · Motivation

Change only the order of adding, and watch the answer move. Same handful of coins, dropped into the machine in a new order. The little screen keeps a running total, and that is all you see. With a finite pile, every order lands on the same total. With endless coins, the total is wherever the screen settles.

Meet our old friend, the alternating harmonic series. It converges by the Leibniz test, and we call its sum A. And A is not zero: the partial sums pin it between seven twelfths and five sixths.

Halve every term, and we get half of A. That step is allowed: scale every term, and the sum scales too. Slide each halved term under a negative term of A, and fill the gaps with zeros. The zeros change nothing; the running total just pauses.

Now add the two rows, one column at a time. In the odd columns, the positive terms of A fall straight through. Column two: the two halves cancel out. Column four: two negative quarters pile up into a half. The rhythm repeats: one column cancels, the next one merges. Drop the zeros, and the bottom row adds up to three halves of A. This is the course text's own computation, word for word. Same terms, each used once, and yet the sum grew by half. Since A is not zero, the two answers truly differ.

2 · Statements

Did we cheat? First check: is this really a rearrangement?
A rearrangement uses every term exactly once; only the order changes.
Positive terms: 1, 1/3, 1/5, 1/7, … — every odd reciprocal, in order.
Negative terms: −1/2, −1/4, −1/6, … — every even reciprocal, once each.
Two positives, then one negative: only the order of accumulation changed.
Finite sums never cared about order: the commutative law covers them.
Recall 12-01: a series' sum is the limit of its partial sums.
Change the order, and you get a different sequence of partial sums.
A new sequence is free to have a new limit.
Same coins, different running display — and the display was the total.
The course text states it plainly in a note.
Even if a rearrangement converges, it need not keep the original sum.
Suitably rearranged, it can even diverge.
Or converge to any number chosen in advance.
Is any series safe from this? Yes: the absolutely convergent ones.
Theorem 12.13: an absolutely convergent series may be rearranged freely.
First promise: the rearranged series again converges absolutely.

Second, its sum stays exactly the same. So why can a conditionally convergent series be steered anywhere?

3 · Method

This steering argument is a standard extra, not from the course text. From the last episode: positive terms in one bag, negative ones in another. With conditional convergence, both of these series diverge. In plain words, each bag alone adds up to infinity. And since the series converges, its terms shrink toward zero. Pick a target, one point five, like a playlist that must end right at the bell.

Below the target, we draw from the positive bag until we pass it. Three positive terms, and we are just past the target. That is the last episode's fact: the positive bag never runs dry, so we always cross. Above the target, switch to the negative bag until we drop below. One negative term is enough to drop us below. And the negative bag never runs dry either. Repeat: up past the target, back below, again and again.

Each run takes at least one term, so every term is used sooner or later. How far past can we land? No farther than the last term added. The terms shrink to zero, so the misses shrink too. The partial sums close in on the target, zigzag by zigzag. The playlist ends right on the bell: this new order sums to one point five. Any other target works the same way, even a negative one.

Now aim at infinity: targets one, two, three, one after another. Past each target, drop in one negative term, then climb again. Each dip is at most a half, so the partial sums still run off to infinity. Same terms, and this time the sum runs away, just as the note said.

Now the safe side: why is absolute convergence immune? The proof has two steps. Step one: series with no negative terms. Any first stretch of the new series hides inside a first stretch of the old one. No term is negative, so the new partial sums never exceed the old sum. Rising and bounded, so it converges, at most to the old sum. Run it backwards, since each is a rearrangement of the other, and the sums match.

Step two, any signs: split each term into two parts, as last episode did. Absolute convergence makes both of those series converge. Reorder the terms, and both parts are reordered the same way. Step one finishes the job: the sum is unchanged, and so is absolute convergence.

Absolutely convergent: the order of adding does not matter. Conditionally convergent: the order is a control knob. Turn it, and you get any target, or a sum that runs away. Exam trap: convergence alone buys you no protection. Only absolute convergence is protected, and three halves of A proves it. Even when the reordered series converges, it may land somewhere else. And divergence is on the menu too: remember the run to infinity.

4 · Outlook

Where does this matter? Start with expected values. Expectation adds up each outcome, weighted by its chance. But outcomes come in no natural order: which do you add first? So expectation is defined only under absolute convergence. Then no shuffling of the outcomes can change the answer. In later courses, swapping two summation signs leans on the same condition.

Recap: last episode's two bottomless bags fueled the steering. And from the partial sums episode: a sum is a limit, and that is why order matters. One debt remains: the value of A, which the Taylor series episode will reveal. For conditional series, the order is part of the answer.
