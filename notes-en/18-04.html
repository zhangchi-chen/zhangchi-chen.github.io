18-04 Tangent planes and normal vectors: the gradient reappears

Tangent planes and normal vectors: the gradient reappears

1 · Motivation

Tangent planes and normal vectors, where the gradient comes back. Tonight's homework asks for the tangent plane of this ellipsoid at one point. Chapter seventeen's recipe only works when the surface is a graph over the floor.

Solve for it, and a square root appears with two possible signs. Keep the top half and grind out the two partial derivatives by hand. A full page of scratch work later, the plane finally comes out. Now slide the point down to the equator, where the height is zero. The height sits in a denominator, and the derivative blows up. Yet the picture clearly has a tangent plane there, standing straight up like a wall. What we want is a recipe that reads the plane straight off the equation. And the answer is an old friend from episode seventeen four, the gradient.

2 · Statements

A plane curve F(x, y) = 0 at P₀: its tangent line and normal line.
(F_x, F_y) is the normal vector; the tangent line is perpendicular to it.
The normal line runs along that vector, through the same point.
A space curve with parameter t: its tangent line and normal plane.
The derivatives x′, y′, z′ are the direction numbers of the tangent line.
The normal plane passes through P₀, perpendicular to that tangent line.
A surface F(x, y, z) = 0 at P₀: its tangent plane and normal line.
(F_x, F_y, F_z) is the normal vector; the tangent plane is perpendicular to it.
The normal line follows that vector; equal ratios simply mean parallel.
Two equation shapes: a zero dot product means perpendicular, equal ratios mean parallel.

First the plane curve: the course text gives its tangent line and its normal line. The two partials form the normal vector, and the tangent line meets it at a right angle. The normal line follows that vector straight through the point.

Next, a curve in space traced out by a parameter: tangent line and normal plane. The three derivatives are the direction numbers; they point along the tangent line. The normal plane goes through the point, at a right angle to the tangent line.

Then the surface: the course text gives its tangent plane and its normal line. The three partials form the normal vector, and the tangent plane sits square to it. The normal line follows that vector, and equal ratios just mean pointing the same way. Every formula has one of two shapes: zero dot product for perpendicular, equal ratios for parallel.

3 · Method

Why normal? Walk along the curve, and the function never leaves zero. So its differential is zero, just as in episode eighteen two. Read it as a dot product, and the gradient is at right angles to the curve. The gradient from episode seventeen four is back, this time as pure geometry. The course text's first example: the folium of Descartes, at one point on its loop. At that point the two partials are fifteen and minus twelve. Plug them in, and the tangent line and the normal line drop out together. On the picture, the tangent hugs the loop and the normal cuts straight across it. But at the origin both partials are zero, and two branches cross each other. There is no single tangent there; the recipe needs a gradient that is not zero.

On a curve in space, first draw a secant to a nearby point. Divide its direction by the step in t, then shrink that step to zero. One curve, two gadgets: a tangent line running along it, a normal plane cutting across it. For a simple cubic curve at parameter minus one, the direction numbers are one, minus two, three. From there the tangent line and the normal plane follow at once.

If the last partial is not zero, the surface is locally a graph over the floor. Episode eighteen two hands us both partials of the hidden function. Feed them into the chapter seventeen plane, and clear the denominator. Everything gathers into the course text's tangent plane, and the normal vector appears. Now the three variables are equals; any single partial that isn't zero will do.

The course text says it plainly: the gradient is exactly the normal of the level surface. Why? Draw any curve on the surface through the point; the function stays zero along it. The chain rule says the gradient is at right angles to every one of their tangents. So all those tangents lie flat in one plane, and that plane is the tangent plane. And the debt from episode seventeen four is paid: gradients meet level surfaces at right angles.

The course text's third example is our homework problem from the start. At that point the gradient comes out as two, four, six. Two steps of work, and the plane is done. The same plane as the homework, and not a single square root in sight. Quick check: the point itself sits on the plane. The normal line points along one, two, three, the gradient cut in half. And what about the equator point that broke the square root? There the gradient lies flat, the plane stands straight up, and nothing blows up. But where the gradient is zero, the formula collapses into an empty statement. The tip of a cone is exactly such a point, and no plane fits it there. For a graph, the normal is the two slopes and minus one, and we land back in chapter seventeen.

A curve can also arrive as the meeting line of two surfaces, with no parameter given. The course text's second example: a sphere and a cone, meeting at three, four, five. Each surface has its own normal at that point, one gradient apiece. The curve lives on both surfaces, so its tangent must be square to both normals. Picture the two tangent planes as corridor walls; the seam where they meet is our tangent. The direction square to both is their cross product, read with the right hand rule. The course text writes the three components as Jacobian determinants.

These are the Jacobians of episode eighteen three, cashed in at last. Plug in the point, and both gradients are in hand. The three Jacobians come out minus one sixty, one twenty, and zero. Mind the order: the letters cycle around, and swapping a pair flips the sign. So the tangent line is written with a zero in the last denominator. A zero denominator just means the height never moves; the curve is a level circle. The normal plane simplifies to a clean vertical plane. It holds the vertical axis, as a circle's normal plane always does. That zero fits too: the height never changes here, so it cannot steer the other two.

Three kinds of objects, all in one table. In every row, the key direction comes from derivatives, a gradient, or a cross product. Here is the naming trap: a curve owns a normal plane, while a surface owns a tangent plane. It is like two campus buildings whose names pair with the opposite word, so freshmen get lost. Rule of thumb: the word tangent goes to the piece of the same dimension as the object.

4 · Outlook

Where does this show up? In every single frame of a three D phone game. How bright a patch looks depends on the angle between the light and its normal. When a surface is given by an equation, the renderer takes the gradient as its normal. Second use: two surfaces meet at the same angle as their normals do. For our sphere and cone, the dot product of the two normals is exactly zero. So they cross at a right angle, all the way around the circle.

Still owed: episode twenty three uses the unit tangent to link the two kinds of line integral. And chapter twenty two will choose normal directions across whole surfaces. Not every surface plays along: carry a normal around a Möbius band, and it returns flipped. And Stokes' theorem, in chapter twenty two, runs on exactly the surfaces that pass this test. One idea runs through everything today: the gradient stands at right angles to its level set.
