23-01 Vector functions: the derivative is a matrix

Vector functions: the derivative is a matrix

1 · Motivation

Today, the derivative stops being a single number. Back in twenty-one oh three, polar coordinates bent a neat grid into rings. Each small cell's area grew by about r times. Why r? It comes from the derivative of this map. But what is that, exactly?

With one variable, the derivative was a single number, the slope. Oh five oh eight replaced the change by a linear part: one number times the step. In seventeen oh one, with two inputs, it became a pair of numbers. The polar map takes two numbers in and gives two numbers out.

That makes four partial derivatives. Which one is the derivative? A map from the plane into four dimensions has eight. How do we arrange them? Your roommate writes them in one long row. You stack them in a column. Same eight numbers. Is the shape just taste, or is it forced on us? Spoiler: the derivative is a whole spreadsheet of slopes. Oh five oh eight promised more linear maps than slopes. Today it comes true.

Here is the definition, exactly as the course text gives it. The domain is open, so we can step away in any direction. On the left is the true change, an arrow among the outputs. Next comes the prediction: the linear transformation applied to the step. And whatever is left over is the remainder, the small term.

Shrink the step, and the remainder shrinks even faster than the step does. The remainder is an arrow, so each of its components must be that small. One linear transformation per base point, whichever way we step. The prediction is called the differential, and the map itself is the derivative.

Zoom in close, and the curved map and its linear stand-in can't be told apart. This is the tangent plane idea, promoted from a surface to a whole map. With one input and one output, the matrix shrinks to a single slope. With two inputs and one output, the matrix is one row: the pair from seventeen oh one. In general it's a table: one row per output, one column per input.

The course text spells out every entry. This is the Jacobian matrix. Each row belongs to one output, each column to one input. Why this layout? Because multiplying the step must produce the change. A column of inputs goes in, and a column of outputs comes out. So rows must be outputs. The layout was never a matter of taste. The vector function is differentiable exactly when each component is. Each row is the gradient of one component. The matrix is a stack of gradients.

3 · Method

Course text example one: a map from the plane into four dimensions. Four outputs and two inputs, so expect four rows and two columns. Rows one and two: take the partial derivatives of the first two components. Rows three and four go exactly the same way. That zero says the third output ignores the first input. Now plug in the point where both inputs are one. And here is the derivative at that point: four rows, two columns, plain numbers. Read down a column: nudge only the first input, and watch all four outputs respond.

But here comes the trap. Writing down the matrix proves nothing yet. The course text warns us in plain words. Try a function whose second component hides a famous troublemaker. At the origin, all four partial derivatives exist, and the matrix can be written. Yet along the diagonal, the second component stays at one half. So the function isn't even continuous at the origin, let alone differentiable.

The rescue is a sufficient condition: continuous partial derivatives. Put everything on one ladder, every arrow pointing one way only. Differentiable gives you two things for free: continuity, and existing partials. But the arrow from existing partials up to differentiable is crossed out. And none of the arrows on this ladder can be reversed.

Next comes the chain rule. Picture chaining travel adapters on a trip abroad. The chain rule, in the course text's words. First, check the sizes of the two matrices. The middle sizes meet like a plug in its socket, and the outer sizes remain. Swap the order, and in general the sizes refuse to fit. So the size match is a free sanity check, every single time.

Unpack one case: a single row times a two by two matrix. The first entry comes out as two products added together. And that is exactly the tree diagram from seventeen oh three. That tree was a matrix product, unpacked one number at a time. Remember the form invariance of the first differential? Feed in the inner differential, and the product of the two matrices appears. That old magic trick was the shadow of this matrix identity.

When inputs and outputs match in number, the square matrix has one special number, its determinant. Its size tells how much the map stretches tiny areas near the point. For polar coordinates the determinant is exactly r, the factor from twenty-one oh three. Every change of variables in twenty-one oh three and oh four used this very number. Eighteen oh three called it an exchange rate. Now we see the whole rate board behind it.

So how do you actually differentiate a vector function? Differentiate each component with the tools from chapters sixteen to eighteen. Then stack the rows. There are no new rules, only new bookkeeping. And here, the bookkeeping is the whole point.

4 · Outlook

First use: in a physics lab, you measure a distance and an angle. The report wants Cartesian coordinates, and the Jacobian matrix carries your errors across. Second use: a desk lamp with two hinges turns two angles into a head position. Its Jacobian matrix turns how fast each hinge turns into how fast the head moves. Three old debts are paid: the slope, the pair and the tree are one matrix. In twenty-three oh two, a nonzero determinant grows into the inverse and implicit function theorems.

Further on, in normed spaces, the matrix gives way to a bounded linear operator. The definition survives word for word. That is the road into functional analysis. Stack the second derivatives, and you get the Hesse matrix of the course text's next section. It is the extremum test of seventeen oh six, dressed up as a matrix. The derivative was a linear map all along. With many variables, it wears the shape of a matrix.
