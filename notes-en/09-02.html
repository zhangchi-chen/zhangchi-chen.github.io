Integrability: the squeeze between upper and lower sums

1 · Motivation

This time, two fences squeeze a whole crowd of sums. The definition from last time hides a to-do list that never ends. Swap the partition, swap the sample points, and the sums never stop coming.

Picture selling your old textbook in the class chat: quotes keep pouring in. You don't need every quote, just keep an eye on the top one and the bottom one. Any deal lands between those two, and once they move close, the price is settled. Now a puzzle: two functions, and each one breaks at infinitely many points. One of them is integrable and the other is not. So what is the real difference?

Fix the partition, then on each little piece find the supremum and the infimum. Raise each column to the supremum and add them up: that is the upper sum. Use the infimum instead, and the same recipe gives the lower sum. As a pair, these two are called the Darboux sums. Here is how the course text puts it, word for word. Pick any sample point you like: its column always stays between the two fences. So however you sample, the Riemann sum cannot escape the two fences. And the fence is tight: push each sample toward the top, and the sum creeps up to the upper sum. Two numbers do the work of endless samples, like the top and bottom quote.

Being integrable comes down to this: the two fences can be squeezed together. This is the integrability criterion, Theorem 9.3 in the course text. For each epsilon, a single partition that works is all it asks for. Surprisingly, one is enough; every partition fine enough then falls in line. How much the function swings on a piece is called its oscillation. The gap between the fences is oscillation times width, piece by piece, added up. Said another way: squash the little blocks' total area below epsilon.

3 · Method

Now drop in one extra partition point and watch the fences. The upper fence can only come down, the lower one can only go up. That's because a half piece can never reach higher than the whole piece. Keep adding points, and the two fences slowly close in. Merge the two partitions and you see it: a lower sum never climbs above an upper sum.

Push the upper sums as low as they will go: that floor is the upper integral. Lift the lower sums as high as they go: that ceiling is the lower integral. Now meet the Dirichlet function: one at every rational, zero at every irrational. Zoom in as far as you like: both kinds of points are always there. So the upper sum is always one, and the lower sum is always zero. Pile on as many points as you like, and the gap does not budge. Upper integral one, lower integral zero: the Dirichlet function is not integrable.

Three families of functions each have their own trick for shrinking that gap. First class: functions continuous on the whole closed interval. Uniform continuity guarantees it: cut finely enough and every piece barely swings. Every block stays under that line, so together they stay under epsilon. Second class: bounded functions that break at only finitely many points. Wrap every break point inside its own tiny interval. The swing there is big, but the width is next to nothing. Everywhere else the function is continuous, and the first trick takes over.

Third class: functions that only rise, or only fall, across the interval. When it rises, each piece swings by exactly how much the right end beats the left. Slide all the blocks into one column, and they stack up to the total rise, exactly. No matter how you cut, the swings always add up to that same total rise. That column is never wider than the mesh, so the gap shrinks along with it. Shrink the mesh and the gap shrinks with it, so these functions are integrable.

Here comes a friendlier version: the third condition, Theorem 9.16. Set a threshold epsilon and watch the pieces that swing at least that much. Their combined width has to fit under any eta you choose. It works like a course's late days: spend them on any assignment, but the total is capped. Add more points, and the red stretch around the jump keeps getting narrower. Large swings are fine, as long as they take up very little room in total.

Back to the opening puzzle: the Riemann function. The bigger the denominator, the lower the dot, and every irrational sits at zero. Every piece holds an irrational point, so the lower sum is always zero. Only a handful of dots stand as tall as epsilon or taller. Give each one a tiny interval, and keep the total width under eta. Everywhere else the dots stay under the line, so the swing stays under epsilon. The condition holds, so the Riemann function is integrable, and its integral is zero. Infinitely many breaks, and still integrable: the exact opposite of the Dirichlet function. What separates them is how much room the big swings take up in total.

4 · Outlook

One last trap: chain two integrable functions together and see what happens. Continuous outside, integrable inside: the composition is still integrable. The idea: the outer function's uniform continuity, teamed with the third condition inside. Where the inside barely moves, the composition barely moves either. Where the inside swings hard, the total width was tiny to begin with. But take away the outer continuity, and the whole thing falls apart.

Take a function that drops only at zero: one break, clearly integrable. Now feed the Riemann function into it. Rationals between zero and one land off zero, so out comes one. Irrationals land right on zero, so out comes zero. What comes out is the Dirichlet function in disguise, and it is not integrable. The inside herds infinitely many points straight into the outside's single break. It is the same bug we met in chapter four: one bad point, visited again and again.

First payoff: its absolute value and its square stay integrable. Second payoff: a bike fare that jumps up each new minute is integrable, twice over. And next episode's engine runs on exactly this: continuous functions are integrable. These fences are old friends from the completeness chapter, squeezing once again.

Down the road, real analysis squeezes this whole episode into a single sentence. A bounded function is integrable exactly when its break points form a null set. One breaks at every point; the other only at rational points, and that set is small enough. In the end, integrability is all about oscillation. And composition needs a continuous outer function to keep the oscillation in check.
