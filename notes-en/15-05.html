Auxiliary functions: build the function that computes your series

1 · Motivation

Today we build a function with one job: summing a series. Keep adding the reciprocal squares, and the total creeps toward 1.6449. That number is pi squared over six, so where does pi come from? The link runs through the squaring function you already know.

Picture the vending machine downstairs in your dorm. First, decide the snack: which series do you want summed? Then find the machine that stocks it, the auxiliary function. Last, press the right button, the point where we evaluate.

The whole logic fits on one recipe card, four steps. Step one: read the signs and the denominators of the target. Step two: guess the function from four familiar shapes. Parity picks the kind of series: even means cosines, odd means sines. Step three: if you can, pick a point where the function is continuous. At a jump, the series lands halfway between the two sides, not on the formula. Step four, only if needed: add two series together, term by term. We will run this card four times, faster each time.

3 · Method

Run one, the flagship: every sign is plus, and squares sit below. Squares with no gaps: that is the squaring machine. But the coefficients alternate in sign, so the cosines must cancel that. Press pi: each cosine flips sign exactly like the coefficient, and the signs cancel. But first, the safety check: is the extended function continuous at pi? Both sides arrive at pi squared, so the curve is glued continuously. Only the slope breaks there, and the convergence theorem is fine with a corner. So at pi, the whole series equals pi squared. So the reciprocal squares sum to pi squared over six.

One fish, two meals: same machine, a different button, zero. Check it: zero sits in the smooth middle of the parabola, so it is safe. At zero every cosine is one, so only the alternating signs remain. The machine now says the whole series adds up to zero. So the alternating version sums to pi squared over twelve. The trap is assuming safety; both buttons had to pass the check, and we said why. Skip the check with the identity function and it breaks, because pi is a jump. Plug in blindly and you get pi equals zero; the series really gives the midpoint, zero.

Run two: plus signs again, but only odd squares sit below. Missing even terms point to absolute value, whose even coefficients are all zero. Press zero: it is a corner of the triangle wave, but the wave stays continuous. At zero the constant and the sum must cancel exactly. So odd squares sum to pi squared over eight.

Run three: alternating odd reciprocals, the Leibniz series from episode twelve five. First powers below point to an extension with jumps: the identity function. Press half pi, safely inside: the sines cycle one, zero, minus one, zero. The even terms vanish, and the odd ones alternate in sign. So Leibniz's series is pi over four, an old friend re-derived in seconds.

Run four, the signature move: the square wave, pressed at a third of pi. A third of pi lands on a flat stretch of the wave, so it is safe. Now watch the sine values as n climbs: the pattern repeats every six. Multiples of three land on the horizontal axis, so their sine is exactly zero. It works like a filter key that snaps a whole spreadsheet column to zero. Survivors carry root three over two, signs alternating. So the wave's height is that factor times the new series. Divide through, and the new series is root three pi over six. That zero column is the vanishing block of episode fifteen four, hired as a tool. One well chosen point deleted a whole arithmetic progression from the series.

A second flavor: the same square wave, pressed at a sixth of pi. Now the sines take halves and whole ones, repeating every twelve. Multiples of three now carry plus or minus one: a shrunken copy of Leibniz's series. That copy is a third of Leibniz's series, so we already know its value. The rest carry a half, so they form half of a new series. So the wave's height is half the new series plus the known copy. Subtract the copy, and the new series comes out to pi over three. Two sieves gave two new values, and the notes check both numerically.

4 · Outlook

Is it luck that every guess keeps working? Not at all. Uniqueness: two such functions with the same coefficients agree wherever both are continuous. So once a guessed function has the right coefficients, its values are the sums. Two routes to one series must agree: the square wave at half pi gives Leibniz too. A cheap cross check: removing the even terms leaves three quarters, as it should.

A second line squares the coefficients and reaches values this one cannot: Parseval's equality, in fifteen six. Owed to fifteen six: zeta four, beta three, an L value, and why zeta three resists. Everything today ran on the convergence theorem of fifteen two and the zero blocks of fifteen four. Want to delete multiples of five? Press a fifth of pi, and exactly those terms vanish.

Build a function whose expansion you know, then read off the constant you want. That move opens analytic number theory and the integral transform methods of later courses. Snack, machine, button: series, function, point.
