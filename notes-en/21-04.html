21-04 Triple integrals: project, slice, and spherical coordinates

Triple integrals: project, slice, and spherical coordinates

1 · Motivation

Here is a solid dome, and its density changes from point to point. How heavy is it? We want one single number. Back in episode 21 01, a flat plate was weighed by a double integral. Give the plate some thickness, and the story climbs one floor up.

Picture a flat pack wardrobe that came with two instruction sheets. Sheet one says: fix every vertical rod first. Sheet two says: build each whole layer first. Same wardrobe, two plans, and one of them can be far shorter. And a ball in plain rectangular coordinates fills every bound with square roots. Round solids want spherical coordinates, and those bring a strange extra factor. By the end, that factor will be a picture you can see, not a line to memorize.

First the definition. Cut the solid into many small pieces. In each piece, pick any point and read off the density there. Density times volume, added over every piece, gives the integral sum. Let the pieces get finer and finer, and the sums settle on one number. That number is called the triple integral of f over the solid. Here is the definition, exactly as the course text writes it. Every way of cutting and every choice of points must work; luck plays no part. With density one everywhere, the triple integral is just the volume.

Plan one projects first: the solid sits over its shadow, between a floor and a ceiling. A vertical line enters the solid through the floor. And it leaves through the ceiling. Here is the project first formula, as the course text states it. It is the same iterated integral machine from episode 21 02, with one more variable.

Plan two slices first: cut the solid at one height and look at the cross section. First integrate over that whole slice, so each height gets one number. Then stack the layers from the lowest height to the highest. Here is the slice first formula, as the course text states it.

3 · Method

Watch project first at work: one vertical column at a time. The inner integral weighs that one column, from floor to ceiling. The outer double integral sweeps that column across the whole shadow. It wins when the floor and the ceiling are each a single formula over the shadow. If a vertical line pierces the boundary four times, cut the solid into pieces first.

Slice first wins when every slice is a shape whose area you already know. Here is the course text's example: three square terms over a whole ellipsoid. The first term only cares about x, so slice straight across that axis. Each slice is an ellipse, and its area shrinks toward the two tips. Weigh each slice, add along the axis, and this term gives four fifteenths of pi a b c. Slice along the other two axes, and each remaining term gives the same amount. In total, three equal pieces add up to four fifths of pi a b c. This is the slice and add idea from episode 10 01, back on home ground. Here is the rule of thumb: look at the solid first, then at the integrand. A clean floor and ceiling says project; easy slices, or density by height alone, says slice.

Now cylindrical coordinates: polar coordinates on the floor, and the height left alone. Here is how the course text writes cylindrical coordinates, Jacobian included. It is polar plus height, so the Jacobian is still r, just as in episode 21 03. Example: a bowl shaped by a paraboloid, closed by a flat lid at height 4. We integrate the squared distance from the central axis over the bowl. The angle goes all the way round; the radius stops where lid meets bowl. The integrand times the Jacobian gives r cubed, and the answer is eight pi over three. Nothing new to memorize: cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates with a height.

Spherical coordinates locate a point by one distance and two angles. The angle phi opens down from the north pole, from zero to pi. And theta sweeps around the axis, all the way round, as in polar coordinates. Here are spherical coordinates as the course text writes them, factor included.

So where does that factor come from? Let's build one small cell. Two nearby spheres give the cell its thickness. Two nearby cones cut an arc on a circle of radius r. Two half planes through the axis cut a short arc from a latitude circle. This latitude circle shrinks by a factor of sine phi. Multiply the three edges, and the volume of the cell appears, factor and all. Each angle edge brings one r, and the latitude circle brings the sine. Spin the globe in a geography app: latitude circles shrink as you near a pole. Near a pole the cells get thin, and the sine of phi shrinks to match. The Jacobian, worked out the long way, agrees exactly. Time for a sanity check: integrate one over a ball of radius R. The three pieces multiply to four thirds pi R cubed, the school formula exactly.

Now read the bounds off a picture: the distance runs from the origin to the far surface. This sphere passes through the origin, so its far edge depends only on phi. The cone is one fixed angle, so phi runs from zero up to it. And theta sweeps all the way around. Stack the three ranges, keep the factor, and the ice cream volume is set up. The answer looks like a ball's volume, trimmed by how wide the cone opens. Check: open the cone out flat, and the whole ball comes back.

First bug: the sine quietly turns into a cosine. Over the whole ball the cosine cancels itself out, and the ball has no volume. Second bug: measuring the angle up from the floor instead of down from the pole. Then the formulas send a point on the equator straight to the north pole. Before writing any bound, mark both angles on the picture. And keep the habit from episode 10 02: test a setup on the ball before trusting it.

4 · Outlook

First use: the mass of a solid is the integral of its density, our opening question answered. In episode 21 07, centroids and moments of inertia run on these same integrals. In episode 22 03, Gauss's formula turns the flow through a surface into these integrals. And in episode 23 02, this very determinant comes back as a test.

A starred section of the course text uses the same machine on balls of any dimension. Peel off one dimension at a time, and climb the ladder. And a probability density in several variables is integrated in exactly the same way. Project, slice, or go round: let the solid tell you which plan to follow. Same wardrobe, different plans, and now you know which instruction sheet to open first.
