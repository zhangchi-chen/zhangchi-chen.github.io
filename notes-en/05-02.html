05-02 Differentiable and continuous: joining up, but maybe not smoothly

Differentiable and continuous: joining up, but maybe not smoothly

1 · Motivation

Differentiable and continuous. Joining up, but maybe not smoothly. A problem set question. Is this glued function differentiable at zero? The two pieces meet at the origin, with no gap at all. A classmate writes: it is continuous at zero, therefore differentiable. Look closer. It arrives climbing from the left, and leaves flat to the right. Joined up, yes. But joined smoothly? That is the real question.

Between these two words run two arrows, one in each direction. The last episode left this open. Does differentiable force continuous? The classmate leaned on the reverse arrow, from continuous to differentiable. Spoiler. The first arrow holds, and the proof fits in a single line. The reverse arrow breaks, and it breaks in three different ways. A new tool, the one sided derivative, will name each failure for us.

2 · Statements

We start from the finite increment formula in the course text. Read it like a shop receipt that itemizes the whole change. Line one is what the tangent line predicts. Line two is a leftover, tiny even next to the step itself. Even with no step at all, the receipt still balances. It is just the definition in disguise, multiplied through by the step.

Theorem five point one. Differentiable at a point means continuous there. Continuity says only this: shrink the step, and the change dies. Differentiable asks for more: the ratio must settle on one number. So differentiable is the stronger promise, sitting inside continuous. The course text adds a note: differentiable is only a sufficient condition. The witness is the absolute value: continuous at zero, but sharply bent.

Definition two splits the limit into a right version and a left version. The right derivative lets the step shrink from the right side only. The left derivative does the same, approaching from the left side only. Theorem five point two ties the two halves back together. The derivative exists exactly when both sides exist and agree.

3 · Method

Now the one line proof. Take the receipt and let the step die. Both lines die: the tangent part, and the leftover even faster. So the change dies too, and that is exactly continuity. Bonus: the change dies at the pace of the step, trapped in a wedge.

Continuity only promises that the change dies. It says nothing about the rate. The change may die too slowly, slower than any straight line allows. Or too violently, swinging back and forth between two lines. Or with a different slope on each side, like the absolute value. So there are three ways to be continuous yet not differentiable.

Failure one is the corner, the absolute value at zero. Like a corridor with a sharp turn: you walk in one way and out another. From the right, every secant from the corner has slope one. From the left, slope minus one, every single time. Both sides exist, but they disagree, so there is no derivative.

Failure two is the vertical tangent: the square root at zero. It is continuous at zero: the root shrinks with the step. But the ratio grows without bound, and the secants stand straight up. Like a stairwell ladder too steep to walk: a tangent, but no slope. Note that zero is an endpoint here, so only the right side is in play.

Failure three is oscillation: a curve that wiggles faster and faster near zero. Still continuous at zero: two straight lines squeeze it shut. Its quotient boils down to a pure sine wobble. Like a washing machine on spin cycle, it rattles and never settles. It keeps hitting one and minus one, so no limit, and no derivative. Three portraits: the sides disagree, the ratio explodes, the ratio never settles. All three are continuous. Continuity alone is simply not enough.

Back to the homework. Piecewise joins are where this theorem lives. Step one, check continuity. Both pieces head to the same value. Why first? If it were not continuous, theorem five point one would settle it at once. Step two, the right side. The ratio shrinks to zero. Step three, the left side. The ratio is one, every single time. Step four, compare. They differ, so it is continuous but not differentiable. So the classmate loses the marks. A corner was hiding at zero. The habit to keep: continuity, right side, left side, then compare. Careful: a one sided derivative is not the limit of the derivative.

4 · Outlook

Use one: read the theorem backwards. No continuity, no derivative. Last episode already leaned on it, for the Dirichlet example away from zero. So last episode's debt is paid: differentiable forces continuous, never the reverse. Use two: a phone data plan, a flat fee up to the cap, then a price per gigabyte. At the cap the bill joins up, yet the slope jumps from zero to five. The two one sided derivatives are two prices: the last gigabyte free, the next one five.

Later, the derivative limit theorem will turn these joins into a shortcut. But step one stays: continuity at the join can never be skipped. Multiply by one more x, and the wobble is tamed: the derivative at zero is zero. Yet its derivative function breaks at zero, a story for chapter five, episode six. Chapter six will show a derivative never politely jumps. Continuous, or wildly broken.

A glimpse ahead. With two variables, this episode splits into two stories. Slopes along both axes can exist, while the surface tears at the origin. That surprise is why the two variable course rebuilds differentiability from scratch. Continuous means joined up. Differentiable means joined up smoothly.
