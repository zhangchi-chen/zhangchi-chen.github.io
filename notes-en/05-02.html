05-02 Differentiable and continuous: joining up, but maybe not smoothly

Differentiable and continuous: joining up, but maybe not smoothly

1 · Motivation

Differentiable and continuous: joined up, but maybe not smoothly. Last time, absolute value had no derivative at zero. Yet you can draw its graph without lifting the pen. So what does differentiable ask for, beyond continuous?

Picture walking down a corridor that folds sharply. Your feet never leave the floor; the path is unbroken. But at the fold, your heading changes all at once. Or a stairwell ladder: connected, but far too steep to walk. Or a washing machine on spin, rattling back and forth. All three stay connected, yet at one spot the direction is unclear.

First question: does differentiable force continuous? Second question: does continuous force differentiable? One is quick to prove; the other fails in three ways. This settles the question the last episode left open.

Start by rewriting the definition of the derivative. The finite increment formula splits the change in y into two terms. The first term is the rise along the tangent line. The second term is small even compared with the change in x. It even holds when the change in x is zero. Theorem five point one: differentiable at a point means continuous there. It runs one way only, and its proof is a single sentence.

To see how failures happen, look at each side separately. Definition two: the right derivative and the left derivative. Right derivative: the change in x shrinks through positive values only. Left derivative: the same limit, through negative values. Together they are the one-sided derivatives.

Theorem five point two: exactly when does the derivative exist? Both one-sided derivatives must exist, and the two values must be equal. The text adds: differentiable is only a sufficient condition for continuous. Absolute value is continuous at zero, yet not differentiable there.

3 · Method

Why must it be continuous? We need the change in y to vanish. Key move: itemize it like a receipt, with the finite increment formula. Both items vanish, so the total does too. That is exactly continuity at the point. Differentiability gives more: the change in y is at most a fixed multiple of the change in x.

Continuity only promises the change shrinks to zero, not how fast. It may shrink too slowly, like a square root. It may oscillate as it shrinks. Or the left and right derivatives may differ, like absolute value.

Counterexample one: absolute value, at zero. From the right, every secant has slope exactly one. So the right derivative is one. From the left, the slope is exactly minus one. Both one-sided derivatives exist, but they are unequal, so it is not differentiable there, by Theorem five point two. The graph is unbroken, yet no single tangent line fits.

Counterexample two: the square root, at its endpoint zero. It is continuous at zero: the root shrinks to zero. Its difference quotient simplifies to a single fraction. As h shrinks, it grows past every bound. The tangent stands vertical, so no derivative exists. It does shrink, just far more slowly than h.

Counterexample three: an oscillating function. It is squeezed between two lines through zero. So with value zero at zero, it is continuous there. Its difference quotient is just a sine. As h shrinks, the secant slope oscillates between minus one and one. Two sequences shrinking to zero give quotients one and minus one. So there is no limit, and no derivative at zero. Continuous everywhere, not differentiable at zero; zoom in, it still oscillates.

Three cases. One: one-sided derivatives exist, but differ; two, the quotient is unbounded; three, it oscillates with no limit. This list is not complete: a quotient can be unbounded and oscillate at once.

A classic exam question: is it differentiable where the formula switches? Example five: a cosine piece on the right, a straight line on the left. Step one, check continuity: both pieces head to zero. Step two, right side: the quotient tends to zero. So secants from the right flatten out to horizontal. On the left, the quotient is always one, so the left derivative is one. Step three, compare: zero is not one, so no derivative at zero. Even though f is continuous at zero. The routine: continuity first, then both one-sided quotients, then compare. Why continuity first? Without it, there is no derivative to find. Careful: a right derivative is not the right-hand limit of the derivative.

4 · Outlook

Use one: like joining a curved path to a straight road on a campus map. Continuity at one ties the constants together. Continuity alone still allows unequal one-sided derivatives. The left derivative is two; the right derivative is a. Match them: a is two, so b is minus one. The straight piece is exactly last episode's tangent line.

Use two: where f is not continuous, it cannot be differentiable. The floor function jumps at every integer, so no derivative there. Last episode's Dirichlet example leaned on exactly this, away from zero. So last episode's question is answered, in one direction only.

In chapter six, the derivative limit theorem will shorten these checks. It also says when the two symbols from the warning agree. Multiply by one more x. Now the quotient is squeezed to zero. A later episode returns to it: its derivative is discontinuous at zero.

Outlook: in two variables, this story splits in two. Partial derivatives along the axes can exist while f is not continuous. That surprise is one reason two-variable calculus rebuilds differentiability. Chapter six will prove Darboux's theorem: a derivative existing throughout an interval has the intermediate value property; hence if it has a discontinuity, it must be of the second kind.
