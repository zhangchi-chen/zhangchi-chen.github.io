06-02 Lagrange's mean value theorem: at some instant you hit the average pace

Lagrange's mean value theorem: at some instant you hit the average pace

1 · Motivation

Lagrange's mean value theorem. At some instant, you hit your average pace exactly. The 800 metre test: two laps, and the watch stops at three twenty. 800 metres in 200 seconds: 4 per second on average. But you never held a steady four: a fast start, a tired finish. So was there one instant when you ran exactly four metres per second? Your watch shows lap splits, but never that instant. Intuition says yes: first you were faster than four, then slower. If your speed never jumps, it has to pass through four somewhere. Today's theorem says yes, and it asks for even less than that.

Plot distance against time, and the run becomes a rising curve. Your average speed is how steep the chord is, from start to finish. Your speed at an instant is how steep the tangent is. So: does some tangent tilt exactly like the chord?

2 · Statements

Say it in words first: some instant's speed equals the average.
Then translate: f′(ξ) = [f(b) − f(a)]/(b − a).
Left side: the speed at one unknown instant ξ.
Right side: total distance over total time — the watch's average.
Theorem 6.2 (Lagrange). If f is (i) continuous on the closed interval [a, b]
and (ii) differentiable on the open interval (a, b),
then there exists at least one ξ ∈ (a, b) such that f′(ξ) = [f(b) − f(a)]/(b − a).
Clause (i): no teleporting — the curve never jumps, ends included.
Drop it: a jump at the finish, and no tangent matches the chord.
Clause (ii): a speed exists at each inside instant; it needn't vary smoothly.
Drop it: a shuttle-run turn, f = |x| on [−1, 2]: slopes ±1, never 1/3.
The conclusion: at least one such point — possibly several.
And ξ sits strictly inside: never at the start or finish.
Geometric reading: some tangent is parallel to the chord.
Motion reading: at some instant, the speedometer shows the average.

First in words: at some instant, your speed equals the average. Then translate it into one formula. On the left, the speed at one unknown instant, called xi. On the right, total distance divided by total time: the watch's average.

Theorem 6.2. Condition one: f is continuous on the closed interval. Condition two: differentiable on the open interval. Then at least one inside point, xi, makes the formula true.

Condition one means no teleporting: the graph never jumps, not even at the ends. Drop it, jump at the finish, and no tangent matches the chord. Condition two: a speed exists at each inside instant, and it needn't change smoothly. Drop it: a sharp shuttle run turn, and no instant matches the average. The conclusion promises at least one such point, maybe several. And xi sits strictly inside, never at the start or the finish.

Read it as geometry: some tangent runs exactly like the chord. Read it as motion: at some instant, your speed reads the average. Rolle's theorem is the special case: equal ends make the chord flat. Last episode left a debt: what if the two ends are not level?

3 · Method

In lab reports, you subtract the trend line to see the wiggle. Do the same here: take the curve, minus the chord. That gap is our auxiliary function, called F. Check the ends: both are zero. Subtracting a straight line keeps both continuity and differentiability. So Rolle steps in: somewhere inside, F has a flat tangent. A flat spot here means the original tangent matches the chord's tilt. Move it across, and that is exactly the theorem. Undo the subtraction: the flat tangent becomes the parallel one. Debt paid: one subtraction turns Rolle into Lagrange. That's our second time handing a problem to Rolle.

First equivalent form: multiply through by the length of the interval. Second form: xi becomes a fraction of the way along, between 0 and 1. Third form: written as a step forward from the start. And it works whichever end comes first. Theta just says how far through the run that instant falls. It keeps xi honestly inside, without giving its address. Try a simple function, and finding xi means a nasty equation. Good news: you will never need to solve it.

Three corollaries turn the theorem into everyday tools. Corollary one: zero derivative at every point of an interval, then the function is constant there. Why: pick any two points, and the theorem makes their difference zero. And the word interval carries real weight. This function has zero slope everywhere it lives, yet takes two values. Its domain breaks at zero: two pieces, two constants. Use it: arcsine plus arccosine has zero derivative inside. So it is constant, and plugging in zero gives pi over two.

Corollary two: two functions with equal derivatives on an interval, differ only by a constant. Why: their difference has zero derivative, and Corollary one finishes it. Example: sine squared and minus cosine squared share one derivative. Corollary two says they differ by a constant, here exactly one. This plus c will come knocking again in chapter eight.

Corollary three: continuous near a point, differentiable around it, and the derivative settles to a limit, then the derivative at the point exists and equals it. This is the derivative limit theorem. Why: each secant slope equals some tangent slope, squeezed toward the point. So the secant's limit is the derivative's limit, from both sides.

4 · Outlook

The derivative table episode left a debt at piecewise joins. Take this piecewise function, joined at zero. First, it is continuous at zero: both pieces arrive at zero. Step two: on the left, the slope tends to one. On the right, the slope tends to one as well. The theorem hands us the slope at zero: one, with no messy quotients. But careful: the converse fails. Our old friend, which wiggles faster and faster near zero. Its derivative at zero exists, yet nearby derivatives never settle down. So the theorem is a sufficient test, not a necessary one.

Now a reflex: whenever you see a difference of values, think Lagrange. Show that arctangent rises no more than its input does. The difference appears, and a xi slides in underneath. That factor is at most one, so the bound follows. We never learned where xi is, only that it exists.
