16-01 Plane point sets: does the boundary count as one of us

Plane point sets: does the boundary count as one of us

1 · Motivation

Plane point sets. Does the boundary count as one of us? Your study group has a chat, and some people are already in it. Some people are clearly outside, and some got invited but never joined. Being on the edge, and actually being a member, are two separate questions.

Mathematics stages the same scene, with a ring called D. Each point's distance is at least one, but below two. The inner circle, radius one, belongs to D. The outer circle, radius two, is left out. And yet, both circles cling to D just as tightly.

A classmate reasons that if D is not open, it must be closed. Back on the line, a half-open interval is neither. Open and closed are not opposites. We need precise words first. Two questions. Where do you sit? And does the set crowd in around you?

2 · Statements

First tool: the neighborhood U(A; δ).
It is the inside of the circle of radius δ around A.
Squares work too: each fits inside a bigger copy of the other.
Remove A itself: that is the punctured neighborhood U°(A; δ).
It looks at A's surroundings and ignores A itself.
The course text sorts points into three kinds by position.
Interior point: some disk around A lies entirely inside E.
Exterior point: some disk around A misses E completely.
Boundary point: every disk around A meets E and its complement.
Interior points belong to E; exterior points do not.
A boundary point may or may not belong to E.
Second question: does E crowd around A?
Accumulation point: every punctured disk around A holds points of E.
Equivalently, every neighborhood holds infinitely many points of E.
A point of E that is not an accumulation point is isolated.
Note: an accumulation point need not belong to E.
Now open and closed sets, in the course text's words.
Open: every point of E has its own disk inside E.
Closed: E keeps every one of its accumulation points.
A set with no accumulation points counts as closed too.
One last definition: regions.
Connected: any two points link by a polygonal line inside the set.
Open region: a nonempty, connected open set.
Add its boundary, and you get a closed region.
All of these share one name: regions.

Our first tool is the neighborhood of a point. It is everything strictly inside a circle around A. Squares work just as well: each shape fits inside a bigger copy of the other. Dig out the center point, and you get the punctured neighborhood. It looks only at the neighbors, never at the point itself.

The course text sorts every point into three kinds. An interior point owns a small disk that stays wholly inside the set. An exterior point has a small disk that never touches the set. At a boundary point, every disk, however small, catches points from both sides. Interior points are members, and exterior points are not. And a boundary point? It might belong, or it might not.

Second question: does the set crowd around this point? At an accumulation point, every punctured disk still catches points of the set. Put another way, every neighborhood holds infinitely many points of the set. A point of the set that is not an accumulation point is called isolated. Careful, an accumulation point does not have to be a member.

Next, open and closed sets, exactly as the course text puts them. In an open set, every member owns a little disk inside the set. A closed set keeps every point it crowds around. And a set with no accumulation points at all is called closed as well.

One last definition, regions. Connected: any two points link up by straight legs, never leaving the set. An open region is an open set that is nonempty and connected. Add the boundary, and the open region becomes a closed region. All of these are simply called regions.

3 · Method

One grid. Rows ask where you sit, columns ask whether the set crowds you. A point deep inside the ring is an interior point and an accumulation point. In fact, every interior point is automatically an accumulation point. So one cell, interior yet isolated, can never be filled. A point on the outer circle is a boundary point, and an accumulation point. Now take dots at one, one half, one third, and onward. Each dot sits alone, an isolated point, and also a boundary point. Its disk holds the point itself, and plenty of outsiders. Exterior points live outside the grid, the set keeps its distance.

The course text's first example is exactly our ring. Every point on either circle is a boundary point. On either circle, every disk, however small, reaches both sides. Both are boundary points, yet one is in and one is out. Being a boundary point, and being a member, are separate tests. Here is an exam favorite: a closed set owns every one of its boundary points. Otherwise, a boundary point outside would be an accumulation point it forgot. An open set, on the other hand, owns none of its boundary points. Every member is interior, so some disk around it never touches the outside. Neither kind may keep some and lose some, just like our ring. So the ring is neither open nor closed, and our classmate's shortcut fails.

Think of these dots as occupied seats, packing tighter near the origin. Shrink the circle around the origin as far as you like, seats still show up. Why must there be infinitely many? Suppose only a few were nearby. Pick a radius below the nearest distance, and the punctured disk is empty. So every neighborhood must hold infinitely many, and proofs lean on exactly this.

Here's a dictionary, from the number line to the plane. The interval around a point turns into a disk around a point. Intervals turn into regions, and endpoints turn into boundary points. And keeping your endpoints becomes being closed. The plane is just a roomier space, not a brand new subject.

Now shade the first and third quadrants. It's open, since every point has room for a disk that avoids both axes. But any zigzag path joining the two pieces has to cross an axis. The pieces meet only at the origin, and the origin is not a member. So this set is open, but it is not a region. Picture a campus cut in two by a river, with no bridge anywhere.

A bounded set fits inside some disk centered at the origin. The two quadrants are unbounded, no disk can hold them. The diameter is the least upper bound of distances between two of its points. It plays the part that length plays for an interval. The ring's diameter is four, approached by opposite points but never reached. Why never more than four? Go through the origin, each leg is shorter than two. That detour is the triangle inequality, a tool we'll keep using. The unit square's diameter is its diagonal, and here it's actually reached.
