Binomial endpoints: guess from the function, then prove with Raabe

1 · Motivation

Binomial endpoints. First a guess from the function, then a proof with Raabe. In fourteen oh four we proved the series gives back the function inside the interval. But the two endpoints were left blank, and the course text sent us elsewhere. Today's scorecard: three ranges of alpha, two endpoints, every box covered.

Like a parcel at your door: you weigh it and guess what is inside. Then you open it on camera anyway, just to be sure. The plan: a guess from the function first, then a proof with three tools.

Why are endpoints hard? There the ratio of neighbouring terms tends to one. And a limit of exactly one leaves the ratio test with nothing to say. So we need a sharper test, and before that, a good guess.

2 · Statements

The course text's endpoint table for the binomial series.
α ≤ −1: neither endpoint belongs, the domain is (−1, 1).
For −1 < α < 0, the right endpoint joins: (−1, 1].
For α > 0, both endpoints join: [−1, 1].
For α = 0, 1, 2, … the series is a polynomial; the table means the rest.
Theorem 14.6(ii), quoted from 14-01.
Converge at the left endpoint, and the sum is right continuous there.
In plain words: the endpoint sum is the limit from inside.

Here is the course text's endpoint table for the binomial series. When alpha is minus one or less, both endpoints are left out. Between minus one and zero, the right endpoint joins in. When alpha is positive, both endpoints join. When alpha is a whole number, the series just stops; the table is about every other alpha.

Theorem fourteen six, part two, from fourteen oh one. If the series converges at the left endpoint, the sum is right continuous there. In plain words, the sum at the endpoint is the limit you reach from inside.

Raabe's test from twelve oh four, in limit form. It is built for exactly the case where the ratio creeps up to one. It measures how fast: multiply the gap from one by n, then take the limit. Above one, the series converges; below one, it diverges.

3 · Method

Step one: ask the function what the sum would have to be. At minus one the function hands us zero to the alpha. For positive alpha that is just zero, a perfectly finite value. For negative alpha, the function shoots off to infinity. So the guess: at minus one, it converges exactly when alpha is not negative.

At plus one the function gives two to the alpha, finite for every alpha. Careful: when alpha is minus one, the function gives one half there. Yet its partial sums flip between one and zero forever. So listen to the terms: they die out exactly when alpha is above minus one. That is the necessary condition from twelve oh one, our first filter. The guess at plus one: converge exactly when alpha is above minus one. Two minutes of guessing, all pencilled in. Now we open the box.

Take a negative alpha, and suppose the series did converge at minus one. Then theorem fourteen six makes the sum right continuous at minus one. So that finite sum must equal the limit from the right. But that limit is infinite, and no finite number can match it. So for every negative alpha, it diverges at minus one. The memory trick just became a proof, and the theorem did the heavy lifting. That is why the uniform convergence card from fourteen oh one mattered.

Step two: watch the sizes of the coefficients. At both endpoints the terms have exactly these sizes; only signs change. Divide one term by the one before, and almost everything cancels. Rewrite it as one minus a small gap. The ratio creeps up to one, which is exactly where Raabe works. Multiply that gap by n, the way Raabe asks. As n grows, it settles at one plus alpha. Raabe reads it off: positive alpha lands above one, negative below.

Once more, slowly, with alpha one half and minus one half. On each side, the neighbour ratio comes out like this. Times n, the left side tends to one and a half, the right to one half. This is exactly the step we learned Raabe's test for, in twelve oh four.

For positive alpha, Raabe says yes: both endpoints converge absolutely. Then theorem fourteen six gives the sums: zero on the left, two to the alpha on the right. For negative alpha, the sizes alone add up to infinity. At minus one the terms all share one sign, so it simply diverges.

Now the middle case at plus one: Raabe already rules out absolute convergence. But here the terms alternate in sign: up, down, up, down. Their sizes keep shrinking, because alpha is above minus one. And they fade to zero, the same necessary condition we met earlier. The Leibniz test from twelve oh five finishes the job: it converges. It converges, but not absolutely. That is conditional convergence. Middle row done: left end out, right end in.

Last row: alpha at most minus one. Now the ratio is at least one, so the sizes never drop below one. For alpha minus two, the terms at plus one swing wider and wider. The general term refuses to shrink, so both endpoints diverge. Every pencilled guess survived the unboxing.

A ten second check before any exam: alpha plus or minus one half. One half gives the square root, landing softly at zero. That zero is honest; the sums creep down to it. Minus one half shoots straight up at minus one. That matches the course text's series nine, valid up to plus one. If your answer fights the function's shape, go back and check.

4 · Outlook

Use one: a series for the square root of two. The partial sums zigzag in on it. Use two: the left endpoint gives an exact sum for free. These sizes add up to exactly one, slowly but surely.

Raabe from twelve oh four, Leibniz from twelve oh five, the term test from twelve oh one. And the theorem from fourteen oh one turned our guess into a proof. The blank column from fourteen oh four is filled, with no outside help.

This habit travels far: guess from the shape, then test. In differential equations, how a solution behaves near the edge is guessed this way. So is how integrals behave at their endpoints, later in analysis. Each time, the right sharp test confirms the guess. Guess from the function, then prove it with Raabe.
