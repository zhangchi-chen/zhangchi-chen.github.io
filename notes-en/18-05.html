18-05 Lagrange multipliers: the best you can afford

Lagrange multipliers: the best you can afford

1 · Motivation

Lagrange multipliers: getting the best you can afford. Your dorm needs an open box with a fixed volume. Cardboard costs money, so we want as little of it as possible. Add the four walls and the bottom: that is the surface. The volume is the budget, and it must be spent exactly. Spend the whole budget and pay as little surface as possible.

First instinct: solve for the height, then plug it in. The surface becomes a function of two free variables. It works, but the algebra already feels clumsy. Change the condition, and the height may refuse to come out. We want a systematic method that never has to solve first.

2 · Statements

The problem: extremize f under side conditions φₖ = 0.
f is the objective function: what you pay or enjoy.
Each φₖ = 0 is a side condition you must obey.
m < n: fewer conditions than variables, so freedom remains.
A smaller story: 8 to spend, coffee costs 1, a meal 2.
Spending it all puts you on the budget line x + 2y = 8.
Satisfaction is f = xy; its level curves are hyperbolas.
Without the budget, xy just keeps growing: no unconstrained maximum.
On the line, f does peak: that is a conditional extremum.
How do we find that peak without trying every point?

Here is the problem: make f extreme while the side conditions hold. f is the objective function, the thing you pay or enjoy. Each side condition is a rule the point must obey. Fewer conditions than variables, so there is still room to choose.

A smaller story: eight to spend, coffee costs one, a meal costs two. Spending it all puts you on the budget line. Satisfaction is the product, and its level curves are hyperbolas. Without the budget, satisfaction just keeps growing, with no peak at all. Restricted to the line, satisfaction does peak: a conditional extremum. How do we find that peak without trying every single point?

3 · Method

Step one: the implicit function theorem makes the curve a local graph. Step two: riding along the curve, only one variable is left. Step three: the chain rule, plus the implicit slope formula. The result: the two gradients are parallel at that point. In the course text's words: the two curves share their tangent.

Now the picture: the budget line, and one level curve. This level crosses the line at two affordable baskets. At a crossing, walk along the line and satisfaction still rises. Any crossing leaves an affordable improvement on the table. Push the level higher: it crosses, and it still crosses. until it just touches, at four coffees and two meals. Push further, and the curve leaves the line: unaffordable. So the best basket is where the two curves are tangent. Tangent curves share their normal, so the two gradients line up. Here one gradient is exactly twice the other. The gradient episode did the work: gradients meet level curves at right angles.

Parallel means some number lambda balances the two gradients. Two components plus the side condition make three equations. They are exactly the stationary points of a single function. The course text calls it the Lagrange function. Differentiating in lambda hands the side condition back for free.

Back to the box: four partial derivatives, all set to zero. The key move: multiply through, and every lambda term matches. Subtracting the equations pins everything down. A square base, and the height is half the side. Out comes the least amount of cardboard. Try a volume of 4: a two by two by one box, surface 12.

And lambda? It is not leftover scaffolding. Ask: what if you could afford a little more volume? The least surface grows at exactly the rate minus lambda. At volume 4, one extra unit costs about 2 more of surface. Same in the allowance story: one more unit buys about two more satisfaction. So lambda is the price of the constraint.

More conditions? Give each one its own multiplier. Theorem 18.6: when the rank condition holds, the multipliers exist. Rank m means the constraint gradients point in truly different directions.

A plane slices a paraboloid, and the cut is an ellipse. Which points sit nearest to the origin, and which farthest? Use the squared distance, and give each condition its own multiplier.

Swap the two coordinates, and the whole picture stays the same. Subtracting confirms it; the other branch would make the height negative. Exactly two points survive.

On the ellipse, they are its lowest and highest points. Their distances are about 0.58 and 4.2. The setup is the real work; after that it is routine.

An honest question: does solving the system prove a maximum? Counterexample: along this cubic, the stationary point is no extremum. The method only lists candidates; the problem itself decides. Too flat or too thin wastes cardboard, so the lone candidate is the minimum. The ellipse is closed and bounded, so both extremes exist, one per candidate.

Drop the rank condition, and the method can miss the answer. At the cusp, x is smallest, yet the system has no solution. The reason: the constraint gradient vanishes there, leaving no normal to match.

4 · Outlook

A bonus: the method doubles as a machine for inequalities. For every box, the surface is at least this cube root bound. Try a unit cube: 5 against about 4.76. It holds. Equality holds exactly at the best shape.

The unconstrained extrema episode left a debt: what about the edge? Inside, set the gradient to zero; on the edge, it is a constrained problem. Here the edge wins: largest value 1, smallest value minus 1. Boundary extremes are exactly constrained extremes on the edge curve.

Economists call lambda a shadow price. With inequality constraints, the idea grows into the KKT conditions. Machine learning and operations research solve such systems by the million. The best you can afford is where the level curve just touches the budget.
