18-01 The implicit function theorem: a function hidden inside an equation

The implicit function theorem: a function hidden inside an equation

1 · Motivation

A function is hiding inside an equation, and we are going to find it. Some equations are friendly, like this one: just the unit circle. Solve for y, and the circle splits into an upper and a lower branch.

Now meet the stubborn equation the course text likes to use. Try to get y alone, and it hides in two places at once. Every rearrangement puts y right back on the other side.

Yet when we plot the solutions, a calm, smooth curve appears. Sweep a vertical line across: one meeting point, every single time. The reason: the partial derivative in y never falls below one half. So a perfectly good function is hiding in there. We can’t write it down, so can we still trust it and use it?

Back on the circle, one input gives two answers, so which one counts? The theorem will answer both, but only by looking near a single point.

2 · Statements

Setup: an equation F(x, y) = 0 through a point P₀(x₀, y₀).
Theorem 18.1, existence and uniqueness of an implicit function: four hypotheses.
(i): F is continuous on a region D, with P₀ inside it.
(ii): F(x₀, y₀) = 0, the initial condition — P₀ lies on the curve.
(iii): the partial derivative F_y exists and is continuous on D.
(iv): F_y(x₀, y₀) ≠ 0 — moving up through P₀, F truly changes.
Then the theorem promises conclusions 1° and 2°.
1°: inside a neighbourhood U(P₀), the solutions form a single graph.
A graph means every vertical line meets it exactly once.
Its domain is the interval (x₀ − α, x₀ + α), and f(x₀) = y₀.
And F(x, f(x)) ≡ 0: plug the implicit function in, the equation holds.
“Uniquely”: inside U(P₀), no second branch sneaks in.
2°: the implicit function is continuous on its interval.
Now the catch: the promise is local.
Like a friend texting “the cafeteria is quiet right now” — not all semester.
On the circle, near (0, 1), y is a function of x.
Near (1, 0), a vertical line meets it twice, or not at all.
Shrink the box as much as you like: it never becomes a graph.
And there F_y = 2y is exactly zero: hypothesis (iv) fails.
Only a neighbourhood is promised — never the whole curve.

The setup: one equation, and one point lying on its solution set.

Here is the theorem as the course text states it, with its four hypotheses. Hypothesis one: no jumps in F, and our point sits safely inside. Hypothesis two, the initial condition: our point really lies on the curve. Hypothesis three: the vertical rate of change varies without jumps. Hypothesis four, the heart of it: step up through the point and F truly changes.

And here is what the theorem gives back in return. Conclusion one: zoom in near the point, and the curve becomes a graph. Being a graph means every vertical line meets it once, never twice. It lives on a small interval around the point and passes right through it. Plug the implicit function back in, and the equation holds all along. Uniquely means no second branch sneaks into the box. Conclusion two: the implicit function draws without lifting the pencil.

Here comes the catch: the promise is only local. Like a friend saying the cafeteria is quiet right now, not for the whole semester. Near the top of the circle, y really is a function. Near the rightmost point, a vertical line hits twice, or misses entirely. Shrink the box as far as you like; the trouble stays. And exactly there, the partial in y is zero; hypothesis four breaks. Say it out loud: only a neighbourhood, never the whole curve.

3 · Method

Why must it exist at all? One picture tells the whole story. Think of a search grid where every column hides exactly one target. Step one: suppose the partial in y is positive at our point. By continuity, that stays true on a whole small box around it. So as you climb any vertical segment, F only goes up. Step two: our point is a zero, so below it F is negative, above it positive. Step three: slide sideways, and the signs hold along the edges. If an edge loses its sign near a corner, we simply make the box narrower.

Step four: pick any column; it starts negative and ends positive. The intermediate value theorem guarantees a zero somewhere in between. And since F only rises, it can never meet zero a second time. Do this in every column, and the dots line up into the implicit function. That rectangle with its coloured edges is the entire proof. Both tools come from chapter sixteen, episode four, now put back to work.

Why is f continuous? The same picture, moved a little, answers that. Draw a thin box around that point; again its bottom is negative, its top positive. Columns close by find their zero inside the thin box, so f barely moves.

Why the vertical direction? Because that is the variable we solve for. The vertical direction has to cut through the curve, not run along it. At the rightmost point, one partial vanishes, but the other does not. Swap the roles, and the same theorem hands back a function the other way round.

Careful: these conditions are enough, but they are not required. Take this cubic equation: at the origin, hypothesis four fails. Yet equal cubes mean equal numbers, so the curve is just a straight line. So a zero there does not rule out an implicit function.

But more often, when hypothesis four fails, the conclusion falls apart. This figure-eight curve crosses itself right at the origin. Near the origin, every column except the middle one meets it twice. Shrink the box as you like; the crossing stays right inside.

Add one more continuity, and the implicit function gets a derivative. It comes with a formula built from the two partial derivatives. Test it on the circle, where the formula simplifies nicely. Near the top, the explicit upper branch has exactly that slope. With more variables the story is the same; for now, just remember the name.

4 · Outlook

First use: this stubborn equation is Kepler’s equation for an orbit. Time ticks evenly, and the hidden function tells us where the planet is. We never need a formula; the theorem promises the answer moves without jumps.

Second use: the proof is a recipe a computer can follow. Test the middle, keep the half where the sign still flips, and repeat. Twenty halvings later, the hidden value is known to four decimals.

A debt we leave for the next episode: derivatives without solving. For systems of equations, this condition grows into a Jacobian determinant. Later, tangent lines will be read directly off such equations. Looking ahead, the same rectangle argument scales up to whole systems. That is the complete implicit function theorem, waiting in chapter twenty three. And through that door lie smooth surfaces and manifolds. Remember: where the y partial is not zero, a function is hiding nearby.
