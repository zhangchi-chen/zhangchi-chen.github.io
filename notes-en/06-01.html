06-01 Fermat and Rolle: three conditions, none removable

Fermat and Rolle: three conditions, none removable

1 · Motivation

Fermat and Rolle, and three conditions you cannot drop. Saturday, the hiking club sets off on a loop trail. You leave from one parking lot and come back to it, same height. Here is the claim: somewhere along the way, the path was perfectly flat. It could be a summit, a valley, or a path that never climbs. Flat means the tangent lies level, with zero slope. In symbols, some point inside has derivative zero.

Then your roommate sketches three trips where the claim falls apart. Each time, one piece of gear stayed home: the tent, the boots, or the map. So which conditions does the claim really need, and why?

2 · Statements

First, the engine underneath: Fermat's lemma.
At an extremum where f is differentiable, f′(x₀) = 0.
Clause one: f is defined on a whole neighbourhood of x₀.
Clause two: f is differentiable at x₀.
Clause three: x₀ is an extremum point, a local max or min.
Then the tangent there is horizontal: f′(x₀) = 0.
Why? Draw chords from the peak x₀ to nearby points.
On the right, chords run downhill: the quotient is ≤ 0.
On the left, chords climb: the quotient is ≥ 0.
Both one-sided limits equal f′(x₀): at most 0, at least 0, so 0.
Recall 05-02: a derivative needs both one-sided derivatives to agree.
At an endpoint only one side exists, so nothing squeezes.
Now Rolle's theorem turns the hike into mathematics.
Three conditions go in; one flat point comes out.
Condition (i): continuous on the closed interval [a, b].
Condition (ii): differentiable on the open interval (a, b).
Endpoints are exempt: a semicircle's vertical ends are fine.
Condition (iii): equal heights at both ends, f(a) = f(b).
Conclusion: some ξ in (a, b) has f′(ξ) = 0.
At least one, maybe several, and it won't say where.
The text warns: drop any one condition and it may fail.

First, the engine that drives everything: Fermat's lemma. At a peak or dip where the slope exists, the slope is zero. First, the function lives on both sides of the point. Second, it has a derivative right at that point. Third, the point is a local peak or a local dip. Then the tangent there must lie flat.

Why? Draw chords from the peak to nearby points. On the right, every chord runs downhill. On the left, every chord climbs. One side says at most zero, the other at least zero, so it is zero. Recall episode five two: a derivative needs both sides to agree. At an endpoint you get only one side, and the squeeze fails.

Now Rolle's theorem turns the hike into mathematics. Three conditions go in, and one flat point comes out. Condition one, the tent: no breaks anywhere, endpoints included. Condition two, the boots: a tangent at every inner point. The endpoints don't need a tangent: a semicircle with steep ends still works. Condition three, the map: you finish at the height you started. Then at least one inner point has a flat tangent. At least one, maybe several, and the theorem never says where. The course text warns: drop any one, and the conclusion may fail.

3 · Method

The plan: find a summit or a valley strictly inside. Step one: an unbroken trail on a closed stretch has a highest and lowest point. That guarantee comes from episode four three, and it spends condition one. Case one: highest equals lowest, the trail is level, any point works. Case two: the trail really goes up and down. Because the ends are level, they can hold at most one of the two extremes. So the other one sits strictly inside, thanks to condition three. Inside, there is a tangent, thanks to condition two. And Fermat's lemma lays the tangent flat right there. One proof, and each condition runs its own step.

Now the gear test: leave one condition at home and keep the rest. Wrong map: drop condition three and walk a straight ramp. Unbroken and smooth, but you finish higher than you started. The slope is one everywhere, so it is never flat. No tent: drop condition one and lift only the starting point to height one. Both ends sit at height one, and inside it is smooth. Still slope one everywhere: the bottom height is never reached.

No boots: drop condition two and walk a sharp V. It is unbroken, and both ends sit at height one. The only dip inside sits at zero, and it has no tangent. Elsewhere the slope is positive or negative one; Fermat finds nothing. Three trips, three failures, each breaking the step its condition supported. Careful: a parabola with unequal ends still has a flat bottom. They guarantee a flat spot, but it can appear without them.

Now the Rolle pattern at work, counting roots. Take a function differentiable everywhere whose slope is never zero. Then it has at most one zero. Suppose, to the contrary, it hits zero twice. Between them, all three conditions hold. Rolle produces a flat point between them: a contradiction. The move: build a stretch where all three conditions hold, then let the flat point work.

These flat spots have a name: stationary points. Fermat says a smooth peak or dip is always stationary. The reverse is false. Look at the cube curve at zero. The tangent is flat, yet the curve slips right through it. Lower on the left, higher on the right: no peak, no dip. Stationary is not the same as extreme. Keep this picture; it comes back.

4 · Outlook

First use: how many times does this cubic cross zero? Its slope is always at least three, never zero. So by the pattern, it has at most one root. It runs from below zero to above zero, so at least one. Exactly one root, and it lies between zero and one.

Second use: between two zeros, the slope must vanish somewhere. Take a cubic with roots at one, two and three. Rolle on each gap gives a flat point in each. Check: the slope vanishes near 1.42 and 2.58. In general, every gap between neighbouring roots hides a flat point.

Unequal ends? A single subtraction fixes that, next episode. That subtraction is the first helper function; the full kit comes at chapter's end. This pattern keeps coming back: equal ends force a flat spot inside. With integrals: a running area that ends where it began. In two variables, equal boundary values force a level tangent plane. Whenever a problem hands you two equal values, a flat spot hides between them. Three conditions, three ways to fail, and not one is optional.
