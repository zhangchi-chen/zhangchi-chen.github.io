21-08 Improper double integrals: the Gaussian integral falls to polar coordinates

Improper double integrals: the Gaussian integral falls to polar coordinates

1 · Motivation

Improper double integrals, and how polar coordinates crack the Gaussian integral. Everyone runs into this bell-shaped curve sooner or later. How much area sits under the right half, all the way out?

The reflex is to find an antiderivative and plug in the ends. But no formula built from familiar functions does the job. Same wall that stopped us when switching the order. Picture it: the direct stairwell up to the answer is locked.

So we take the campus shuttle instead, a detour through the plane. Square the mystery number, and it starts to look like a double integral. But this quadrant runs on forever; there is no finite area to chop.

Our double integral was built by chopping up a bounded region. Once the region is unbounded, the chopping never ends. So we need a new definition, and then a way to really compute. Spoiler: the answer has a pi hiding inside it.

Here is the course text's definition, word for word. First, the function lives on a region that never ends. Next, draw any smooth loop that wraps around the origin. The loop cuts a bounded piece out of the region. On that bounded piece, our old double integral already works. Measure how close the loop ever comes to the origin. Now push the loop farther and farther out, in every direction. The captured totals must settle on one finite number, whatever the loop's shape. Then we say the improper double integral converges, to that number.

Why should the loop's shape get no say at all? Disks, squares, odd blobs: just different ways to sweep out the plane. If two sweeps disagreed, which number would we even believe? Good news: for a nonnegative function, every shape gives the same answer.

The course text packs this into one theorem; here it is. For a nonnegative function, the totals just must never pass a ceiling. With nothing negative, a bigger region can only add more. Always climbing, never past the ceiling: they have to settle down. With signs allowed, the course text shows convergence means absolute convergence. Unlike the single-variable case, there is no conditional convergence here. And here is the example we came for. Two numbers to earn: one for the quadrant, one for the bell.

3 · Method

Step one: take a quarter disk, the shape polar coordinates love. The function only cares how far you are from the origin. Polar area pieces carry an extra r, the stretch rate we met before. That extra factor is the key that unlocks the exponential. On the quarter disk, the answer comes out in closed form. Push the radius out, and the values climb toward a quarter of pi. Remember the polar disk example? Its seed sprouts right here.

Disks hand us the number, but the unknown we want lives on squares. On the square, the function splits into two one-variable factors. Done as iterated integrals, the square integral is a perfect square. That quiet step comes from the order-switching episode.

Here is the heart of it: squeeze the square between two quarter disks. The small quarter disk fits snugly inside the square. The far corner sits a bit farther out, along the diagonal. So the big quarter disk through that corner covers the whole square. Think of delivery zones: the small round zone quotes low, the big one quotes high. The function is positive, so a bigger zone always costs more. Both outer prices come straight from the polar formula.

Now let the square grow, and watch the two quotes close in. With side one, the square sits well between the quotes. With side two, the quotes are nearly touching. In the limit both quotes hit a quarter of pi, so the middle must too. So the unknown, squared, is exactly a quarter of pi. The area is positive, so take the positive root, and we are done. The old wall finally has a measurement, taken from a side it could not guard. A classic slip: reporting a quarter of pi as the one-variable answer. A quarter of pi is the square; the answer is its square root.

Four symmetric quadrants, and the whole plane gives exactly pi. Over the whole line, the bell's area is the square root of pi. Stretch the variable a little, and the answer becomes the root of two pi. Divide by that constant, and the normal density has total area exactly one. As the course text says, probability theory uses it all the time.

Why did we have to climb into the plane at all? With no elementary antiderivative, every one-variable trick hits the locked door. Ride up to the plane, split, squeeze, and ride back down. That is the two-dimensional detour, the shuttle around the locked stairs.

Tool one: a nonnegative function with capped totals converges. The Cauchy test: if it fades at least like a power higher than two, it converges. Why two? Rings get bigger farther out, and that eats one power of decay. That two is the dimension of the plane; on a line, the cutoff was one. Strategy: positive function, endless region? Fill it with growing disks first. If the radial integral converges, you are done; the theorem already handled shape.

4 · Outlook

The course text uses the same trick to link the Beta and Gamma functions. Two old tools, polar coordinates and iterated integrals, one brand new number. Later, in probability, the normal distribution leans on today's constant. Looking ahead, the same computation works in any number of dimensions. It also explains the value of the Gamma function at one half. The heat kernel and the Wiener measure, later on, stand on this one integral. A locked stairwell, a detour through the plane, and pi was waiting there.
