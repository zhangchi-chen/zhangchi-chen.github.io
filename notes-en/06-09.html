06-09 Extrema and maxima: call the roll of every candidate

Extrema and maxima: call the roll of every candidate

1 · Motivation

Finding extremes today means calling every candidate by name. A homework problem asks for the largest and smallest values on an interval. Classmate A sets the derivative to zero and announces a minimum of four. Classmate B sketches the graph and says the minimum is zero. One function, two answers. So who is right?

Draw it, and the lowest point turns out to be a sharp corner. Setting the derivative to zero never finds it; no derivative exists there. And the top value, five, is reached twice, once right at the edge. So which points deserve a place on the candidate list? Like a team captain before a match: call every name and skip nobody.

2 · Statements

Points where f′ = 0 are called stationary points.
But this is only necessary, not sufficient.

Tempting slogan two: every extremum point is a stationary point.

Stationary ⇏ extremum, and extremum ⇏ stationary: keep both pictures.

Left of x₀ the derivative is ≤ 0; right of x₀ it is ≥ 0.
Down, then up: the bottom of a valley, a minimum.

Try 2x³ − 9x² + 12x: stationary points at x = 1 and x = 2.

Local extremum versus global minimum: two different questions.

Fermat: at a differentiable peak or valley, the derivative is zero. Points with zero derivative are called stationary points. But that condition is only necessary, not sufficient. First tempting slogan: every stationary point is an extreme point. Take x cubed at zero: the tangent lies perfectly flat. Yet the curve keeps climbing right through it. No extremum. Second tempting slogan: every extreme point is stationary. Take the absolute value of x: zero is clearly the lowest point. Yet no derivative exists: the left and right slopes disagree. Neither direction holds. Keep both pictures in mind. So the candidates are stationary points, plus points with no derivative. Making the list is not winning. We need sufficient conditions.

Here is the first sufficient condition, as the course text states it. At the point itself we only ask for continuity; a corner is allowed. On the left the slope is never positive; on the right, never negative. Going down, then up: the bottom of a valley, a minimum. Flip the signs, up and then down, and you get a maximum. This is the monotonicity rule from the inequalities episode, used near one point. Notice: the test never needs the derivative at that point.

Now the second sufficient condition, in the course text's words. Stand at a stationary point and simply read the sign of the second derivative. It is like glancing at the scoreboard instead of watching every rally. Try the cubic from the homework: it has two stationary points. At the first one it is negative: a local maximum, five. At the second it is positive: a local minimum, four. Classmate A computed correctly, but only answered a local question. A local extremum and a global minimum are two different questions.

3 · Method

Now the first test goes to work on a function with a sharp point. Differentiate, then tidy it into one fraction. One stationary point and one corner: a candidate of each kind. Read the sign chart: positive, negative, positive. Up, then down at the corner: a local maximum of zero. Down, then up at the stationary point: a local minimum of minus three. The graph agrees: a sharp peak at zero, a rounded valley at one. At the corner there is no derivative, so the second test cannot even begin. Handling corners is exactly where the first test shines.

Why does the second test work? One line of Taylor's formula explains it. Expand to second order; at a stationary point the first derivative term vanishes. What remains is a bracket times a square. The square is positive away from the point. Near the point the small term fades, so the bracket keeps the second derivative's sign. So a positive second derivative gives a minimum, a negative one a maximum. This is the second differential sign rule promised in the higher differentials episode. At a stationary point both share one sign. Promise kept.

But if the second derivative is zero too, the scoreboard tells us nothing. Three functions show the same zero readings, yet end three different ways. Then fall back on the first test, or look at higher derivatives. The course text names a third sufficient condition; we read it without proof. For the fourth power, the first nonzero derivative is the fourth, and positive: a minimum. For the cube, the first nonzero order is three, odd: no extremum. Now the course text's cautionary function. Every derivative at zero vanishes, yet zero is a genuine minimum. Every order stays silent, but the first test still works: down, then up.

Now back to the homework, and the global question. The course text's procedure for a continuous function on a closed interval. Scan one: the stationary points inside, here one and two. Scan two: points with no derivative, here the corner at zero. That corner is the awkward teammate nobody remembers to call. Why a corner? The expression inside the bars changes sign only at zero. Scan three: both endpoints, the two players on the goal line.

Then just compare the values. No more theory is needed. The roll call reads about three point six, then zero, five, four, five. The minimum, zero, sits at the corner classmate A never called. The maximum, five, is reached twice: at a stationary point and at an endpoint. The local minimum, four, is not the smallest value. Roll call finished: on the minimum, classmate B was right.

4 · Outlook

Word problems rarely hand you a formula, so first build the function. Turning the words into a function of one variable is the real mathematics. A ship's hourly fuel cost is proportional to the cube of its speed. The data: at ten kilometres an hour, fuel costs six per hour. Everything else costs ninety six per hour. The target is the cost per kilometre, as a function of speed. There is a single stationary point: twenty kilometres an hour. Cost explodes at both ends, so twenty is where it is cheapest. The minimum cost is seven point two per kilometre.

Next, a thirty centimetre square of card: cut the corners and fold an open tray. Cut squares of side x, and the volume depends on that one number. The scans give one stationary point, five, plus the two endpoints. Compare: cut five centimetres, and the tray holds two thousand cubic centimetres. In general, cut away one sixth of the side.

Today kept the second differential promise and reused the monotonicity rule. And convexity, in a later episode, will make some of these questions easy. With several variables, candidates come from a zero gradient, plus the boundary. In statistics, maximum likelihood estimation is exactly this episode, on a likelihood function. Put every candidate on the list, then compare them all.
