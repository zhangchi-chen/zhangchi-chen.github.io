09-01 The definite integral: partition, approximate, sum, take the limit

The definite integral: partition, approximate, sum, take the limit

1 · Motivation

The definite integral, in four moves: partition, approximate, sum, take the limit. Here is a region whose top edge bends. So how big is it, really? Rectangles and triangles come with formulas. A bent edge comes with nothing.

Two classmates try it. Each slices the region into four strips. The first one measures every strip at its left edge. The second one measures at the right edge, and the total comes out different. 0.219 or 0.469. Which one is the real area? Cut finer, into sixteen strips, and the two answers drift toward each other. Sixty four strips, and the gap shrinks once more. Slide the leftover pieces sideways, and they stack into a single thin column. So area should be the one number these sums settle on.

Physics hits the same wall: how much work does a changing push do? Force times distance works, but only when the force holds still. So chop the path into tiny pieces, and on each one pretend the force is frozen. Area or work: the same sum, wearing two units. One skeleton, four steps: partition, approximate, sum, and take the limit.

2 · Statements

Step one: drop points into [a, b].
These points form a partition T; its mesh is ‖T‖.
The n − 1 points in between cut [a, b] into n pieces.
Each piece has its own width, Δxᵢ.
The widest piece sets the mesh ‖T‖.
Small ‖T‖ means no piece is wide, not even one.
Step two: in each piece, pick any point ξᵢ.
Its height f(ξᵢ) stands in for the whole strip.
Step three: add up height times width.
This sum is the integral sum, also called the Riemann sum.
Move the ξᵢ, and the sum changes.
Step four: make every piece thin, and watch the sum.
Any partition fine enough, any choice of ξᵢ: the sum lands within ε of J.
Then f is Riemann integrable on [a, b], and J is its definite integral.
First, someone names a tolerance ε, as tight as they like.
Then you answer with a δ: keep ‖T‖ below it.
Any partition that fine counts, not just one you picked.
Any choice of the points ξᵢ too, not the convenient ones.
Then all those sums sit within ε of J.

Step one: sprinkle points along the interval. This is a partition; the widest piece's width is its mesh. The cutting points between the ends always make one more piece than points. Every piece gets its own width. The widest piece alone decides the mesh. A small mesh means no piece is wide. Not a single one.

Step two: in every piece, pick a point. Any point you like. The curve's height there stands in for the whole strip. Step three: height times width, then add them all. The course text names it the integral sum, also the Riemann sum. Slide those points around, and the total shifts.

Step four: thin out every piece, and watch where the total goes. Any fine enough cut, any points at all: the sum lands right next to one number. Then the function is called integrable, and that number is its definite integral.

First, someone names a tolerance, epsilon, as tight as they please. You answer with delta, a cap on how wide any piece may be. Every partition that fine must pass, not just the one you like. And any points inside the pieces, not just the handy ones. Every such total lands inside that tight band.

3 · Method

Back to our first curve, and now we cut finer and finer. The total stops wandering and settles down. Now freeze the cuts, and just shuffle the points inside them. The total jiggles, and the jiggle fades as the pieces get thinner.

Think about guessing a month of lunch money. Each stretch of days, you jot down one price, from whichever queue you stood in. Shrink the time slots, and your choice of queue matters less and less. That is exactly what the definition insists on: one total, whatever the queue.

Careful here. Lots of points is not the same as thin pieces. Cram a thousand points into the left half. The right half is still one fat piece, so the mesh is still one half. On that one fat piece, moving the point swings the total by three eighths. A thousand points, and the total still refuses to settle. Equal pieces are different: more points really do make every piece thinner. So equal pieces can help you compute, but they can never be the definition.

The sentence shape is old, borrowed from chapter three: same discipline, new objects. Back then, one input gave exactly one output. Here, one mesh hides countless partitions and countless choices of points. As the course text remarks, the integral sum is not a function of the mesh. So this limit means two kinds of steadiness at once, over cuts and over points.

Why so strict? Ask anyone who has been fooled by one bad sample. Meet the Dirichlet function: one at every rational, zero at every irrational. Every piece contains rationals. Pick only those, and the total is exactly one. Pick irrationals instead, and the total is zero, no matter how fine you cut. Allow an error of one half, and no single number sits that close to both. So it is not integrable. The full story comes in the next episode.

Now run the definition backwards, and tame a scary looking sum. Pull one factor out of every term, and a familiar shape appears. It is a Riemann sum, for one gentle curve on the unit interval. Equal pieces, and each height taken at the right end. Take for now that this function is integrable. The next episode backs it up. Then equal pieces and right ends are enough, since the mesh does shrink to zero. So the sum creeps up toward the natural log of 2, about 0.693. We borrow that value for now. The tool that computes it arrives two episodes later.

That tall sign is just a stretched letter S, for sum. The two ends of the interval sit below and above: the limits of integration. The tail of the symbol still says height times width. The letter inside is a placeholder. Rename it, and the number stays put.

4 · Outlook

Area under a curve that stays above the axis, now written as an integral. The work of a changing force, written in the same new language. And the lunch budget: the month's total is the integral of how fast you spend. An exam trick: inside a limit of sums, spot the function and the interval.

Next episode swaps all those samples for a squeeze between two numbers per cut. Later in the course, arc length is defined by these very four steps. The definition tells you what the integral is. It never tells you how to get it. Two episodes from now, differentiating and integrating turn out to undo each other. Then these sums retire, but the skeleton comes back for volumes and surface areas. Four steps, and one demand on top: every choice gives the same answer.
