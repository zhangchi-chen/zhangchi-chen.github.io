23-02 The inverse and implicit function theorems in full: a nonvanishing determinant

The inverse and implicit function theorems in full: a nonvanishing determinant

1 · Motivation

The inverse and implicit function theorems, in full. In the triple integrals episode, spherical coordinates cost you a volume factor. Here is a question we skipped: can you always swap back? Put the point on the vertical axis, and every turning angle lands on the same spot. There the angle cannot be recovered, and the volume factor is zero as well.

A coincidence? Chapter eighteen already met this pattern with a single equation. Where the partial in y is not zero, a small window shows an honest graph. Where it vanishes, the curve stands upright, and no window shows a graph.

Now take several equations at once: one wall becomes a row of walls. And when can a map of space be undone, at least near one point? Both questions get one answer: a Jacobian determinant that does not vanish. As in chapter eighteen, the promise is local: only near the point.

3 · Method

First: why is the inverse map's derivative the inverse matrix? Wrap the parcel, then unwrap it, and you hold exactly what you started with. The last episode's chain rule makes the product the identity. Square matrices whose product is the identity are inverses of each other. In one variable we took a reciprocal; here it grows into matrix inversion. Careful: those two lines quietly assumed the inverse exists and is differentiable. And the condition cannot be dropped: the two determinants must multiply to one.

A worked example from the course text: spherical coordinates. Here is the forward Jacobian matrix, one row for each new coordinate. Its determinant is the familiar volume factor from the triple integrals episode. It vanishes exactly on the vertical axis, and nowhere else. The inverse divides by that determinant, so its angle row explodes near the axis. Near the axis, the same tiny step sends the angle swinging wildly. Two episodes, one determinant: once it priced volume, today it decides what can be undone. Every polar and spherical swap in chapter twenty-one quietly leaned on this condition.

So how does the course text prove the inverse exists? Four frames. Frame one: the second condition lets us rescale, so the derivative at the point is the identity. The map is the identity minus a residual: solving becomes a fixed point hunt. Frame two: the residual pulls any two points closer, by a fixed fraction. And the first condition earns its keep: continuity keeps the residual's derivative small. Frame three: feed the answer back in, again and again, from the known point. Like finding a friend's door by halving the corridor that is left, each time. Steps shrink geometrically, so the iterates form a Cauchy sequence and settle down. Frame four: the limit point solves the equation: the wanted inverse image. Two different solutions would have to end up closer than themselves, so there is just one.

Now the implicit theorem drops out of the inverse one, with a single trick. Build a new map: keep the free part, swap the rest for the equations. The new map is square, and its derivative is a triangle of blocks. So the determinants agree: the third condition is exactly what the new map needs. So the new map can be undone, and a zero second part means our equations hold. Differentiate the identity with the chain rule: the two blocks must cancel. Multiply by the inverse block, and the theorem's formula falls into our hands. With one of each, it collapses to the old quotient from chapter eighteen.

Last stop: Lagrange multipliers, packed into one matrix formula. Read it as: the objective's gradient is a mix of the constraint gradients. With two variables and one constraint, it is the tangency picture of chapter eighteen. The rank condition lets the implicit theorem turn the constraint set into a graph, so multipliers exist. Drop the rank condition and the method can fail: look for the leftmost point of a cusp. At the cusp the constraint's derivative is zero; no multiplier can balance.
