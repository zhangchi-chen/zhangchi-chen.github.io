08-05 Rational functions: split the parcel first, sign for each item separately

Rational functions: split the parcel first, sign for each item separately

1 · Motivation

Tonight's problem set hides one integral that none of our tricks can touch. Degree four on top, degree five below: one big awkward parcel. Gluing fails: the top is not the derivative of the bottom. Trigonometric substitution? There's no radical here to unlock. Integration by parts? Whichever piece you differentiate, the leftover is just as ugly.

Shrink the problem to a little cousin with a quadratic below. A classmate splits it into two small fractions. Each piece is a plain hyperbola, and added together they rebuild the curve exactly. And each piece integrates in a single line, straight to a logarithm.

That is the whole idea: break the big parcel open at the depot. And every standard box already has a known route home. For rational functions, the era of guessing is over. Four stations, and they always run in the same order.

2 · Statements

Improper fraction: the numerator's degree is at least the denominator's.
Proper fraction: the numerator's degree is strictly smaller.
First promise: an improper fraction is a polynomial plus a proper fraction.
Second promise: a proper fraction is a sum of partial fractions.
Next, the rule for which partial fractions each factor produces.
A factor (x − a)ᵏ gives k fractions, one for every power up to k.

Why linear? Over a quadratic, a degree-one top is still proper.

The course text starts with a guarantee. Improper means the top has at least the degree of the bottom. Proper means the top's degree is strictly lower. First promise: any improper fraction peels into a polynomial and a proper fraction. Second promise: every proper fraction splits into partial fractions.

Next comes the rule for what each factor produces. A linear factor raised to a power gets one fraction for every power, up to the top one. A quadratic factor works the same way, except each numerator is linear. Why linear? Over a quadratic, a first degree top still keeps it proper. The discriminant condition says this quadratic has no real root. Compare a quadratic that does cross the axis: it splits into two linear factors.

Finally, every one of these integrals comes down to just two forms. Form one is a constant over a power of a linear factor. Form two is a linear top over a power of the quadratic. So the job has two halves: reach these forms, then integrate each one.

3 · Method

Station one: before anything else, compare the degrees. Our big parcel has four below five: already proper, so it walks right through. But a cubic over a quadratic is improper, so we divide first. The quotient and the remainder are both just x. The polynomial integrates term by term; only the proper remainder rides on. Skip the division, and the split can never work out. Once cleared, the left is cubic while the right is at most linear: no match.

Station two: factor the denominator, using real numbers only. Try small whole numbers: two and minus two both make it vanish. Divide those out, and the denominator falls into three real factors. At minus two the graph only kisses the axis: that factor comes squared. Its discriminant is minus three, so this quadratic stays whole. In general, every real denominator breaks into just these two kinds of factor. Our integrals live in the real numbers, and real factors are all they need.

Station three: every factor spawns its own stack of boxes. The single linear factor spawns a single box. The squared factor spawns two boxes, one for each power. The quadratic spawns one box, with a linear numerator inside. Line them up: four boxes holding five unknown coefficients. Five unknowns against a fifth degree denominator: the counts agree. Here is the classic silent bug: the squared factor gets only its top box. Test it on a small case: a constant would have to equal a line. Put the missing box back, and both coefficients come out as one. The rule: a power gets one box for every exponent up to the top.

Station four: pin down the coefficients, starting by clearing denominators. It holds for every value, so we get to pick the value. Fast lane at pickup: plug in two, and every term but one disappears. So the first coefficient is one, signed for on the spot. It works because that factor multiplies every other term. Plug in minus two, and the same trick hands over minus one. Careful: this root reveals only the top box, not the lower one. For the other three, match coefficients of like powers on both sides. Solve it, and the last three fall into place. Roots get the fast lane, while the rest pay their way with algebra. And there it is: the parcel sorted into four standard boxes.

Now we sign for each box, starting with form one. First power gives a logarithm; higher powers, the power rule. Our three linear boxes give two logarithms and one power. Form two: break the top into the bottom's derivative plus a leftover constant. Ours: minus half the derivative, plus a half. The derivative piece is pure glue, straight out of the gluing episode. No absolute value needed, since this quadratic never dips below zero. For the constant piece, complete the square in the denominator. Then the arctangent formula closes the box. For higher powers, the reduction formula from the parts episode steps the power down first. Every box is signed for, and the whole integral fits on one line.

One last habit before you integrate: the split is an identity of functions. Wherever both sides make sense they agree, so test one easy point. Take a point we never used to solve: the left side is minus four ninths. The right side, box by box, lands on the same number. A match. Now watch this little test catch a slip. Copy one coefficient wrong, and the right side misses: the slip is caught. A few seconds of arithmetic before integrating can save the entire run.
