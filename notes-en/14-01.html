14-01 Abel's theorem: convergent points join into one interval

Abel's theorem: convergent points join into one interval

1 · Motivation

Abel's theorem answers one question: where can a power series converge? Pick any number for x, and it turns into an ordinary series. Every choice of x gets its own answer: converge, or diverge. Our test series converges at minus one and at one half, but fails at one. So what shape can all the convergence points take together? Could it be scattered islands, working far out but failing nearer in?

Picture a long dorm corridor, with a loudspeaker right at the stairwell. If a far room hears the announcement, every nearer room hears it louder. And if one room hears nothing, every room farther out is silent too. So the rooms that hear it form one single block around the stairwell. The only open question sits right at the two ends of that block. Abel's theorem proves that power series follow exactly this rule.

We work with power series in their basic form, built around zero. Here is Abel's theorem, word for word from the course text. All we assume is convergence at one single point other than zero. Then every point closer to zero converges, and converges absolutely. Both sides count, even the side where we never tested anything. And if it diverges there, every point farther out diverges too. The mirror point, at exactly the same distance, gets no promise at all.

From this theorem, the course text builds the radius of convergence. All the convergence points together form one interval, centered at zero. Its half length is R, the least upper bound of how far convergence reaches. Three cases: zero, positive infinity, or a finite positive radius. Inside the radius it converges; beyond it, it diverges. Right at the two endpoints, either outcome is possible. The open stretch between the endpoints is called the interval of convergence. Add whichever endpoints converge, and you get the domain of convergence. The course text's second example is our old test series. Keep that lopsided domain in mind; we will earn it by hand soon.

3 · Method

Now the proof, and its goal: spread from one convergent point inward. Convergence there forces the terms to shrink to zero, so they stay bounded. That single bound is all we keep about the coefficients. Now pick a point closer to zero; the ratio of distances is below one. Pull out the ratio: each term sits under a geometric bound. Picture it: every term hides under a geometric ceiling. Compare with a convergent geometric series, and absolute convergence follows. The closer the point sits to zero, the faster the ceiling falls. One single inequality carries the whole theorem.

The divergence half needs no new idea at all. Suppose some point farther out did converge. Then the first half forces convergence back at our divergent point. Impossible. So every point farther out must diverge.

Now watch our test series, one radius at a time. No holes: each convergent point pulls in everything nearer to zero. No islands: each divergent point rules out everything farther away. So the convergence points join into one single interval around zero. The scattered islands we feared at the start simply cannot happen.

Where the shading stops is decided by the coefficients alone. First extreme: factorials make the terms explode at any nonzero x. Only zero survives: the radius is zero. Second extreme: even at ten, the terms climb, then the factorials win. Neighbour ratios die out at every x: the radius is positive infinity.

Now for the dangerous spot: the two endpoints. Right at the radius, the ratio is one, and a flat ceiling proves nothing. Abel's theorem falls silent there, so each endpoint is checked by hand. Chapter twelve supplies the tools: the Leibniz test, p series, and Raabe's test. At one, the example becomes the harmonic series, which diverges. At minus one, the signs alternate and the terms shrink to zero, so Leibniz says converge. So the domain includes minus one but not one: two endpoints, two fates.

Four series, all with radius one. Can every endpoint pattern occur? Pause the video and decide each endpoint yourself. With squares on the bottom, both ends converge absolutely; both are included. As just shown: minus one in, one out. Leibniz rescues one, but at minus one all signs agree and it diverges. With growing coefficients, the terms never die at either end, so both ends diverge. All four patterns occur, so the radius alone never predicts the endpoints.

4 · Outlook

A bonus, without proof: inside the interval, it converges uniformly on every closed piece. The M test fence works on any smaller closed interval inside. So the property theorems apply: the sum function is continuous on the whole interval. If an endpoint converges, the uniform Abel test stretches uniformity to it.
