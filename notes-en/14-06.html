14-06 Euler's formula: e, i, and pi in one frame

Euler's formula: e, i, and pi in one frame

1 · Motivation

Euler's formula: three famous constants in one frame at last. These three each own a chapter of the course, and never pose together. Then a classmate turns up wearing all of them on one shirt. But any real power of e is always positive. So how could that power be minus one? The old recipe breaks: nobody can multiply something an imaginary number of times. We need a definition that never asks how many times.

But the Taylor series episode gave this function a second face. That series only ever adds and multiplies. And complex numbers know how to add and multiply as well. So we simply let the series define the exponential. Last time functions became series, today a series builds a function.

2 · Statements

Warm-up: a complex number is a pair, a point in the plane.
Multiplication follows its own rule for pairs.
The imaginary unit i = (0, 1) gives i² = −1.
A complex series adds terms zₙ = aₙ + i bₙ one by one.
Its partial sums walk the plane and settle at one point.
A complex series converges if and only if
its real part series and imaginary part series both converge;
if the series of moduli converges, the series converges absolutely.
Why it works: neither part can be longer than the modulus.
And the modulus is at most the two parts added.
So every chapter-12 test works on each part, unchanged.
In particular, absolute convergence still implies convergence.
Definition (5): eᶻ = 1 + z + z²/2! + ⋯ + zⁿ/n! + ⋯;
the series has radius +∞, so it converges on the whole complex plane.
Why +∞: the n-th root of 1/n! tends to 0.
Cauchy–Hadamard from 14-02 gives R = +∞, unchanged in the plane.
So eᶻ is defined at every single point of the plane.
Better still: the moduli add up to e^|z|, so it converges absolutely.
On the real axis, the new eᶻ is exactly the old eˣ.

Warm up first: a complex number is just a pair, a point in the plane. Multiplying two pairs follows its own special rule. Apply the rule to the imaginary unit: its square is minus one.

A complex series adds its terms one at a time. Its partial sums take a walk, and settle down at one point. The course text: a complex series converges exactly when its real part series and its imaginary part series both converge. and if the term sizes add up to something finite, it converges absolutely. The reason: neither shadow is ever longer than the arrow. And the arrow is never longer than the two legs together. So every test from chapter twelve carries over, untouched. In particular, absolute convergence still forces convergence.

The course text defines the exponential by this very series. Its radius is infinite, so it converges on the entire complex plane. Why infinite? The coefficient roots shrink to zero. The Cauchy Hadamard formula works verbatim: the radius is infinite. So the exponential now makes sense at every point of the plane. Better still, the term sizes add up to a finite exponential, so convergence is absolute. On the real axis, the new exponential is exactly the old one.

3 · Method

The key move: feed in a purely imaginary number. Everything depends on the powers of the imaginary unit. The powers cycle through four values, then come back to one. Each factor of i is a quarter turn.

Now feed that cycle into the series, one term at a time. The even powers all come out as real numbers. The odd powers each keep exactly one factor of i. Nothing was reshuffled: the two columns are simply the real and imaginary part series. The real part series is an old friend from the Taylor table: cosine. The imaginary part series is the other old friend: sine. Both columns converge, and the criterion puts them back together. One series, two columns, two old friends.

That is Euler's formula, boxed. And it holds for every real number, with no exceptions. Now plug in pi. Cosine gives minus one, sine gives zero: minus one it is. Move the one across, and zero appears. Five constants, one group photo, and no magic: just cosine and sine at pi.

Now put this point on the plane. Its distance from the origin is always exactly one. As the angle grows, the point walks the rim, like a friend circling a roundabout. You read where they are off two axes: cosine across, sine up. It walks at unit speed: the angle is exactly the distance walked. Velocity stays perpendicular to position, the circular motion picture from the derivative episodes. A quarter lap brings it exactly to i. Half a lap lands on minus one: the shirt formula, as a walk.

Any complex number can be written as a length times a turn. The modulus sets the length, the angle sets the direction. Multiply by a point on the circle, and the whole plane turns. The multiplication rule confirms it: a pure rotation, nothing else.

And sums in the exponent turn into products. The proof multiplies the two series: a Cauchy product. Each diagonal collapses neatly, thanks to the binomial theorem. But adding along diagonals is a reshuffle. Are we allowed to? Yes, both converge absolutely, and the product theorem from the absolute convergence episode applies. The rearrangement lesson: absolutely convergent sums do not care about order. Without it, a convergent alternating series can square into a divergent one. Put two turns into the law: turning twice means adding the angles. Compare the two parts, and the angle addition formulas come for free. And De Moivre's formula is left to you, as a course text exercise.

4 · Outlook

First use: rewrite a simple complex number as a length and a turn. Its eighth power: eight eighth turns make one lap, and the answer is sixteen. Second use: the sixth roots of one sit evenly around the circle.

Circuit and signal courses use this from day one: alternating current becomes a spinning arrow, and differential equations turn into plain algebra. Later, in complex analysis, one derivative brings all the others with it. And the flat counterexample from the Taylor episode dies there. Next chapter's Fourier series stay real, but this spinning picture keeps coming back. A series built a function, and three constants finally share one frame.
