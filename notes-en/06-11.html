06-11 Auxiliary function constructions: walk backwards from the conclusion

Auxiliary function constructions: walk backwards from the conclusion

1 · Motivation

Auxiliary functions, built by walking backwards from the conclusion. A familiar exam problem gives a function that is zero at both ends. It wants an inside point where position times slope equals height. The first reflex is Rolle's theorem on f itself, giving a flat tangent. Correct, but the parcel landed at the wrong address.

Rolle's theorem needs the two ends at the same height. But the problem hands you a conclusion, not two equal ends. So we trace the parcel backwards, from the doorstep to the warehouse. Every construction answers one question, what to subtract so both ends agree.

Replay the founding move, starting from a curve with unequal ends. Subtract the chord, and the curve tilts until both ends sit at zero. Rolle gives a flat tangent, which on the original curve is parallel to the chord. That, in five seconds, was the proof of Lagrange's theorem. Today that single move grows into a toolkit, four cards and one check.

The engine underneath everything is Rolle's theorem. The first two clauses mean no breaks and no sharp corners. Clause three, equal ends, is the one we usually have to manufacture. The payoff is a flat tangent somewhere inside. Key reading, Rolle makes a derivative vanish, not the function itself. So the target has to be read as the derivative of some F equal to zero.

The construction behind Lagrange's theorem subtracts the chord. Check both ends, and each one comes out to zero. And a flat tangent here means the original matches the chord's slope.

Cauchy's construction does the same, measuring with a second function. Same check, and both ends come out to zero again. Rolle then says the two rates keep a fixed ratio at one shared instant. Both are Rolle in costume, the original minus a correction that evens the ends.

3 · Method

Think of four saved route presets, and the destination picks which one. Card one, direct difference, for targets that contain a chord slope. The recipe subtracts the chord, exactly as in Lagrange's own proof.

Card two, the exponential factor, for targets mixing f and its derivative. The recipe multiplies f by an exponential factor. Differentiate, and the target pops out inside the bracket. The exponential never vanishes, so a flat tangent for F is exactly the target.

Instance, if f is zero at both ends, some point has slope equal to height. Lambda is minus one; damp f, and the ends stay zero. Rolle flattens F at one point, and there slope equals height.

Card three, split and splice, for targets that name two different points. Cut at a chosen point, the middle, or one the intermediate value theorem supplies. Instance, the heights at zero, one and two are zero, one and three. On the left half the chord climbs at one, and so does the tangent somewhere. On the right half the chord climbs at two, and a tangent matches it. Different halves, so the two points can never coincide.

Card four, symmetry, for targets that pair a point with its mirror image. Build the new function by adding f to its own mirror image. Its two ends agree automatically, both equal the same sum. Rolle then says a point and its mirror share the same slope. Fine print, the middle point satisfies this identity for free. So swap to the twin, f minus its mirror. Lagrange then says the two mirror slopes add up to twice the chord slope.

Now the backward method, starting at the doorstep, which is the target. Back to our exam problem, the one Rolle on f could not reach. Step one, gather everything on one side so it reads as zero. Step two, ask whose derivative looks like this. The quotient rule rings a bell, that numerator is right there. Divide by that safe nonzero square, and the auxiliary function appears.

Here is the new function beside the old one, same zeros, peak nudged left. Check one, both ends of F sit at zero. Check two, a flat tangent for F is exactly the target, scaled by a nonzero factor. Rolle finishes the job, and the tangent at that point aims at the origin. The skill is to read the target as a derivative in disguise. Multiply or divide until a familiar line from the derivative table shows up.

Where do the cards come from? The conclusion votes for its construction. Card one comes from geometry, see a chord slope, subtract the chord. Card two comes from wanting the derivative to be the target, so we multiply by a factor. Card three comes from the two points the conclusion names. Card four comes from the mirror symmetry inside the conclusion.

Before writing the word Rolle, take the doorstep photo, a quick self check. A test case, a function that starts and ends at height one. The target, some point where the slope is minus the height. The naive move is to call the target itself the auxiliary function. But Rolle flattens the derivative, which here would need a second derivative.

Use card two instead, so the derivative carries the target. Check one, the left end gives one, the right end gives e. Unequal, the check fails, and the failure itself is telling us something. Try the constant function one, its slope is zero, never minus one. So the given data simply cannot carry this target.

Repair the data, let the right end be one over e, and both ends match. Two checks, equal ends, and the derivative is the target up to a nonzero factor. Equal ends alone prove nothing, the plain function has them and still misses. On the exam, a mean value proof becomes a two minute sorting job. Pick the card, write the function, self check, then call on Rolle or Lagrange once.

4 · Outlook

First use, between two zeros of a polynomial, its derivative plus itself has a zero. Card two again, and here the zero lands near 0.618. Second use, from zero to one, two slopes whose reciprocals add to two. Card three, cut at height one half, found by the intermediate value theorem. Two Lagrange steps, and the two pieces add up to exactly two.

Every card is Rolle or the chord shift from episodes one and two of this chapter, in costume. Targets with two functions ride on Cauchy's construction, from episode four of this chapter. Card three's cut point often comes from chapter four's intermediate value theorem.

Card two returns, word for word, as the integrating factor of first order linear differential equations. And walking back from the conclusion is how proofs get found in every later course. The destination picks the route, and the doorstep photo confirms delivery.
