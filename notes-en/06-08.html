06-08 Taylor's formula, part two: the error has a price tag

Taylor's formula, part two: the error has a price tag

1 · Motivation

Today, the leftover piece of Taylor's formula finally gets a price tag. Your calculator flashes e to nine decimals. But which of those digits can you actually trust? Last time, the exponential became its Taylor polynomial plus a little-o tail. Plug in one and stop after four terms: about 2.6667. The very first decimal is already wrong, and the little-o term never warned us. Little-o only speaks about the moment the input shrinks toward zero. At one itself, it hands us no number at all, not even a rough one.

Picture two delivery messages: one says arriving soon, the other says by Friday at six. Peano's remainder is the first message: true, but with no time attached. Lagrange's remainder is the second one: a real window, by Friday evening. You never learn which van is carrying your parcel, only the window it will land in. That unknown van is xi, and the window is the bound on the error.

So there are really two questions: can we ignore the error, and how big can it get? Peano answers the first one: limits, and how things behave right near the base point. Lagrange answers the second one: approximate values and honest error estimates. The tricky sine limit goes on the left; e to six decimals goes on the right.

Hypothesis two: one more derivative, f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾, exists on the open interval (a, b).

Conclusion: f(x) equals the Taylor polynomial plus one extra term.

Here is Theorem 6.10, word for word as the course text states it. First hypothesis: the function and its derivatives up to order n stay continuous on the closed interval. Second hypothesis: one more derivative exists, but only on the open interval. It is the same pattern as Lagrange's mean value theorem: continuous on the closed, differentiable on the open. The conclusion: the function equals its Taylor polynomial, plus one extra term. And that extra term looks just like the next Taylor term, with one small change. The top derivative sits at a hidden point xi, not at the base. Xi sits strictly inside that stretch, and that is all we are told.

The course text calls this final term the Lagrange-type remainder. Theta is the fraction of the trip already covered at that hidden point. Center it at zero and you get the Maclaurin formula, number eight.

Now read the remainder as a mean value sentence. Same shape: a derivative at an unknown point, times a power of the step. Two upgrades: the order goes up by one, and a factorial sits underneath. Xi exists, but it has no address. No formula will ever tell you where it lives. The working habit: cap that top derivative by one number M across the whole interval. Then, wherever xi hides, the remainder stays under the same ceiling. Let the worst case speak for every possible hiding place.

3 · Method

Now shrink n to zero: the polynomial is just the starting value. The course text says it plainly: at order zero, formula seven is exactly the Lagrange formula. So Taylor's theorem is a grown-up version of the mean value theorem. We met it in the mean value episode; it is the order zero member of this family.

How do we prove it? With Cauchy's mean value theorem, from a few episodes back. Freeze the target point, and let a fresh variable t run toward it. F measures what is left over when the Taylor polynomial is built at the moving point. Its partner G is just the bare power of the step, one order higher. When t reaches the target, both of them hit zero: the perfect setup for Cauchy. Differentiate F, and the terms cancel in pairs until a single one survives. Cauchy's theorem hands us the ratio, and out pops the remainder exactly.

That is why the hypotheses look the way they do: F has to qualify for Cauchy. The chapter's whole family in one frame, mean value theorem in the middle. Cauchy powers the proof, and at order zero Taylor lands right back on the mean value theorem.

First real job: compute e with an error below one in a million. Every derivative of the exponential is itself again, so the bookkeeping is easy. Plug in one, and e becomes a sum of factorial fractions plus one remainder. Theta is another anonymous van, yet that factor always stays below three. So the error stays below three over a factorial, a window set by n alone. With n at eight, the window is still wider than one in a million. With n at nine, it finally drops below one in a million. Every extra term is a prepayment, and the delivery window shrinks again. Ten terms, from order zero to nine, add up to 2.7182815. The true e sits in a sliver of a window, just above that sum. That is the budget habit: pick n first, then pay with one single bound.

4 · Outlook

Second job: the sine of one half, with the very same skill. Every derivative of sine is a sine or cosine, trapped between minus one and one. So the remainder has a clean cap, and xi is never needed. Let the cubic stand in for the sine of one half. Free upgrade: the fourth-power coefficient is zero, so the cubic counts as order four. So the error is at most about 0.00026, ten times tighter than before. The value is 0.47917, with an error under 0.0003. A digit only counts as known when its error band is written right beside it.

Here is the price of never knowing xi: every bound is a worst case. For e, the bound is about 2.7 times the true error. Safe but generous: you planned around the slowest van that could possibly come. Compare that with the sine limit from last time, which never needed a bound at all. There the little-o tail just had to vanish faster than the cube, and Peano was enough. Rule of thumb: a limit calls for Peano, a number with digits calls for Lagrange.

That pays off the debt from last episode: the little-o tail now has a real price. And the whole mean value family of this chapter closes into a single net. Next episode, the second sufficient condition for extrema just reads this second-order line for its sign. Further down the road, numerical analysis turns this price tag into a budget discipline. You choose n so that the truncation error fits under the digits the machine can hold. Balanced against rounding error, that is the daily craft of scientific computing. Peano tells you the error fades away; Lagrange tells you how big it can be. And a number without its error band is only half a number.
