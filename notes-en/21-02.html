Switching the order of integration: draw the region first, then compute

1 · Motivation

Switching the order of integration. Draw the region first, then compute. Last episode built the double integral, but never computed one. Tonight's homework is this integral over a small triangle.

A classmate integrates in y first, on the inside. The inner step needs an antiderivative of that exponential. The course text says no elementary formula exists for it. Stuck, like a door that will not open from this side.

Meanwhile, two roommates add up the parcels on the same shelf. One of them adds the shelf level by level. The other goes column by column. Both get twenty six kilograms. Same total, two routes. But on an uneven shelf, one route can be much easier. A double integral is the same shelf, with infinitely thin levels. Today's question: which way to slice, and how to write the bounds.

Start easy: a rectangular base under a curved roof. Freeze one value of x, and cut the solid there. The area of the cut face is the inner integral. Let x slide across, and the faces sweep out the whole solid. The outer integral adds all those faces up. For this roof, the face shrinks as x grows. Adding them all up gives a volume of exactly 3. It is the slice and add idea from chapter ten, run in two steps. Slice the other way, holding y fixed, and you get 3 again. Levels or columns, it is the same shelf and the same total.

3 · Method

Now the most dangerous step of the chapter: switching the order. The classic bug is swapping bounds by algebra, without a sketch. The cure is a four step routine. Read the old bounds, sketch the region, slice the other way, write new bounds.

Example: let us switch the order of this one. Step one, read: y starts on the parabola and climbs to height 1. Step two, sketch it: above the parabola, below the top line. Step three: cut the other way, with a horizontal line. It enters at the y axis and leaves through the parabola. Step four, write it down, and the switch is done.

Now the tempting wrong answer, the one that looks symmetric. Draw the region it really describes. That is the piece below the parabola, a different region entirely. Put in the constant one, and the areas disagree: two thirds against one third. Never switch by algebra alone. Let the sketch do it.

Back to tonight's homework. Its three sides are the y axis, the top line, and the diagonal. Order one slices vertically, from the diagonal up to the top. The inner integral runs straight into the same wall. Order two slices horizontally, from the y axis to the diagonal. Now the troublesome factor just sits still, like a constant. And the inner integral comes out in one line. The door that was stuck opens from the other side. One substitution later, the answer is about four hundredths. The lesson: pick the order before you compute, not after you drown. And the wall itself? Episode twenty one oh eight measures it, by going up a dimension.

A second example, where the region fights back. Find the area of the triangle these three lines cut out. Three corners: the origin and two mirror points. Slice vertically, and the roof changes at the corner, so we need two pieces. Each piece gives three quarters: one and a half in all. Slice horizontally, and now the right wall bends, two pieces again. No surprise: the triangle is its own mirror image across the diagonal. And half base times height agrees, the old school way. Lesson: when a boundary changes formula, you must split. Count the corners first.

A two second scan: the outer bounds must be plain numbers. Any bound with a variable in it belongs inside. Caught at a glance: the outer bound mentions a variable that is already gone.

4 · Outlook

Where does this pay off every day? In statistics. Take a joint density on the unit square, heavier toward the far corner. Integrate along each vertical line: out comes the marginal density of X. Switch the order, and the same theorem hands you the marginal of Y. And each marginal still adds up to one, just like the joint density.

A second use, from the 800 meter test: speed piles up from acceleration. And distance piles up from speed: two layers of integrals. Switch the order over that triangle, and one layer is left. A push early in the race keeps paying for the rest of it.

An honest warning: the hypotheses are doing real work. In the course text's exercises, the double integral exists, yet both iterated integrals fail. And the reverse happens too: the iterated integrals exist, the double integral does not. So check the hypotheses before switching. Continuity is the safe bet.

Later comes the general Fubini theorem: the same statement in looser clothes. And in probability, marginal densities become a daily tool. So, draw the region first, then compute.
