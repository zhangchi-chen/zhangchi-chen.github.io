15-02 The convergence theorem: at a jump, the midpoint wins

The convergence theorem: at a jump, the midpoint wins

1 · Motivation

Today: what a Fourier series does when its function jumps. Last time we stopped at a promise, not an equation. Today we earn the equals sign, but only under conditions.

Our test case: flat on the left, a straight ramp on the right. Last episode's recipe gives its coefficients right away. So the real question: right at the end point, what does the series add up to? Classmate one: the ramp tops out at pi, so that's the answer. Classmate two: step past the end, you're back at the start, and it's zero there. Both of them reasoned carefully, and still they disagree.

So let the series speak for itself, adding one term at a time. Watch the partial sums there creep upward, toward half of pi. Half of pi: exactly halfway between the two reports. Why halfway? And when can we trust the series in the first place? One theorem answers both.

2 · Statements

Theorem 15.3, the convergence theorem, in the course text's words.
Piecewise smooth, as the course text defines it.
Clause one, piecewise smooth: finitely many smooth arcs.
Allowed: discontinuities of the first kind — both one-sided limits exist.
At finitely many points f′ may break, keeping finite one-sided limits.
Our f qualifies: a jump at ±π, a change of slope at 0.
Clause two, 'at every x': convergence point by point.
No uniform convergence is promised: near the jump, points lag far behind.
Clause three: the sum is the average of the two one-sided limits.
f(x₀+0) is the height you meet coming in from the right.
f(x₀−0) is the height you meet coming in from the left.
At a continuity point they agree, so the series gives f(x).
At a jump they disagree, and the series reports their midpoint.
The corollary: continuous and piecewise smooth means equality everywhere.
Careful: 'continuous' includes the seams where periods meet.
Our f fails there: at each seam, height π meets height 0.

Here is the convergence theorem, exactly as the course text states it. First the key condition, piecewise smooth, in the text's own words. First phrase, piecewise smooth: the graph is finitely many smooth pieces. Jumps are allowed, as long as the graph has a definite height on each side. At a few points the slope may snap to a new value, but each side settles.

Our example passes: it jumps at the two ends and bends in the middle. Second phrase, at every point: each point settles on its own schedule. Nobody promised the same pace everywhere; points near the jump lag far behind.

Third phrase: the series lands on the average of the two side limits. The right limit is the height you meet walking in from the right. The left limit is the height you meet walking in from the left. Where the graph is unbroken, both sides agree: the series gives the function's value. Where the graph jumps, the sides disagree, and the series reports the halfway value.

The corollary: if nothing ever jumps, the series equals the function everywhere. Careful: continuous means continuous where one period meets the next. Our example fails at the seam, where the top of the ramp meets the flat floor.

3 · Method

We'll use this theorem, not prove it; the proof waits in the course text. Still, we can see why halfway is the only fair report. Every coefficient is an integral, so it listens to both sides of the jump. When a partial sum looks around a point, it weighs left and right exactly alike. Picture two classmates on opposite sides of a door, each reporting what they saw. A fair report favors neither side, so it lands right in the middle. So the halfway value is not a rule someone chose; symmetry hands it to us.

Now the exam favorite: what happens at the two ends? Periodicity welds the two ends together, and the interval closes into a loop. Think of a corridor whose two ends are really one door. Arriving from the left, you see the top of the ramp. Step through the door, and you're on the flat floor at the other end. The course text's first example does exactly this, and lands on half of pi. Not pi, not zero, but half of pi, at both ends at once. That's the trap: the end has a value, and the series ignores it. The rule at the two ends: average what you see from each side of the door. And the partial sums confirm it: both ends drift toward half of pi.

Here's a sneaky idea: change the function at just one point. Lift the value at the origin to two; the conditions still hold. One point has no width, so no integral can notice it. Same integrals, same coefficients, so the very same series. At that point the series still returns zero, not two. Same at the end: move its value anywhere, and the series still says half of pi. The series listens only to the two sides, never to the point itself. The series reaches your new value only if that value already sits halfway.

First payoff of the equals sign: feed in a point, and out comes a number fact. Where the graph is unbroken, use the function's value; at a jump, the halfway value. Put in pi: every sine dies, and every cosine flips to plus or minus one. The theorem gives half of pi; the series keeps only the odd terms. Those odd terms keep piling up, closing in on one fixed number. Clear the fractions, and the course text's equation appears. There it is: the odd squares add up to pi squared over eight. It's the first special value of the course; episode fifteen oh five mass-produces them. Check it at the origin, where the graph is unbroken: the same number falls out.

A standard companion fact, Gibbs: next to a jump the partial sums overshoot, and the bump never shrinks. Every partial sum is unbroken, so it has to climb across the gap. Recall the tube from thirteen oh two: around a jump, no thin tube can hold a partial sum. So across a jump the series settles point by point, never all together. Unlike the term test from twelve oh one, smoothness decides here, not size.

4 · Outlook

The hypotheses work like an entrance exam: pass them first, then write equals. A few jumps are fine; they pass the exam. A break that wiggles forever fails, and the theorem promises nothing there. That settles last episode's debt: under these conditions, the wavy sign becomes equals. With the equals sign in hand, the rest of the chapter can lean on it.

First use, audio: keep only a few harmonics of a square wave, and its edges start to ring. Second use, photos: the halo beside sharp edges in a compressed picture is that same overshoot. And the full proof is a debt we leave for a later course. Looking ahead: the proof runs on locality; what happens at a point depends only on its neighborhood. In signal processing, that idea becomes wavelets, which study one spot at a time. And later, real analysis proves convergence for far rougher functions than these. Unbroken points give the function, jumps give halfway, and the ends average across the door.
