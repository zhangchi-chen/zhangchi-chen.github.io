13-03 The M-test: one fence pins every tail

The M-test: one fence pins every tail

1 · Motivation

The M test: one fence that pins down every tail. Here is the homework: does this series converge uniformly on the whole line? Last time we had one tool: watch the worst error shrink to zero. But that needs the sum function first, and nobody gave it to us.

So we turn to the terms, one wiggle at a time. Each wiggle stays under its own ceiling, everywhere. Now line up those ceilings as a row of fence posts. And together the posts add up to a finite total, a benchmark from twelve oh two.

Picture a pile of deliveries whose boxes change size every day. But box number n always fits under its own shelf. The shelf heights add up to a finite budget, so any stack of late boxes is short. And it holds on every day at once, which is exactly what uniform means. One convergent series of plain numbers can pin down infinitely many functions at once.

2 · Statements

Here is the theorem, as the course text states it.
Clause one: all the terms uₙ(x) live on one set D.
Clause two: ΣMₙ is a convergent positive-term series of plain numbers.
Clause three: |uₙ(x)| ≤ Mₙ at every x in D.
And for every n: one number Mₙ for each term.
Conclusion: then Σuₙ(x) converges uniformly on D.
Its other names: the M-test, or the test of dominant series.
ΣMₙ is said to dominate Σuₙ(x) on D: a dominant series.
Read it slowly: Mₙ contains no x at all.
So one N will serve every x — the one-ruler idea from 13-02.
Now break a clause: let the bound depend on x, as for Σxⁿ on (−1, 1).
Here |xⁿ| ≤ |x|ⁿ, and Σ|x|ⁿ converges at each single x…
…but those totals blow up near the ends — no single fence survives.
Bounds that move with x buy only pointwise convergence.
And ΣMₙ must converge: posts of height 1 prove nothing.
Recipe: pick numbers Mₙ, check the bound, check ΣMₙ converges.

Here is the theorem, word for word from the course text. First, all the terms live on the same set. Second, a convergent series of positive numbers, with no x anywhere. Third, each term stays within its bound at every point. And it holds for every term, one number per term. And the payoff: the series converges uniformly on the whole set.

It goes by two other names: the M test, and the test of dominant series. The number series is said to dominate the function series. Read slowly: each bound is a plain number, with no x. So one cutoff will serve every point, the one ruler idea from last episode.

Now break a clause: let the bound depend on the point, and test the geometric series. At each single point, those bounds do add up to something finite. But near the ends those totals blow up, and no single fence survives. Bounds that change from point to point only buy convergence point by point. The bounds must sum to a finite total; bounds of one prove nothing. So the recipe is: choose the numbers, check the bound, check the sum is finite.

3 · Method

Why should it be true? The whole proof fits in two lines. The engine underneath is the uniform Cauchy criterion. It asks that every block of the tail be small everywhere, with one cutoff. Step one: the number series converges, so by the old Cauchy criterion its tail blocks are tiny. And that cutoff comes from numbers alone; the point never enters. Step two: the triangle inequality, then each term's bound, at every point. So every block of the tail sits below the bar, everywhere at once. The criterion is satisfied, so the series converges uniformly, and we are done. All parts are recycled: the criterion from twelve oh one, the one cutoff from thirteen oh two.

Back to the homework, the sine series on the whole line. Sine never gets bigger than one, so each post is one over n squared. And the posts sum to a finite total, a p series straight from the benchmark bank. So it converges uniformly on the whole line, and the fence took seconds. The cosine series takes the very same fence. Notice what we never needed: the sum itself. That is its edge over last episode's sup criterion: no limit function needed in advance. And that pays off last episode's debt: proof of uniformity, without knowing the limit.

When the right post is not obvious, find each term's highest point. Its peak sits at one spot, half as tall as one over n squared. So that peak height is our post, and the whole line is covered. Rule of thumb: each term's highest point is the shortest post you can ever use.

Now the exam favourite: the geometric series on the open interval. On the whole open interval, the convergence is not uniform. Last episode did the work: the worst remainder grows without bound. But shrink the set to a closed interval a little inside. There each power stays under a fixed post; those posts sum to a finite total. So it is uniform on every such closed interval inside. The course text calls this being uniform on closed subintervals. The fence holds on every marked aisle, but not on the whole open floor. Here is the table to remember: the set you ask about decides the answer. The trap: uniform on every closed piece does not mean uniform on the open whole. Each fence exists, but their totals explode as the interval reaches for the ends.

Before building any fence, there is a quick first check. If the terms themselves fail to fade to zero uniformly, stop right there. Live use: each power still reaches height one near the right end. So the series fails on that interval, refuted in a single line. And the bigger interval fails too, because it contains this one. Careful, though: passing this check proves nothing at all. Its terms fade uniformly, yet their best posts add up to infinity.

Be honest: these spikes converge uniformly, yet no convergent fence exists. Series that oscillate call for specialists. Here the fence collapses: its posts are at least the harmonic series, which diverges. There the uniform Abel and Dirichlet tests take over. Today we only name them; their first real job comes in fourteen oh one.

4 · Outlook

First use: any trigonometric series whose coefficients add up absolutely to something finite. The fence is just the size of the coefficients, the same at every point. Uniform on the whole line, a fact the Fourier series chapter will lean on.

Second use: the famous function Weierstrass built from cosine waves. The fence is a geometric series, so the convergence is uniform on the whole line. And the famous punchline: it is continuous everywhere, yet has a slope nowhere. Why we want uniformity at all is the next debt: three theorems about the sum function.

So remember the picture: one convergent series of numbers pins every tail at once. A glimpse ahead: this fence idea keeps growing in later courses. In differential equations, existence proofs fence in a chain of approximate solutions. Comparison functions do the job there: the same fence, with taller posts. One fence, every point, every tail: that is the M test.
