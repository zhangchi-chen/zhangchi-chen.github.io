Episode 02-06 · Mathematical Analysis

Subsequences & the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem

Theorem (Bolzano–Weierstrass)

Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.

1 · What a subsequence is

A subsequence is what a remote that only fast-forwards can stop at: indices must strictly increase.

Definition

(ank)k≥1 is a subsequence of (an) when n1 < n2 < n3 < ⋯

Non-examples

  • ✗ (a3, a3, a7) — index 3 used twice (a replay)
  • ✗ (a7, a3, a12) — 7 → 3 is a rewind

Consequence: nk ≥ k for every k, because k strictly increasing positive integers end at k or later.

2 · The bookkeeping of “take the max”

When a band holds infinitely many terms, a construction picks the next term with

nk+1 > max(n1, …, nk), equivalently nk+1 > nk

Cross off used indices, move the pointer forward, pick any surviving term in the band. Infinitely many terms in the band means a survivor always exists.

✎

This step carries no mathematical content beyond “the pointer only moves forward”.

3 · The peak-point route

Call am a peak if am ≥ an for all n > m.

Case 1: infinitely many peaks → the peaks form a non-increasing subsequence.

Case 2: finitely many peaks → after the last peak every term is exceeded by a later one; jumping forward to larger terms gives an increasing subsequence.

Lemma. Every sequence has a monotone subsequence.

Peak lemma (monotone subsequence) + bounded ⟹ monotone and bounded ⟹ convergent. This proves the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem using sequence tools only.

4 · The other route (deferred)

Bisection / nested intervals: keep halving, always keeping a half that contains infinitely many terms.

📌

Proof deferred — it rests on completeness and will be collected in the Completeness chapter.

5 · What it buys

an = (−1)n diverges, yet a2k = 1 → 1 and a2k−1 = −1 → −1: two constant subsequences.

Every bounded sequence hides convergent subsequences in its oscillation. Next episode: upper and lower limits — the largest and smallest limits you can hide in a sequence.

Self-check

  1. Which of these is a subsequence of (an)?
    • A. (a2, a2, a5, …)
    • B. (a4, a9, a16, …)
    • C. (a5, a3, a8, …)
    Correct answer: B. Only 4 < 9 < 16 strictly increases; the others replay or rewind an index.
  2. For every subsequence (ank), which inequality always holds?
    • A. nk ≤ k
    • B. nk ≥ k
    • C. nk = k
    Correct answer: B. The k-th of k strictly increasing positive integers is at least k.
  3. Why do constructions require nk+1 > max(n1, …, nk)?
    • A. It is where convergence is proved
    • B. It only keeps the indices strictly increasing
    • C. It shrinks the band
    Correct answer: B. It is pure bookkeeping: the pointer only moves forward, nothing more.
  4. When is am a peak?
    • A. am ≥ an for all n > m
    • B. am ≥ an for all n < m
    • C. am > am+1 only
    Correct answer: A. A peak is at least as large as every later term.
  5. If a sequence has only finitely many peaks, what can we build after the last peak?
    • A. A non-increasing subsequence of peaks
    • B. An increasing subsequence
    • C. Nothing in general
    Correct answer: B. Each term after the last peak is exceeded by a later term, so jumping forward gives an increasing subsequence.
  6. What about an = (−1)n?
    • A. It converges to 0
    • B. It diverges but has convergent subsequences
    • C. It has no convergent subsequence
    Correct answer: B. The even and odd terms form constant subsequences 1 and −1.
