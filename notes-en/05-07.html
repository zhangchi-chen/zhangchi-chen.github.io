05-07 Parametric curves: dy/dx is a ratio of two rates

Parametric curves: dy/dx is a ratio of two rates

1 · Motivation

Parametric curves, and why a slope is really a ratio of two speeds. Picture the dot of a friend gliding across the campus map. The app knows how fast it moves east, and, separately, how fast it moves north. But your eye only cares where the arrow is heading. That heading is a slope, how far north for each step east. Two east and one north, so the slope is one half.

Now the homework version of the same question. The task: find how steep it is at time zero. First reflex: solve for the time, then plug it in. Bad news: no elementary formula gives the time back. The tangent is clearly there, yet we cannot reach it. Another trick is to cancel the d t. Fast, but is it a reason? Today we find the honest reason behind that ratio.

2 · Statements

The setup, in the course text's words.
A point moves; at time t it sits at (φ(t), ψ(t)).
t is the parameter: a clock, not a coordinate.
Nudge the clock by Δt; the point jumps to a nearby spot.
Join the two spots: that's a secant.
Its slope: change in ψ over change in φ.
Divide top and bottom by Δt: fair, both are real numbers.
Let Δt → 0: top and bottom tend to the two derivatives.
The bottom's limit isn't zero, so the secant slope → ψ′(t₀)/φ′(t₀).
So: tangent slope = vertical speed ÷ horizontal speed.
Why demand φ′(t₀) ≠ 0? The bottom must not vanish.
Zero east speed: the arrow points due north, no finite slope.
Formula (2) says the same for every t.
It names its route: y = ψ(φ⁻¹(x)), a function of x through t.
Undo φ to get back t, then feed t to ψ.
Well-behaved curves get a name: smooth curves.
Continuous derivatives: the two speed dials never jump.
φ′² + ψ′² ≠ 0: the two speeds are never both zero.
The point never stops, so it always has a heading.
Hence a tangent everywhere, its angle α(t) turning continuously.
A counterexample: x = t³, y = |t|³.
Both have continuous derivatives, yet the curve is a V.
At t = 0 both speeds are zero: the point stops at the corner.
Stopped, it can turn a sharp corner; moving, it can't.

Here is the setup, word for word from the course text. A point travels, and each moment fixes where it is. The parameter is a clock, not a coordinate. Nudge the clock a little, and the point hops nearby. Join the two spots, and you get a secant. Its slope is the rise over the run. Divide top and bottom by the time step; these are real numbers. Shrink the time step, and top and bottom settle on two speeds. The bottom does not shrink to zero, so the secant settles into the tangent. So the tangent slope is vertical speed over horizontal speed. Why insist that the bottom is never zero? With no eastward speed, the arrow points straight north and the slope breaks.

Formula two says the same thing at every moment. It names the hidden function hiding behind the curve. Undo one map to recover the time, then apply the other.

Well behaved paths get their own name: smooth curves. Continuous derivatives mean the two speed dials never jump. And the two speeds are never zero at the same moment. The point never stops, so it always knows where it is heading. So there is a tangent everywhere, and its angle turns without jumps.

Now a counterexample built from two cubes. Both formulas have continuous derivatives, yet the path is a sharp V. At time zero both speeds die, and the point stops at the corner. Only by stopping can it turn a sharp corner; while moving, it cannot.

3 · Method

The reason runs on two gears from the chain rule episode. Step one: the height is a function of the width, via time. The chain rule splits it into two rates. But the second factor is a puzzle; we never had time as a function of position. The inverse rule says: just flip the other rate. Flipping only works when that rate is not zero, the same condition again. Mesh the two gears, and the formula drops out. Every step was a proven rule; nothing got cancelled. So cancelling is a memory aid, not a reason. That symbol is not a number yet; the next episode gives it meaning.

First example from the course text: the top half of an ellipse. First, the two speeds. Divide them, and a cotangent appears. Halfway round, the slope is zero, flat at the top. Slide toward the right end, and the slope plunges toward negative infinity. That matches the ellipse: flat on top, steep at both ends. At the end point the horizontal speed is zero, and the formula is off duty. Yet the tangent exists; the vertical speed is alive, so it stands upright. At this point the slope is minus one half. One point plus one slope, and the tangent line is written.

Next, the cycloid, traced by a pebble stuck in your bike tyre. Here are its equations, with the wheel radius as the scale. Again, first the two speeds. Divide and simplify, and a tidy half angle cotangent appears. A quarter of the way round, it climbs at forty five degrees. Half a turn, and the tangent lies flat on top of the arch. A full turn: the pebble touches the road and stops for an instant. Right there the path grows a cusp, the very stop we warned about. It arrives heading straight down and leaves heading straight up.

Polar curves join in: the angle simply plays the clock. One named example: the cardioid, a heart shaped curve. The course text has a formula for the angle between tangent and radius; we only name it.

One last temptation: going one order higher. The guess: just copy the pattern with second derivatives. Two classmates test it on the cycloid, a quarter turn in. Classmate A follows the guess and gets zero. Classmate B computes honestly and gets minus one. Their first readouts agreed; now one says zero, the other minus one. The first formula was earned by two rules; the guess was merely copied. Why it breaks is left for the last episode of this chapter.

4 · Outlook

Back to the homework problem from the start. The two speeds are two and one. Slope one half, tangent done, and we never solved for the time. The same two east, one north as the dot on the map.

First use: the tip of a robotics club arm, logged against time. Its heading comes straight from the ratio of the two speeds. Second use: a basketball shot after class. At zero point four seconds the slope hits zero: the top of the arc.

Looking back, this pays the promise of chapter five, episode four: two rules as a pair. Looking ahead, the next episode makes this division mean what it says. The zero versus minus one puzzle waits for the chapter finale. A wheel, a robot arm, a map app: motion logged by time comes in pairs. And the ratio of the two speeds is where you are really heading. A ratio of two speeds: the arrow on the map.
