Motivation

  1. The differential: a linear stand-in that survives a change of variable.
  2. Two lines in your notes seem to argue with each other.
  3. Line one: dy/dx is a single symbol, not a fraction.
  4. Line two: the parametric formula looks exactly like cancelling dt.
  5. If it is not a fraction, why does cancelling never go wrong?
  6. Today dy and dx become two real numbers, and the slash earns its place.
  7. A square photo for your dorm wall, side x₀ = 10 cm.
  8. Its area is the side squared: S = x², here 100 cm².
  9. Enlarge the print: the side grows by a small Δx, say 1 cm.
  10. The extra area: two long strips plus one small corner.
  11. Each strip is x₀ by Δx, so together they make 2x₀Δx.
  12. The corner is Δx by Δx, that is (Δx)².
  13. With 10 cm and 1 cm: strips 20 cm², corner just 1 cm².
  14. Halve Δx: the strips halve, but the corner drops to a quarter.
  15. Divide the corner by Δx: you get Δx, which goes to zero.
  16. So the corner is an infinitesimal of higher order than Δx.
  17. The strip part, linear in Δx, is the differential dS = 2x₀Δx.

What the statement says

  1. Here is the definition, as the course text states it.
  2. Give x₀ a step Δx and watch the change Δy.
  3. Suppose Δy can be written as a constant A times Δx…
  4. …plus a remainder that is o(Δx).
  5. o(Δx) means: even divided by Δx, it still tends to zero.
  6. Then f is called differentiable at x₀.
  7. The term A·Δx is the linear principal part of Δy.
  8. It is the differential, written dy at x = x₀.
  9. Fix x₀: then dy depends only on the step Δx.
  10. Plot the true change Δy against the step Δx.
  11. Every line through the origin is a linear rule; which one hugs Δy?
  12. A wrong slope, like 1.5, leaves an error about half of Δx.
  13. Slope 2 leaves an error of exactly (Δx)², which is o(Δx).
  14. So the linear stand-in, when it exists, is unique.
  15. Now pinch-zoom into the graph at x₀, like a map on your phone.
  16. Ten times closer, the curve already looks nearly straight.
  17. A hundred times closer, curve and tangent can no longer be told apart.
  18. What survives the zoom is the tangent's rise over Δx: that rise is dy.
  19. The gap Δy − dy is (Δx)², shrinking much faster than Δx.
  20. Theorem 5.9: f is differentiable at x₀ if and only if f′(x₀) exists.
  21. And then the constant A is exactly f′(x₀).

Method / proof core

  1. Why is this theorem almost free in one variable?
  2. If f is differentiable, divide Δy = A·Δx + o(Δx) by Δx.
  3. As Δx → 0, the quotient tends to A, so f′(x₀) = A.
  4. Conversely, if f′(x₀) exists, look at the leftover Δy − f′(x₀)Δx.
  5. Divided by Δx, it is quotient minus limit, which tends to 0.
  6. A linear function of one variable is just multiplication by a constant.
  7. One number decides it, and that number must be the derivative.
  8. In two variables, having slopes no longer guarantees a linear stand-in.
  9. So there, differentiable is defined by the linear stand-in itself.
  10. Now take the simplest function, y = x.
  11. Its derivative is 1, so its differential is dx = 1·Δx = Δx.
  12. For the independent variable, dx is the step Δx itself.
  13. So dy = f′(x)·Δx can be written dy = f′(x) dx.
  14. At each point, dy and dx are two ordinary numbers.
  15. Divide them: dy/dx = f′(x) is a genuine quotient.
  16. That is why the derivative is also called the differential quotient.
  17. Line one in your notes is upgraded: the slash is now a real division.
  18. Every derivative rule becomes a differential rule: just multiply by dx.
  19. The product rule times dx gives d(uv) = v du + u dv.
  20. For y = f(g(x)), the chain rule gives dy = f′(u) g′(x) dx.
  21. But g′(x) dx is exactly du, the differential of u.
  22. So dy = f′(u) du, the same shape as if u were independent.
  23. This is the invariance of the first-order differential form.
  24. Like a group project: whoever holds the pen, the result reads the same.
  25. Demo one: the circle x² + y² = 1.
  26. Take the differential of both sides, without picking an independent variable.
  27. Invariance gives d(y²) = 2y dy, so 2x dx + 2y dy = 0.
  28. Read it one way: dy/dx = −x/y, wherever y ≠ 0.
  29. Read it the other way: dx/dy = −y/x, wherever x ≠ 0.
  30. At (1, 0) the tangent is vertical: dy/dx fails, dx/dy = 0 works.
  31. One equation, two readings, and nobody chose who was independent.
  32. Demo two pays last episode's debt: parametric curves.
  33. Take last episode's cycloid, traced by a point on a rolling wheel.
  34. In terms of t, dx = (1 − cos t) dt.
  35. And dy = sin t dt.
  36. Both are numbers, so divide: the dt cancels honestly.
  37. At t = π/2 the slope is 1, just as last episode found.
  38. Last episode's ratio dy/dt over dx/dt is a true quotient of differentials.
  39. Demo three: y = exp(sin(ax + b)), three layers deep.
  40. Outer layer: dy = exp(sin(ax+b)) · d(sin(ax+b)).
  41. Middle layer: d(sin(ax+b)) = cos(ax+b) · d(ax+b).
  42. Inner layer: d(ax + b) = a dx, and we are done.
  43. Whatever stands in front of dx is the derivative.
  44. Each step was dy = f′(u) du with a new u; nothing was declared.

Applications and outlook

  1. First use: linear approximation, f(x₀ + Δx) ≈ f(x₀) + f′(x₀)Δx.
  2. If 1 yuan buys 1.02 units abroad, what does one unit cost?
  3. Use f(x) = 1/x at x₀ = 1, where f′(1) = −1.
  4. Linear estimate: 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  5. The true value is 0.98039…, slightly larger.
  6. 1/x stays above its tangent, so the linear estimate runs low.
  7. Second use: how a measuring error spreads.
  8. Cut by hand: side 10 cm, off by up to 0.1 cm.
  9. Area error ≈ 2x₀Δx = 2 · 10 · 0.1 = 2 cm².
  10. That is the opening strip again; the corner adds only 0.01 cm².
  11. A 1% error in the side becomes about 2% in the area.
  12. One warning: invariance belongs to the first order only.
  13. The second-order differential loses it; the next episode shows why.
  14. In chapter 8, substitution in integrals runs this invariance backwards.
  15. In two variables, the differential becomes dz = A dx + B dy.
  16. A linear map of the plane, whose o(ρ) remainder will define differentiable.
  17. Later: a matrix between spaces, then a linear operator in functional analysis.
  18. The idea never changes: up close, change is almost linear.
