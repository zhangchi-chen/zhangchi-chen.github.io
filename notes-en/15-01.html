15-01 Fourier coefficients: projecting onto sines and cosines

Fourier coefficients: projecting onto sines and cosines

1 · Motivation

Today we find out how much of each wave hides inside a function. Last chapter, power series handed over their coefficients without a fight. Differentiate at zero, divide by a factorial, and out it comes. Now a new kind of series walks in, built from waves.

Imagine a wave that is secretly built this way. You are handed the wave. How much of the first cosine does it hold? Try the old trick first: look at the single point zero. One equation, with infinitely many unknowns tangled inside it. Differentiating does not help either; every term hangs on. We need a move that keeps one term and silences all the others.

Late at night in the dorm, two flashlights point at right angles. A pencil between them throws one shadow down and one sideways. Switch one light off, and the other shadow does not move. And that only works because the two directions are perpendicular.

In space, the first component of a vector is just one dot product. The other two directions are perpendicular, so they contribute nothing. So here is the question: can functions be perpendicular too?

A dot product multiplies matching entries, then adds them up. Think of a function as a vector with one entry at every point. Multiply point by point, and adding up becomes integrating. And orthogonal means exactly this: the inner product is zero. The definition: the product integrates to zero on the interval. Multiply two of the waves, and the areas above and below cancel out.

Meet the family: the constant, then cosines and sines. Together they form an orthogonal system of functions. The course text's table: mixed products give zero, squares do not. Two different members give zero; a member with itself never does. One entry stands out: the constant gets two pi, the rest get pi.

The theorem: if the series converges uniformly, its coefficients have no freedom left. First clause: the function already is such a series. Second clause: the convergence is uniform, and that will matter. The payoff: every coefficient is f weighed against its own wave. In the flashlight picture, each coefficient is a shadow. The shadow's height is the inner product, scaled by the size of the axis.

Now turn it around and start from any periodic integrable function. These numbers are the Fourier coefficients; the series is the Fourier series. As long as f is integrable, every coefficient can be computed. But look at the sign: a tilde, not an equals sign. The tilde only records the coefficients; it promises nothing about the sum. Turning tilde into equals is a debt for the convergence theorem.

3 · Method

Let's verify one entry of the table with our own hands. The product to sum identity splits it into two plain cosines. Because the two numbers differ, neither new wave is flat. Over a full lap such a cosine has equal hills and valleys, so it integrates to zero. Both pieces vanish, so the whole integral is zero. Verified. Now a wave times itself: one half plus a faster wave. Only the half survives, and over a full lap it gives pi. And that pi is precisely the one we divide by in the formula. Every other entry in the table plays out the same way. A cosine times a sine is an odd function, so symmetry alone gives zero.

Now the extraction, in three frames. First, multiply both sides by one chosen cosine. Second, integrate every term over one full period. Is that allowed? Yes, because the convergence is uniform. Multiplying by a bounded wave keeps the convergence uniform. The term by term theorem from chapter thirteen is doing real work here. Third, orthogonality wipes out every column except one. One survivor is left: the k th coefficient times pi. Divide by pi, and the coefficient formula drops out. Use a sine instead, and the sine coefficient is the one left standing. Back to our mystery wave: its shadow on the first cosine is exactly one. Every other shadow comes out just as cleanly, and the recipe is recovered.

Why the odd half on the constant? Multiply by one and integrate. The constant has the odd entry two pi, and it eats the half. So the constant obeys the very same formula. It's just tidy bookkeeping. Careful here: computing coefficients needs only integrals, not convergence. The theorem assumed convergence; the definition assumes nothing at all. So hold back the equals sign until the convergence theorem arrives.

Warm up time: the function x on one period, copied along the line. This function is odd, so multiplying by any cosine stays odd. Symmetry from chapter nine kills every cosine coefficient. For the sine coefficients the integrand is even, so take twice the right half. Integration by parts, from chapter eight, does the heavy lifting. The result: alternating signs, shrinking like one over n. Here are its shadows, one for each sine, flipping sign as they fade. Pin it on the wall: the Fourier series of x, written with a tilde. Still a tilde: we computed coefficients, we did not add anything up. Dirichlet's test, from chapter twelve, already showed that this series converges. But does it add up to x itself? That is the convergence theorem's job. Keep this sawtooth handy; it returns later in the chapter.

4 · Outlook

Look back: the method never needed anything special about sines. All it needed was a perpendicular family and a projection. Swap in another perpendicular family, and the very same formula runs. The course text's last exercise hands you one such family to practise on.

First use: fitting a line by least squares is a projection in disguise. Second use: an equalizer reads one shadow per band, then turns each up or down. Swap the sines for wavelets or perpendicular polynomials, and medical scanners run on the same projection. Orthogonal directions plus projection: perhaps the most reused idea in applied mathematics. Tools we borrowed today: integration by parts, symmetry, and term by term integration. And one debt stays open: turning the tilde into an equals sign. Each wave casts its own shadow, and no two shadows ever interfere.
