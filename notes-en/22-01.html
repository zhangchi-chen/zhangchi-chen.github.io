22-01 The first-type surface integral: putting a surface on the scale

The first-type surface integral: putting a surface on the scale

1 · Motivation

The first type surface integral, or how to put a curved surface on the scale. It is your roommate's birthday, and the gift is a basketball to wrap. First instinct: measure the shadow it casts on the desk. Seen from above, the shadow is just a flat disk. But the skin of the ball is four times the size of its shadow. Counting the shadows of the top and the bottom still covers only half. The steeper a piece of the ball, the less shadow it casts.

Now it is glitter paper, heavier wherever the glitter piles up. How heavy is the wrap, when the density changes from point to point? In chapter twenty we weighed a wire with density. A curved shell with density calls for its twin. The real question: how does a curved piece relate to the flat ground below?

The definition replays chapter twenty in four moves: cut, pick, add, and shrink. Here is the definition, word for word from the course text. First, cut the surface into small pieces and note each one's area. In every piece pick a point and read off the value of f there. Weight each reading by the area of its piece, and add everything up. The mesh is the diameter of the widest piece, the worst case of the cut. Now let the mesh shrink to zero, and every piece shrinks with it. If the sums close in on one number, no matter how we cut or pick, then that number is the first type surface integral of f over the surface.

Line by line, it is the wire definition lifted by one dimension. Set the function to one everywhere, and the sum is simply the total area. Just as integrating one along a wire measured how far it runs. Put a density in for the function, and the integral weighs the shell. The definition can weigh any shell, but it gives no way to compute.

Theorem twenty two point one moves the whole job down to the shadow. On the left, the integral we want, over the curved surface. On the right, an ordinary double integral over the flat shadow. We plug in the surface's height, so the function is still read on the surface. And one new factor, the root, which stretches the shadow back to the surface. The fine print: a smooth surface with continuous slopes, and a continuous function on it.

3 · Method

Why a root at all? Zoom in on one tiny piece of the surface. Up close it is nearly flat, a little patch of the tangent plane. Straight below sits its shadow, a small rectangle on the floor. The patch is tilted, and gamma measures how far its normal leans from vertical. Picture a tilted skylight over your desk: its glass is bigger than the floor it covers. The shadow is the patch shrunk by a cosine, so divide it back. The normal vector of the tangent plane hands us that cosine directly. So the root is exactly how much the surface stretches its shadow. The steeper the slope, the bigger the root, right where the shadow was hiding paper.

Its little sibling lives in chapter ten: the arc element of a graph. Same story one dimension up: a slope stretches whatever sits above the shadow. Chapter ten warned us: strips that skip the root get the area wrong.

Example one: the sphere's cap above a plane, weighted by one over height. The cap is one graph: the sphere's upper height. Its shadow is the disk under the cap's rim. First key move: on a sphere, the root simplifies to radius over height. Second key move: the two roots multiply out, and the radical disappears. What is left depends only on the distance from the center, made for polar coordinates. In polar form, the angle sweeps once around and the radius runs to the rim. The radius integral is a logarithm, and the answer comes out clean. The moral: when the integrand is chosen well, the roots simply melt away.

An old habit from chapter ten: test a new formula where you know the answer. A flat plate has no slope, so the root is one and nothing stretches. For the whole sphere, the two halves add up to the known area. Check passed.

That check quietly split the sphere, because one graph cannot cover it. A vertical line pierces the sphere twice, so one height per point is not enough. Split it into an upper graph and a lower graph, one sign each. Both halves cast the same disk below, and each brings its own root. And if the integrand is symmetric as well, do one half and double it. Example two: integrate the squared distance from the center over the whole sphere. But on the sphere that squared distance is the same everywhere. A constant integrand is just value times area, no root ever written. Reading tip: simplify the integrand on the surface before touching the root.

Bug number one: dropping the root on a slope. A window at forty five degrees over a unit square: the wrong way says one. Its real area is about one point four one; the missing root made the difference. Bug number two: projecting a cylinder's wall straight down. Its shadow is only a circle, a curve with no area to integrate over. The tangent plane stands upright, the cosine hits zero, and the formula breaks. The fix: cut it into front and back halves and project sideways, or use parameters. Add the two halves, and out comes the familiar side area of a can.

4 · Outlook

Mass, centroid and inertia: chapter twenty one's recipes, now on surface pieces. Back to the birthday gift: buy paper by the area, not by the shadow. Most projected integrals end in polar coordinates, straight from chapter twenty one. This whole episode was the surface twin of weighing a wire.

Ahead, surfaces get a side, and flux through them turns into volume integrals. Looking ahead: surfaces that are not graphs get a two parameter formula, named here only. Graphics engines and map apps measure the area of terrain from height maps exactly this way. The paper you need is never the footprint you see.
