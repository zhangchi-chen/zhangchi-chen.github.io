Episode 1.3 · after: infinite decimals, density

Completeness of ℝa first look

This episode is informal on purpose. It plants pictures and vocabulary; a later chapter proves that all the faces below are equivalent.

1 · The hole, made concrete

Inside ℚ, take the set below. It is bounded above (by 2, even by 1.5). Hunt for its least upper bound from both sides.

S = {x ∈ ℚ : x² < 2}

From below (in S)

11² = 1 < 2
1.41.4² = 1.96 < 2
1.411.41² = 1.9881 < 2
1.4141.414² = 1.999396 < 2

From above (upper bounds)

22² = 4 > 2
1.51.5² = 2.25 > 2
1.421.42² = 2.0164 > 2
1.4151.415² = 2.002225 > 2

If a rational q is too small (q² < 2), some member of S sits above it — so q is not an upper bound.

If a rational q bounds S (q² > 2), a smaller rational still bounds S — so no upper bound in ℚ is the least.

The two packs squeeze toward √2, and √2 ∉ ℚ (asserted here, not proved). In ℚ the hunters converge to a ghost: a bounded set with no rational supremum. That is a hole.

2 · Three slogans for completeness

i · Closed under limits

Closed under “take a limit” and “approximate”: a convergent hunt must end at a real number.

ii · No holes

The line is a continuum.

iii · Smallest such set ⊇ ℚ

ℝ is the smallest set containing ℚ with these properties: add exactly what approximation demands, nothing more.

3 · The seed, in decimal language

For a = 1.41421356…, the lower truncation aₙ (n digits after the point) and the upper truncation aₙ + 10⁻ⁿ trap a at every precision. The intervals nest and their lengths 10⁻ⁿ → 0.

an ≤ a ≤ an + 10−n
The seed (accepted on faith in this course)

Every infinite decimal is a real number, and every real number is an infinite decimal.

This is already a completeness statement in disguise; the course grows everything else from it.

5 · Why anyone cares

The promise from episode 1 (intermediate value theorem): a continuous f on [a, b] with f(a) < 0 < f(b) must cross zero. Bisection halves the interval forever; completeness guarantees the hunt converges to an actual number. Example: f(x) = x² − 2 on [1, 2].

Self-check

Pick an answer in your head, then reveal.

Which bounded set of rationals has no supremum in ℚ?
  1. A{x ∈ ℚ : x ≤ 2}
  2. B{x ∈ ℚ : x² < 2}
  3. C{x ∈ ℚ : 0 < x < 1}
Answer: B. Its least upper bound would be √2, which is not rational; the other two have suprema 2 and 1 in ℚ.
Which promise belongs to completeness rather than density?
  1. AEvery window (a, b) with a < b contains a rational.
  2. BNested closed intervals with lengths → 0 close in on a real number.
  3. CBetween two rationals there is always another rational.
Answer: B. Options A and C are density promises (ℚ keeps them yet has holes); only B says every legitimate hunt ends.
If a rational q > 0 has q² > 2, what follows for S = {x ∈ ℚ : x² < 2}?
  1. Aq is the least upper bound of S.
  2. BA smaller rational is still an upper bound of S.
  3. Cq belongs to S.
Answer: B. Upper hunters can always shrink a little and still bound S, so no upper bound in ℚ is least.
What is the “seed” this course accepts?
  1. AEvery infinite decimal is a real number, and every real number is an infinite decimal.
  2. BEvery real number is rational.
  3. CEvery Cauchy sequence of rationals has a rational limit.
Answer: A. The seed is the infinite-decimal description of ℝ; it is already a completeness statement in disguise.
Which is NOT one of the five faces of completeness named here?
  1. AMonotone bounded sequences converge.
  2. BEvery bounded sequence is monotone.
  3. CCauchy sequences converge.
Answer: B. That statement is simply false (think of (−1)ⁿ); the five faces are Dedekind cuts, nested intervals, monotone bounded, Cauchy, and the supremum property.
Bisecting f(x) = x² − 2 on [1, 2], what does completeness guarantee?
  1. AThe algorithm stops after finitely many steps.
  2. BThe nested intervals close in on an actual real number, √2.
  3. CThe limit of the midpoints is rational.
Answer: B. The hunt never stops, but it ends at a point of ℝ: the crossing point √2 exists, and bisection computes its decimal digit by digit.
