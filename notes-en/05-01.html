05-01 The derivative: speed at this instant, slope at this point

The derivative: speed at this instant, slope at this point

1 · Motivation

One idea, two questions: how fast right now, and how steep right here. Dinner is on the way, and the app shows how fast the rider goes right now. The average over the whole trip is easy: just distance over time. Yet right now the app shows more. Averaged over which stretch of time? In one single instant, the rider covers no distance and uses no time. Zero over zero tells us nothing at all. So shrink the time window: a minute, then ten seconds, then one second. The averages settle on one number, and that is the speed right now. So speed right now quietly hides a limit of averages.

Now a second puzzle. What exactly is a tangent line? The school answer: a line that meets the curve exactly once. Yet a vertical line also meets the parabola just once, and it is clearly no tangent. And the cubic passes right through its own tangent at the origin. Counting meeting points fails in both directions. We need a better idea. The idea: pick a nearby point and draw the line through both. Let the second point slide in, and the line swings toward one position. That final position is the tangent. Once again, a limit.

Here is how the course text pins down speed right now. Plot distance against time, and average speed becomes the slope of a secant. Let the second moment close in from either side, and the averages close in too. If that limit exists, it is the instantaneous velocity.

The tangent is defined the same way: as a limiting position. One point stays put, while the other runs along the curve toward it. The secant swings, and where it ends up is the tangent. So the tangent slope is the limit of the secant slopes.

Now set the two formulas side by side. Rename the letters, and the two formulas become one and the same. Two doors open into one room: change out over change in, then a limit.

Peel off both stories, and what remains is the definition. First, the function must live on a whole stretch around the point. So the moving point can come from either side, as close as we like. Second, the limit of the ratio has to exist. Then the function is differentiable there, and the limit gets its name. In increment form, the input takes a step and the output follows. The derivative is the limit of that ratio as the step shrinks. The ratio itself is the average rate of change, the difference quotient. The derivative is the rate of change at that very point. Keep one sentence: change out over change in, then take the limit.

3 · Method

Let us compute one by hand: the squaring function at the point one. Take a small step and land on a second point of the curve. Write down the ratio: how much the output rose, over the step. Expand, cancel the step, which is never zero, and a simple answer drops out. Watch the secant as the point slides in: its slope closes in on two. So the derivative at one is two, computed, not guessed. A line through that point with slope two: that is the tangent. A quick check: the parabola stays above the line, touching it once.

Now treat the ratio itself as a function, and draw it. It is a straight line with a single hole, right at the point. At the point itself the ratio makes no sense, and it never has to. The limit only looks nearby, and the hole sits exactly at height two. It is exactly the function limit of chapter three, so every old instinct applies.

Here is a tempting shortcut: step out the same distance on both sides. This symmetric ratio divides by the whole width between the two points. When the derivative exists, this shortcut does land on it. But the trap runs the other way: the shortcut can exist without the derivative.

Take example two from the course text: the absolute value at zero. On the right the ratio is always one; on the left, always minus one. Two different values, no single limit: no derivative at zero. Now the shortcut: the two heights are always equal, so the top is zero. So its limit is zero. Everything looks calm. Yet example two says there is no derivative. The shortcut fooled us. The symmetric secant hops right over the corner without a glance. Like a classmate who averages the first and third quiz, and never notices the second was not handed in. A real derivative pins one end at the point and checks each side on its own. The lesson: a calm symmetric ratio does not make a function differentiable.

Differentiability is decided one point at a time. Now a strange one: the square, times the Dirichlet function. At rational points it follows the parabola; at irrational ones it sits at zero. At zero, the ratio is squeezed inside a narrowing wedge. The wedge closes up: the derivative at zero exists, and it is zero. At any other point, the function keeps jumping between two levels. The top refuses to shrink while the bottom does, so the ratio blows up. So the derivative exists at exactly one point, nowhere else. So the derivative, as a function, lives only where the limit exists.

The easiest case of all: a function that never changes. The top is always zero, so the derivative is zero everywhere. File it as the first line of a table that will grow later. Three ways to write the same derivative. For now, read the last one as a single symbol; a later episode makes it a real fraction.

4 · Outlook

The same ratio keeps showing up in different clothes. A thin rod: its density at a point is the same ratio, then a limit. Printing class notes: the slope of the cost curve is the marginal cost. At five hundred copies, one more copy costs about two yuan. Electric current, reaction speed: every rate is this one sentence. In later courses, the objects become vectors and matrices. But the sentence stays the same: change out over change in, then a limit.

Two questions stay open, and the next episode owes us answers. If a function is differentiable at a point, must it be continuous there? And at a corner, what happens if we look from one side only? Speed right now, slope right here: one ratio, one limit.
