14-02 Cauchy–Hadamard: the limsup's moment of glory

Cauchy–Hadamard: the limsup's moment of glory

1 · Motivation

The Cauchy Hadamard formula: the upper limit's moment of glory. Last time we learned that every power series has a radius. Today's question: how do we read the radius off the coefficients? Usually one ordinary limit of the roots hands us the radius. Now meet the third example in the course text. Odd powers get powers of a third; even powers get powers of a half. Take the nth roots, and they hop between a third and a half. They never settle down, so there is no limit at all. So the ordinary limit formula has nothing to work with. Yet this series clearly has a radius somewhere. It converges at one, but at three the even terms blow up. So it lies between one and three. But where?

Think about your bus delays across a whole semester. One awful day the bus was forty minutes late, the worst day ever. What you really plan for is the worst delay that keeps coming back. Read the coefficients the same way: skip the one-offs, watch what recurs.

3 · Method

Why the upper limit, and not simply the supremum? Change one early coefficient to a hundred, and the supremum jumps to a hundred. But changing one term never changes convergence; the radius stays one. The upper limit ignores one-offs, just like your commute plan. And the lower limit? In our example it would claim a radius of three. At two and a half, the even terms keep growing, so the lower limit lied. The worst subsequence decides, and that is exactly the upper limit.

Now the proof, in three frames. Frame one: run chapter twelve's root test, upper limit form, on the absolute series. The key move: the size of x simply slides outside the root. Frame two: below one, the series converges absolutely, inside the radius. Above one, infinitely many terms stay bigger than one, so the series diverges. Frame three: exactly at one, the test says nothing. That is last episode's rule: check the endpoints by hand. Draw the V shaped graph and compare it with height one. When the roots die out, the graph lies flat, and every point converges. When the roots explode, the graph shoots up, and only zero survives.

And what about the ratio of neighbouring coefficients? It works only when that limit exists; then the roots share it. In our example, the ratios lurch from big to small and back. One branch sinks to zero, the other soars to infinity. No limit. When the limit exists, enjoy it: here the ratios settle at three. The ratio is a handy shortcut; the upper limit formula always works.

Another trap: series with gaps in their exponents. Here every odd coefficient is zero. The ratio would divide by zero, so that shortcut collapses. First repair: substitute a new variable for the square. The square below one means the radius in x is also one. Second repair: the roots hop between one and zero, so the upper limit is one. The course text's fourth example belongs to the same family. Substitute the square: its radius is nine, so in x the radius is three. At plus or minus three the terms creep toward minus one, never zero.

Now let's pack it all into one route map. Like crossing campus: first ask if the shortcut path even exists. Do the ratios settle? Then take the fast lane. Gaps in the exponents? Substitute, or take the upper limit. Do the roots oscillate? Take their largest subsequence limit. Whatever the branch, finish by checking both endpoints by hand.

4 · Outlook

First use: coefficients that mix two growth rates. The roots get squeezed onto three, so the radius is one third. Second use: only perfect squares appear as exponents. The gaps keep widening, so no substitution can close them. The upper limit still works: the roots hit one forever, so the radius is one. Third use: a simple bound on the coefficients already guarantees a radius. A guaranteed radius, without ever pinning rho down exactly.

Paid today: chapter seven's upper limit and chapter twelve's root test both starred. Inside the radius, the next episode will differentiate and integrate term by term. Over the complex numbers, the same formula measures a disk. Complex function theory begins right here; a later episode takes a first look. Later analysis courses describe how fast sequences grow with an upper limit. So is how series solutions behave at their boundary. Even computer science measures algorithm running times with an upper limit. To recap: take the upper limit of the roots, then flip it. It always exists, and the endpoints still get checked by hand.
