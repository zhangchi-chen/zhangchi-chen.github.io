09-06 The integral mean value theorem: the average speed over a stretch of road

The integral mean value theorem: the average speed over a stretch of road

1 · Motivation

An order just arrived: six kilometres in twenty minutes. The app says the average speed was eighteen kilometres an hour. But the speedometer never sat still for a moment. Was there one moment when the needle sat exactly on eighteen? A lazy shortcut: average the first and last readings, and get six. But at six an hour, twenty minutes gets you only two kilometres.

Five quiz scores are easy: add them up and divide by five. But the speed has a reading at every single instant. Infinitely many readings. You cannot add them all and divide. Same word, average, but now it needs a second meaning. Today we define that average, then catch the instant that hits it.

2 · Statements

Cut [a, b] into n equal pieces and read f once in each.
Average those n readings the old way.
Since 1/n = Δx/(b − a), it is a Riemann sum over b − a.
Refine n = 3, 6, 12, 24: the sample mean settles down.
The limit is the average value of f on [a, b].
In pictures: flatten the area into an equal-area rectangle.
For f(x) = 1 + x²/4 on [0, 3], that height is 1.75.
Back to the rider: distance is the integral of speed.
Average speed is distance over time.
Flattened, the area is a rectangle of height exactly 18.
And the curve crosses the 18 line at t = 5 and t = 15.
Some instant really did hit the average exactly.
Lagrange's theorem from 06-02 already said: s(b) − s(a) = s′(ξ)(b − a).
Write the distance as an integral: same sentence, new clothes.
That is today's theorem; let us read it clause by clause.
If f is continuous on [a, b],
then there exists at least one point ξ ∈ [a, b]
such that ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = f(ξ)(b − a).
Clause one: f is one unbroken curve, no jumps.
Clause two: at least one such point, maybe more.
Clause three: the area equals one rectangle of height f(ξ).
Divide by b − a: f(ξ) is exactly the average value.
In short: a continuous function attains its average value.
If f and g are both continuous on [a, b] and g does not change sign on [a, b],
then there exists at least one ξ ∈ [a, b]
with ∫ₐᵇ f(x)g(x) dx = f(ξ)∫ₐᵇ g(x) dx.
(For g ≡ 1 this is Theorem 9.7.)
The mean value point ξ can in fact always be chosen in the open interval (a, b).
Endpoints may work too, but an interior point always exists.

Cut the interval into equal pieces, and take one reading in each. Average those readings the old way. Look closer: that average is a Riemann sum, divided by the length. Cut finer and finer, and the average stops wandering. Its limit is what we call the average value of the function. In pictures: flatten the area into a rectangle of the same size. For this function, the flattened height comes out at 1.75.

Back to our rider: the distance travelled is the area under the speed. And average speed is just distance over time. Flatten that area, and the height is exactly eighteen. The app was right. And the speed crosses eighteen twice: at minute five and minute fifteen. So yes: at some instant the speed hit the average exactly. We have met this before: Lagrange's mean value theorem, back in chapter six. Now write the distance as an integral. Same sentence, new clothes. That is today's theorem. Let us read it one clause at a time.

First: the function is continuous. Then some point in the interval exists, at least one. where the area equals one rectangle at that height.

Clause one: the graph is drawn in one stroke, no jumps. Clause two: at least one such moment, maybe several. Clause three: the whole area becomes one rectangle at that height. Divide by the length, and that height is exactly the average value. In one line: a continuous function always reaches its own average.

The weighted version: both continuous, and the weight never changes sign. Then at least one point exists, and the weighted area is that value times total weight. Set the weight to one: the first theorem returns. And a note: the mean value point can always be taken strictly inside. An endpoint may work too, but there is always one inside.

3 · Method

Why must such a point exist? Three steps, three borrowed tools. Step one, the extreme value theorem: a lowest and a highest value exist. So the graph lives between those two levels. Step two, monotonicity of the integral: integrating never flips an inequality. The area is squeezed between a short rectangle and a tall one. Divide by the length: the average is trapped between the two levels. Step three, the intermediate value theorem: the function climbs from low to high without jumping. So somewhere on the way it must pass the average height. That is our point. Here the point is root three, and the height checks out: 1.75. Three steps, three credits: chapter four twice, this chapter once.

Drop continuity, and step three falls apart. This step function averages one half, yet never equals one half. The bounds still hold. Only the promise of hitting the average dies.

Let's compute one: the average of sine over one arch. The area under one arch is exactly two. Divide by the width pi, and the average is about 0.64. The two end heights average to zero. The true average sits well above. Where is it? Where sine meets that height. Two answers: about 0.69, and its mirror, about 2.45. Both sit strictly inside the interval, just as the note promised. Why inside? Bring back Lagrange, and apply it to the variable limit integral. The chord's steepness is the average, and a matching tangent appears strictly inside.

Now add weights. Think of a course grade where homework and the exam count differently. Shift the weights and the grade moves, yet it never leaves seventy to ninety. The continuous version: average a function with a weight. Weights leaning right pull the average up, from 1.75 to 2.125. A weight of one sign keeps it in range; the intermediate value theorem finds the point.

But let the weight change sign, and the average can escape the range. A straight line, and a weight that dips below zero. The weighted average lands above the maximum. No point can match it. That sign condition is a load bearing wall, not decoration.

Exam use one: quick bounds from the lowest and highest values. Try it on this integral: the integrand slides down from one to one half. Width one, so the integral sits between one half and one. The true value, about 0.785, lands right inside. Read it as an average height, hit at an interior point, and both bounds become strict. Exam use two: signed areas can cancel, absolute areas cannot. Exam use three: knowing only one factor's range, pull it out as one value. For instance, this family of integrals is squeezed down to zero as the power grows.

4 · Outlook

Flashback to chapter nine, episode three: a proof there quietly used this theorem. The thin strip's area is one rectangle at some height inside it: our theorem. Shrink the strip, and continuity makes the derivative equal the function. Looking ahead: the second mean value theorem for integrals, with a monotone factor. The course text states it, and in chapter eleven it drives Dirichlet's test.

One breath ahead: make the weight a probability density, and the average becomes an expectation. And without continuity, only the two bounds survive. Keep one sentence: a continuous function always reaches its average, at some instant.
