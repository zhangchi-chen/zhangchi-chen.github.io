15-03 Odd and even extensions: three ways to open a half interval

Odd and even extensions: three ways to open a half interval

1 · Motivation

One function, half an interval, and three different series to choose from. The homework looks innocent: expand the function x on the interval from 0 to 2. You hand in a cosine series, but the answer key shows only sines. The formulas look nothing alike, yet both partial sums close in on the same line. So who made the mistake, or can one function really have two expansions? Stranger still, at the right end they split: one series reaches 2, the other sits at 0.

Here is the secret: each answer quietly extended the function to a full period first. It is like a timetable with only half the week planned: the other half is up to you. Copy it as a mirror image and you get cosines; copy it upside down and you get sines. Three tools will settle it today: a change of scale, symmetry, and the convergence theorem.

And one remark in the course text answers the homework puzzle outright. One function, one interval, and three honest ways to write it. Everything hangs on the words after a suitable extension. Convergence is settled too: the theorem from the last episode covers all three.

3 · Method

Where do these formulas come from? We simply rename the axis. Stretch the axis by the right factor, and the period two l becomes two pi. The new function g repeats every two pi. Use the theory we already built on g, then translate back. The change of variable trades one over pi for one over l. Every cosine and sine is renamed too, and the course text's series appears. There is only one Fourier theory; the letter l is just a costume. For period four, l equals two, and each term is rescaled.

Next comes symmetry, an old trick from chapter nine, episode five. On a symmetric interval, the left half of an odd function exactly cancels the right half. An even function's halves match, so the total doubles. Now take an odd f: multiplied by a cosine, it stays odd. Turn the left half around onto the right, and every cosine coefficient cancels to zero. Times a sine it turns even, so only the right half counts, twice. For an even f, the roles swap: the sines cancel and the cosines double. Half the coefficients vanish, and the survivors need only the given half.

Back to the homework: our function lives only on the right half, with l equal to two. The theorems want a whole period, so the left half is ours to build. First choice, the odd extension: spin the graph half a turn around the origin. Repeat it every four units, and its series contains only sines. Second choice, the even extension: mirror it in the vertical axis. Repeat that, and you get a zigzag whose series has only cosines. Or fill the left half any way you like, for example with zeros. Then cosines and sines both show up, the course text's more general series.

Yet to compute, you never have to draw the extension at all. The course text's formulas integrate over the given half only. Think of scaffolding: the crane is gone from the photo, but the building keeps the shape it gave. And that shape still decides one thing: what the series does at the two ends.

So which one should you pick? Start by asking about the two ends. Every sine wave in the series touches zero at both ends. A guitar string pinned at both ends sits at zero there, so the sine series fits. Every cosine wave lies flat at both ends. A metal rod insulated at both ends has a flat temperature profile there, so cosines fit. Nothing pinned at the ends? Then take the more general series.

Now the homework, both ways, with l equal to two. For the sine series, one integration by parts gives every coefficient. The result is the sine expansion printed in the course text. The partial sums close in on the line, but at the right end they stay stuck at zero. For the cosine series, the constant is easy, and only odd frequencies survive. Again it matches the cosine expansion in the course text. These partial sums settle quickly and land on the line even at both ends. Inside, both series equal x; the convergence theorem gives each a green light.

But careful: at the ends, the two series go separate ways. At the right end, the odd extension drops from two to minus two. The sine series takes the halfway value, zero, just as the last episode predicted. At the left end nothing jumps, because x itself starts from zero. Any sine series sits at zero at both ends, no matter what the function does there. The even extension never breaks, so its series lands on zero and two exactly. So at the right end, three extensions give three answers. The rule: look for jumps in the extension, not at the formula you were given.

4 · Outlook

First use: the absolute value function is even, so only cosines appear. Only the right half needs integrating, so half the work disappears. Second use: these expansions become the toolkit of episode five in this chapter.

Looking further ahead, this choice turns out to be physical. A vibrating string picks sines; an insulated rod cooling down picks cosines. In a differential equations course, the boundary conditions will make this choice for you.

Today our choice was the symmetry of the extension. The next episode asks a quieter question: which period are we allowed to use? The answer, any period at all, is the chapter's best kept secret. Half an interval, three ways to complete it, and the ends tell you which one.
