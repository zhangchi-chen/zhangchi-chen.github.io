08-02 Gluing the differential: the first substitution as pattern-matching

Gluing the differential: the first substitution as pattern-matching

1 · Motivation

Today we play the first substitution as a matching game. Homework night, and three integrals that look like triplets. The first one is free: the table already knows it. For the second, guess boldly and differentiate: it lands exactly on the integrand. Feeling lucky, we try the very same guess on the third. But this time the check fails: an unwanted factor pops out. Divide it away? Then the quotient rule sneaks in a new term. So why did the middle one work? Watch that extra factor. It is the derivative of the inner function, riding right alongside. That is today's game: spot the pair before you write a single line.

2 · Statements

Theorem 8.4, the first substitution, in the course text's words.
f only needs to be defined on an interval I.
φ is differentiable on J and sends every point into I.
Why: f must be defined wherever φ sends t.
Hypothesis: an antiderivative F of f is already known.
Conclusion: the composite F(φ(t)) is the answer on J.
Proof: differentiate the composite with the chain rule.
Out comes exactly the integrand f(φ(t))φ′(t).
Read right to left, it is just the chain rule — nothing new.
The course text's convenient form: one chain of equalities.
The key move: φ′(t) dt is glued into dφ(t).
Read φ(t) as one block, call it x: now it is plain ∫ f(x) dx.
Use F, then put φ(t) back in place of x.
That is why it is also called the method of gluing the differential.
In practice the letters swap: x is ours, u = φ(x) is new.
And the course text's closing caution, word for word.
Two jobs: shape the integrand expression, then return to x.

Here is Theorem eight point four, the first substitution, as the course text states it. First clause: the outer function lives on an interval. The inner function is differentiable, and all its values land inside. Wherever the inner function lands, the outer one must exist. The hypothesis: we already own an antiderivative of the outer function. The conclusion: just plug the inner function into that antiderivative. One line of proof: the chain rule from episode five oh four. And out comes exactly the integrand. Nothing more, nothing less. Read that line backwards and the theorem is just an old rule. Nothing new.

The course text also gives a convenient form, one chain of equalities. Key move: the derivative factor merges into the differential. Treat the inner function as one block and give it a single name. Apply the antiderivative, then put the inner function back. That is why the course text also calls it gluing the differential. In homework the letters swap roles; do not let that throw you. And here is the course text's closing caution, word for word. Two jobs, then: shape the integrand expression, and come home to the original variable.

3 · Method

Here is the game board, like a card game in a study app. On the right, the differential table from episode five oh eight, read backwards. The rule: when a factor matches a card on the right, a chord lights up. First pair: cosine meets the differential of sine. Second pair: the reciprocal meets the log. Third pair: the lone factor feeds the denominator. Fourth pair: tangent's differential is hiding here. And the leftover decides everything: it must be made of the inner function alone.

But wait: why are we allowed to glue at all? The answer comes from episode five oh eight: the invariance of the first order differential form. The same formula holds whether the middle variable is free or depends on another. So the two sides are one and the same differential. Gluing is honest bookkeeping, not magic. Think of it as keeping a receipt for every currency exchange. At the end, a single differentiation balances the books. Episode five oh eight promised this convenience. Today it earns its keep.

Live computation one: the tangent, which the table never lists. Split it into sine over cosine; the sine factor is cosine's differential, up to a minus. Now the shape is just the logarithm entry in disguise. So the answer is the negative log of the absolute cosine. Check: slide along the graph; the slope always equals the tangent. It holds on each interval avoiding the zeros of cosine.

Live two: a constant sneaks into the denominator. The table only knows the version with a one there. How do we get there? Look at the graph: it is the same bump, just wider and lower. The key move: pull out the constant and glue the rescaled variable. The result is an arctangent with a small factor in front. Notice we never introduced a new letter. The inner piece stayed in plain sight. Check: the two small factors rebuild the constant, and the books balance.

Live computation three, round one: an exponential with a squared inner. The glue: that factor is exactly half the square's differential. Round one closes cleanly: half the exponential, plus a constant. Round two looks almost the same: sine times an exponential of cosine. The tempting answer writes itself. Careful, this is the dangerous step. The picture catches it: the integrand is positive, yet this candidate goes downhill. Sign bookkeeping is where slips happen: the differential of cosine carries a minus. Corrected: the wrong answer slides out, and the minus slides in front. Check: one differentiation brings back the integrand. The books balance.

There are two ways to lose this game. First, the leftover refuses to cooperate. Try the square as the new variable on this one. One factor pairs off nicely, but a stray copy is left behind. That stray piece lives outside the new variable, and the move stalls. The second way: you stop in the new variable and never come home. This one looks finished, but it is still written in the helper letter. An answer left in the new variable is money you never exchanged back. The fix: swap the original expression back in, and the answer is home.

4 · Outlook

First everyday use: the inner function is linear. Its derivative is a constant, so the answer just gains one over that constant. That settles the classic slip: cosine of a multiple, with no guessing. Second use: whenever the top is the derivative of the bottom. Then the answer is the log of the absolute denominator; tangent was one case. Here the denominator stays positive, so no absolute value is needed.

Looking back: the chain rule from episode five oh four, run in reverse, is the whole theorem. And the invariance from episode five oh eight, used backwards, is the glue itself. Ahead in episode nine oh five, definite integrals rerun this, with the limits changing too. Know the table both ways, and a large class of integrals turns into a spotting game. What resists spotting? Radicals of this shape. They need the second substitution of episode eight oh three: choose the new variable first, then invert back. Spot the pair, glue the differential, come home to the original variable, and check by differentiating.
