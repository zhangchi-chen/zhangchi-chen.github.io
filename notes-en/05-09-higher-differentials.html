Motivation

  1. Higher-order differentials: from second order on, a change of variable adds a term.
  2. Two classmates, one homework problem, two different answers.
  3. The problem: y = sin x, where x = t².
  4. The task: find d²y, the second-order differential.
  5. Lin treats x as the independent variable.
  6. He differentiates twice, holding the step dx fixed.
  7. Lin's answer: d²y = −sin x dx².
  8. Two lines, done. Lin closes the notebook.
  9. Zhou substitutes first: y = sin(t²).
  10. Twice in t, and the product rule splits the result in two.
  11. Zhou's answer has two terms, times dt².
  12. To compare, rewrite Lin's answer in t, using dx = 2t dt.
  13. Line the two answers up, term by term.
  14. One term matches; the other, 2cos(t²) dt², is missing from Lin's.
  15. Same function, same problem — so who is wrong?
  16. A step went missing, like an unticked item on a checklist.

What the statement says

  1. First: what is d²y when x is the independent variable?
  2. The definition, in the course text's words.
  3. Key clause: x and dx are independent of each other.
  4. So dy = f′(x) dx varies with x alone; dx is a constant.
  5. Differentiate it once more: d(dy) = f″(x) dx · dx.
  6. Result: d²y = f″(x) dx², the second-order differential.
  7. Careful: three look-alikes that must not be confused.
  8. The course text's warning, word for word.
  9. dx² means (dx)²: the square of one small step.
  10. d²x: the step of x never changes, so d²x = 0.
  11. d(x²) = 2x dx: the L-shaped strip from 05-08.
  12. A square, a zero, a strip: three different things.
  13. But d²x = 0 only because x is the independent variable.
  14. Keep going: dⁿy = d(dⁿ⁻¹y) = f⁽ⁿ⁾(x) dxⁿ.
  15. The course text: orders n ≥ 2 are higher-order differentials.
  16. Divide by dxⁿ: the familiar dⁿy/dxⁿ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(x).
  17. This quotient is honest — but only while x is independent.
  18. Lin used exactly this rule.
  19. But in the homework x = t², so x was never independent.

Method / proof core

  1. The mechanism: start with equal small steps in t.
  2. Send each point through x = t².
  3. The x-steps are no longer equal: they keep growing.
  4. Each gap beats the one before by exactly 0.5.
  5. That growth of the step itself is d²x.
  6. Here d²x = φ″(t) dt² = 2 dt², and 2 × 0.5² = 0.5.
  7. Equal x-steps would mean zero growth: d²x = 0.
  8. Lin assumed equal steps; the homework has growing ones.
  9. Now redo the second differential, and let dx vary too.
  10. dy is a product, f′(x) times dx, and both factors move.
  11. Product rule: d(f′(x) dx) = f″(x) dx·dx + f′(x) d(dx).
  12. The first term, f″(x) dx², is Lin's whole answer.
  13. The second, f′(x) d²x, is the item Lin never ticked.
  14. If x is independent, d²x = 0 and that term drops out.
  15. Back to the homework: f′(x) = cos x and d²x = 2 dt².
  16. Extra term: cos(t²) · 2 dt², that is 2cos(t²) dt².
  17. Add it to Lin's line, and the two answers agree.
  18. Nobody's arithmetic was wrong; one formula was used out of range.
  19. Zhou's answer is the second differential with respect to t.
  20. The general statement, in the course text's words.
  21. First order: the form dy = f′(x) dx survives the change.
  22. Second order: the same form breaks — it is one term short.
  23. This settles 05-08's promise: invariance is first-order only.
  24. Use 1: the second derivative of a parametric curve.
  25. Recall 05-07: dy/dx = y′ₜ / x′ₜ, a ratio of two rates.
  26. Tempting next step: d²y/dx² = y″ₜ / (x′ₜ)².
  27. That reads d²y/dx² as a fraction and forgets d²x.
  28. Correct reading: d²y/dx² means d(dy/dx) divided by dx.
  29. Quotient rule in t, then divide by x′ₜ once more.
  30. Result: (y″ₜx′ₜ − y′ₜx″ₜ) / (x′ₜ)³.
  31. The piece −y′ₜx″ₜ upstairs is exactly the d²x term.
  32. Check on the unit circle at t = π/4.
  33. The correct formula gives −2√2; the guess gives only −√2.
  34. Direct check on y = √(1 − x²): y″ = −2√2. The formula wins.
  35. That pays 05-07's debt: the extra structure is d²x at work.

Applications and outlook

  1. Use 2: a second-order approximation.
  2. Add half of d²y to the linear approximation.
  3. Try √1.1, with x₀ = 1 and Δx = 0.1.
  4. First order: 1 + 0.05 = 1.05.
  5. Second order subtracts 0.00125, giving 1.04875.
  6. True value 1.04881…: the error shrinks about twentyfold.
  7. Taylor's formula (06-07) will make this exact, with a remainder.
  8. Use 3: the sign of d²y.
  9. For dx ≠ 0, dx² > 0, so d²y has the sign of f″(x).
  10. Positive d²y: near the point, the curve sits above its tangent.
  11. Where dy = 0: positive means a minimum, negative a maximum.
  12. Bonus: there f′(x) = 0, so the extra term vanishes anyway.
  13. There, Lin's short form holds whatever the independent variable.
  14. If d²y = 0 there, this test stays silent.
  15. Chapter 6 proves these tests: 06-09 extrema, 06-10 convexity.
  16. Reading dⁿy/dxⁿ as a quotient is safe only when x is independent.
  17. Otherwise write d(dy/dx)/dx and go one step at a time.
  18. Lin's checklist now has every box ticked.
  19. Outlook: with two variables, d²z has three terms.
  20. It is a quadratic form in (dx, dy).
  21. Positive or negative definite: that decides extrema in two variables.
  22. Same lesson: from second order on, track every term.
  23. Two routes, one answer, once nothing is left off the list.
