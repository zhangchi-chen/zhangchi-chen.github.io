09-03 The Newton–Leibniz formula: differentiation and integration make peace

The Newton–Leibniz formula: differentiation and integration make peace

1 · Motivation

Two questions that seemed to have nothing in common are really one. For a long time, these two questions lived separate lives. One group wanted to know how steep a curve is at one point. That is the tangent problem, the core of chapter five. The other group asked how much area sits under a curve. Their trick was to cut the region into thin strips and add them up. Each group had its own tools, its own symbols, its own tricks.

An end of term problem asks for the area under this curve, from 0 to 1. Lin cuts it into strips and grinds through the limit of sums, like episode nine one. After a full page of work, the sums settle on the natural log of 2. Chen goes the other way and hunts for a function with exactly that slope. He finds a logarithm that fits, and its rise from 0 to 1 is log 2 again. Same number, totally different road. Is that luck? Today we show it is never luck.

2 · Statements

Take a continuous function f on [a, b]; here a = 0 and b = 6.
Now let a right edge x slide, shading the area from a to x.
The shaded area depends on x: call it Φ(x).
In symbols, Φ(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt, the variable-limit integral.
As x moves, Φ(x) traces out a graph of its own.
Where f is tall, the area piles up fast.
Where f is low, the area only trickles in.
The claim to earn: Φ grows at a rate equal to the height f(x).
The course text states it as Theorem 9.10.
Hypothesis: f is continuous on [a, b], and nothing more.
Conclusion one: Φ is differentiable at every point of [a, b].
At the endpoints a and b, read it as one-sided derivatives.
Conclusion two: its derivative is exactly f, Φ′(x) = f(x).
For its importance, it is called the fundamental theorem of calculus.
In words: differentiate the accumulated area, and the height comes back.

Start with a continuous function on an interval, here from 0 to 6. Now let a right edge slide along, shading the area behind it. The shaded area depends on where the edge stands; call it Phi. In symbols, this is the variable limit integral. As the edge moves, the area traces out a graph of its own. Where the curve is tall, area piles up fast. Where the curve is low, area only trickles in. The claim to earn: the area grows exactly as fast as the curve is tall.

The course text calls it theorem nine point ten. The only hypothesis is continuity; nothing more is asked. First conclusion: the area function has a derivative everywhere. At the two endpoints, it means the derivative from one side. Second conclusion: its derivative is the original function itself. It is honored as the fundamental theorem of calculus. In plain words, differentiate the piled up area and the height comes back.

3 · Method

The proof is two steps long; keep an eye on where continuity steps in. Nudge the edge a little to the right. The new area is exactly this thin strip. Divide by the width, and you get the strip's average height. Because the function is continuous, the integral first mean value theorem says the curve hits that average. So somewhere inside the strip there is a point with exactly that height. Line one, exactly: the average equals the height at that point. Nothing has been approximated yet; the rectangle holds the same area as the strip. Line two: shrink the strip, and the special point is squeezed onto the edge. The decisive move is continuity: the two heights are pulled together. If the nudge goes left, the strip sits on the left, and nothing changes. So the limit exists, and the area's growth rate is the height. Done.

Take continuity away, and the second step can fall apart. Let the function jump from 1 up to 2 at the point 1. Strips on the right average 2, strips on the left only 1. So the area function has slope 1 on the left and 2 on the right, and no derivative at 1.

First payoff: every continuous function has an antiderivative, and here it is. Back in episode eight one, theorem eight point one promised this, with the proof still owed. Paid in full, with the receipt: the area function itself is that antiderivative. Even a bell shaped curve with no elementary antiderivative gets one this way.

Second payoff: the Newton Leibniz formula, found by chasing one constant. Take any antiderivative at all, found however you like. Both have the same derivative, so the gap between them never changes. This is the corollary of Lagrange's mean value theorem, from episode six two. At the left edge the area is zero, so the constant is where F starts. Now step to the right edge, and the formula falls out. The whole area under the curve equals how much the antiderivative rises. The course text records exactly this chase. Like checking out of a hostel: what you leave with, minus what you came with. Area becomes bookkeeping: find one antiderivative, read two values, subtract.

Theorem nine point one in the course text states it directly. Two hypotheses: continuity, and the derivative identity at every single point. Continuity buys integrability, from episode nine two; the antiderivative gives the value. Careful: if that derivative identity breaks at an inside point, hands off; chapter eleven tells the crash.

4 · Outlook

Back to the opening problem, the limit of sums from episode nine one. With a logarithm as antiderivative, one subtraction gives log 2. Lin's page of sums and Chen's single line were one method all along. Second use: the area of a quarter of the unit disc. Chapter eight's tables hand us an antiderivative with an arcsine in it. Plug in the two ends, and out comes a quarter of pi. Geometry agrees: exactly a quarter of the whole disc.

A use beyond today: a phone charging fast at first, then slower. The charge gained is the area under the rate: end reading minus start reading. With this rate, the formula says 45 percent in half an hour. Second use: the element method of episode ten one runs on this same engine. Anything that piles up at a continuous rate: undo one derivative, then subtract. In episode nine four, the area function goes to work, differentiated and pushed to limits.

With two variables, a region fills up, and its boundary takes over the edge's role. That idea is called Green's formula, and it waits for later. Slopes and areas are one road, walked in two directions.
