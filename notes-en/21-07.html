21-07 Applications of multiple integrals: centroids and moments of inertia

Applications of multiple integrals: centroids and moments of inertia

1 · Motivation

Two questions today: where a body balances, and how hard it is to spin. A lunch tray, loaded unevenly: light on the left, heavy on the right. Where does one finger go, so that nothing tips over? Average the positions, but let the heavy dishes count more. The balance point lands one step toward the heavy bowl. Then the soup spills, and the mass spreads everywhere. There is no list left to add, so the sum turns into an integral.

Second question: a figure skater spins with her arms out. She pulls her arms in, and the spin speeds up. Nothing got lighter, yet every piece moved closer to the axis. Balance weighs positions, spin weighs squared distances, and both need integrals.

2 · Statements

The centroid of a solid V with density ρ, as the course text writes it.
On top: every position x, weighted by the density there.
Underneath: ∭ ρ dV is simply the total mass.
The same recipe gives ȳ and z̄: three coordinates, three ratios.
Uniform density: ρ cancels, and x̄ is the average of x over V.
So a uniform centroid is pure geometry: only the shape matters.
A thin plate D: dV becomes dσ, and ∭ becomes ∬.
Moments of inertia of V about the three coordinate axes:
Each sum of squares is the squared distance to that axis.
About the z-axis only x² + y² counts; height plays no role.

About the x-axis, a plate point sits at distance |y|.

Where do these formulas come from? The micro-element method.

Here is how the course text writes the centroid of a solid body. On top, every position, weighted by how dense the body is there. Underneath sits the total mass, and nothing else. The other two coordinates follow the very same recipe. If the density is uniform, it cancels, and the centroid is a plain average. Then only the shape matters, nothing else. For a thin plate, the same formula lives one dimension lower.

Now the spin cost about each coordinate axis, in the course text's words. Each of those sums of squares is a squared distance to an axis. Spinning about the vertical axis, height plays no role at all. For a flat plate, the height terms vanish and the formulas slim down. For the plate, distance to the horizontal axis is just the height. Tilt the axis any way you like; measure straight across to it. One weighs positions, the other weighs squared distances, both by mass. So where do these formulas come from? An old friend.

3 · Method

The micro element method from episode ten oh one is back: chop the body into small pieces. Pretend each piece is a single dot carrying all of its mass. And now it is just the lunch tray again, with many tiny dishes. Let the pieces shrink, and both sums turn into integrals. Three moves: chop, pretend, and take the limit. The total mass is positive, so the division survives the limit.

Why may we take that limit? Episode ten oh one already answered. First, the whole is the sum of its pieces. Second, treating a piece as a dot errs by far less than the piece itself. Nothing new to believe: we reuse a theorem we already own. Episode ten oh five cooked the same recipes in one variable.

First a uniform body: the upper half of an ellipsoid, shaped like a dome. Before any integral, look for mirror pairs. Each pair cancels, so the balance point sits on the mirror plane. Same trick sideways, and still no integral. Top and bottom differ, so the height needs one honest integral. Slice it at every height; each slice is an ellipse. Weigh each slice by its height and add them up: the top is done. Divide by half the volume, and the centroid sits three eighths of the way up. Lower than halfway, because most of the dome sits near its wide base.

Back to the tray: a flat plate balances on a single pin. Now let the plate grow heavier toward the right. The pin slides right, two thirds of the way across. Up and down nothing changes, so the pin stays at half height.

Now the spinning. Start with a single dot circling an axis. Each turn, the dot runs a circle, and its radius is the distance to the axis. Twice as far means twice as fast, and four times the energy. A dot on the axis never moves, so it adds nothing. For a whole body, chop and add, exactly as before. Each axis formula just reads off its own squared distance.

Careful: the distance to the origin is the wrong distance. Slide a cylinder up along its axis: it spins exactly as before. The true distance stays put, while the wrong one keeps growing. The honest answer ignores the height; the wrong one grows with it. Back to the skater: halve the distance, and that term drops to a quarter.

First worked number: a uniform ring plate, spun about its center. Polar coordinates from episode twenty one oh three, with their extra factor r. The angle contributes a full turn, the radius a difference of fourth powers. Bring in the mass, and out comes the course text's answer. Two sanity checks: a thin ring, and a full disk.

Second worked number: spin the disk about a diameter, like a coin. Lay the diameter along the vertical axis; only sideways distance counts. In polar form it splits into an angle part and a radius part. Put the mass back in, and there is the course text's quarter. Why a quarter? Sideways, a point keeps only half of its squared distance, on average. Both directions split the square evenly, so it is exactly half.

Radius of gyration: squeeze all the mass onto one ring. That ring spins with exactly the same effort as the real body. Swap the ring plate for a bicycle rim of that radius. For a full disk, that rim sits at about seven tenths of the radius. In other words, it is the average squared distance, weighted by mass.

4 · Outlook

Bonus: a joint probability density is just a plate whose total mass is one. Every expectation is the same kind of weighted integral. The expected position is simply the balance point of that plate. A random point on a round disk: how far from the center, on average? In ordinary coordinates, the square roots make it a mess. Switch to polar coordinates, and one line gives two thirds. Credit goes to episodes twenty one oh two and oh three. And the average squared distance is one half: the radius of gyration, squared.

Use one: a loaded bookshelf stays standing while its centroid is above its feet. Use two: bike makers save weight at the rim first, where it counts most. The same section of the course text also handles surface area and gravity; we only name them. Later, episode twenty two oh one weighs surfaces the same way; the course text says so.

Looking ahead, mechanics arranges these integrals into a three by three inertia tensor. And probability keeps reading expectations as these very integrals. Chop, weigh, add, and let the shape choose the coordinates.
