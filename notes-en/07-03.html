07-03 Settling the closed-interval debts

Settling the closed-interval debts

1 · Motivation

Today we settle the closed interval debts. It's semester's end, and our group ledger still has open rows. Chapter four promised three theorems and put off their proofs. And underneath sits the monotone bounded machinery from chapter two.

Here is a shortcut that many of us have tried. Continuity at a point keeps the function bounded nearby. So surely it must be bounded on the whole interval, right?

Try one over x on the open interval from zero to one. Near every single point, it really is bounded. Yet taken as a whole, the graph shoots off forever. So which word in the theorem is really doing the work? The answer is the word closed, so watch where it gets spent.

3 · Method

We take three keys straight off the ring we just closed.

Here is the course text's boundedness proof, all in one breath. Step one: continuity at a point hands us a neighbourhood and a local bound. Every point gets its own, so infinitely many neighbourhoods. Like a little photo frame for every moment of a trip. Together they form an open cover of the closed interval. Step two: the Finite Covering Theorem keeps only finitely many. One album, and it still shows the whole trip. Step three: pick the largest of finitely many bounds. Every point sits in some kept neighbourhood, so the big bound holds. The word closed gets spent right here, in step two.

Back to one over x: every point has a local bound. The closer to zero, the larger each local bound. Finitely many neighbourhoods always leave a gap near zero. On the open interval no finite subcover exists, so boundedness is lost. From local to finite: remember these three steps.

Next row in the ledger: the Extreme Value Theorem. Bounded, so the values have a least upper bound. Pick points whose values creep up toward the supremum. These points may hop around, but they never leave the interval. Bolzano Weierstrass steps in and pulls out a convergent subsequence. Closedness again: the limit point cannot escape the interval. Continuity there makes the value hit the top. Flip the function upside down, repeat, and the minimum is ours too. Two keys work together: one supplies the bound, the other the point. On the open interval the points run off to one, which is outside.

Next row: the Intermediate Value Theorem. Say the left end sits below the level, the right end above it. Cut it in half, and keep the half where the sign still flips. Again and again, and the kept halves nest one inside another. What never changes: left end below, right end above. The widths shrink to nothing, and the Nested Interval Theorem hands us one point. One side caps it, the other floors it, so it lands on the level. Neither end touches the level, so the point lies strictly inside. The bisection you ran in chapter four now runs as a proof.

Last row: Cantor's theorem, put off back in chapter four. Suppose not: some gap refuses to vanish, however small delta gets. So we get pairs of points ever closer, whose values stay a gap apart. Compactness steps in again and pulls out a convergent subsequence. Their partners get dragged along, and the two sequences braid together. Continuity at that point pulls both values together. But that gap never shrinks, and that's a contradiction. So the function is uniformly continuous there.

Closedness is spent here: the limit point must land inside the interval. For one over x, the braid runs off to zero, outside the interval. Or halve the interval, keeping a half where uniform continuity still fails. The nested point clashes with continuity there: same ending, another key.

4 · Outlook

Back to the ledger, and every row now has a key beside it. Covering for boundedness, and compactness for reaching the extremes. Nesting for intermediate values, and either key for Cantor. Chapter two's row closes too, since today's keys were built on it.

First use: the maximum exists, so chapter six's candidate comparison makes sense. Second use: a continuous map of the unit interval into itself. Value minus input starts at or above zero, ends at or below, so it hits zero. Third use: Cantor's theorem is what will make continuous functions integrable.

Later, local to finite becomes the very definition of compactness. In several variables and in integration theory, the same move replays. Whenever local must turn global on a closed bounded set, this move returns. The ledger closes: every promise from chapter four is kept.
