17-02 Partial derivatives: hold one variable still, differentiate the other

Partial derivatives: hold one variable still, differentiate the other

1 · Motivation

Partial derivatives: hold one variable still, and differentiate the other. With one variable, life was simple: one slope at every point. Now the function takes two inputs, and either one can move.

It is like the wobbly desk in your dorm, rocking two ways at once. Press one edge down, and now it rocks in only one direction. Rocking one way is a one variable problem, and you already know how to solve those.

Same trick for our function. Freeze y, and only one input is left moving. Do it twice, in opposite orders, and two classmates get minus one and one. Same function, same point. So who is wrong? Hold on to that question.

2 · Statements

Definition 2: freeze y at y₀ and let only x move.
The limit is written f_x(x₀, y₀) or ∂f/∂x; y works the same way.
Freeze y = y₀: what remains is a partial function of x.
Nudge x by Δx; the change in z is the partial increment.
Divide by Δx: that ratio is the slope of a secant line.
Let Δx shrink to 0: the secant turns into the tangent.
Its slope is the partial derivative; here f_x(1, 0.5) = 1.
The recipe: treat y as a constant, then differentiate as usual.

Freeze y: f_x = 3x² + 4xy, and the −y³ term drops out.
At (1, 3): f_x = 3 + 12 = 15.

Partials watch only the axes — the two-path lesson of 16-02.

Definition two holds one variable fixed and lets the other move. That limit is the partial derivative, and the other variable gets one built the same way.

Freeze one input, and what remains is a partial function of a single variable. Nudge the input a little; the change in height is the partial increment. Divide by the step, and you get the slope of a secant line. Now let the step shrink to nothing, and the secant swings into the tangent. That slope is the partial derivative, and here it is exactly one.

The recipe is short: treat the frozen variable as a constant, and differentiate as usual. Example two from the course text: a cubic in two variables, at the point one, three. Freeze y. Every term follows the old rules, and the last one vanishes. At that point, we get fifteen. Now swap roles. Freeze x, and let the other one move. Freeze x. Again term by term, and this time the first term vanishes. At that point we get minus twenty five. One point, two rates: up fast one way, down faster the other.

Example three: a power with a variable exponent. Which rule do we use? Freeze the exponent, and it is a plain power, so the power rule applies. Freeze the base, and it becomes an exponential, so its own rule applies. Whoever is frozen decides which rule gets to step on stage.

Now the picture: our function draws a surface over the plane. Slice it with a vertical plane that keeps one input fixed. The cut traces a curve, the graph of the partial function. The tangent to that curve tilts by exactly the partial derivative. Slice the other way instead: a second curve, with its own tangent. Its steepness is the other partial derivative. Two slopes, two tangent lines, both through one point. But two slopes alone do not make a tangent plane; as the last episode showed, that takes differentiability.

How weak are partial derivatives on their own? Watch this function. On both axes the function is zero, so both partial derivatives are zero. But walk in along the diagonal, and the value stays at one half. So both partial derivatives exist, and yet the function breaks at the origin. Partial derivatives only watch the axes; that is the two path lesson from chapter sixteen.

3 · Method

Partial derivatives are functions themselves, so we can differentiate them again. Each one branches in two again, giving four second order partial derivatives. Here are all four, and those involving both variables are called mixed partial derivatives. Careful here: the subscripts record the order in which we differentiate. Take the x derivative first, then switch to the other variable. In the course text, the fraction form keeps the same order, left to right. Some other books flip that order, so check the convention before you read. A two by two table: rows say what comes first, columns what comes second. The two cells off the diagonal are the mixed partial derivatives. Higher order partial derivatives read the same way, one step after another.

First example: an exponential. Let's fill in all four cells. Each y derivative pulls out a factor of two. Second order: the four cells are one, two, two and four times the original. And look: the two mixed partial derivatives agree.

Now a harder one from the course text, built from an arctangent. First, the two first order partials. Then the mixed ones: both orders land on the same answer. They agree again. But do they always?

Back to our two classmates, with minus one and one at the origin. A note in the course text gives their function and both values. Classmate A differentiates in x first, so she watches the vertical axis. Along that axis, she gets exactly minus the step. Now in the other variable: the answer is minus one. Classmate B differentiates in y first, so he watches the horizontal axis. He gets exactly the step. Then in the other variable: the answer is one. Same function, same point, two orders, and two different answers. Nobody made a slip. The two orders are really two iterated limits.

Both start from one quantity: the function at four corners of a small rectangle, with signs. Shrink the width first, then the height: that is classmate A's route. Shrink the height first instead: that is classmate B's route. For our function, the quotient is a simple ratio of squares. Its two iterated limits are minus one and one: exactly our classmates' answers. Just as chapter sixteen warned us: iterated limits can disagree.

Theorem seventeen point seven, Clairaut: if both mixed partials are continuous, they are equal. Why? Apply the mean value theorem twice to that four corner quantity. The quotient equals one mixed partial at some point inside the rectangle. It also equals the other one, at another point inside. Shrink the rectangle, and continuity drags both values to the same corner. So the two mixed partials agree: the iterated limits are pinned together. In our counterexample, the mixed partial hits both one and minus one right next to the origin. So it is discontinuous at the origin, and the theorem's hypothesis fails. From here on, the course text simply assumes mixed partials are continuous. So the order never matters again, and this theorem is the reason why.

4 · Outlook

First use: a sealed water bottle on a sunny dorm windowsill. The pressure inside depends on two things: temperature and volume. The bottle fixes the volume; heating is a partial derivative. Squeeze it at constant temperature, and the other partial derivative takes over.

Second use: a steady temperature in a flat plate obeys Laplace's equation. Our arctangent passes: the two second partials add up to exactly zero.

Back in the total differential episode: partials are just two axis samples of one linear map. In the chain rule episode, partials will be the raw ingredients. In a later episode, the Hessian matrix is symmetric, thanks to Clairaut.

Clairaut is only the first of many theorems about swapping two limit processes. Later come limits with integrals, and derivatives with improper integrals. And integral with integral, when we reach double integrals. Freeze one, move the other; and with continuity, the order never matters.
