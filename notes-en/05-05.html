05-05 The elementary derivative table: three mother cards breed a family tree

The elementary derivative table: three mother cards breed a family tree

1 · Motivation

Today, the whole derivative table grows out of just three formulas. Midnight before the quiz, and two formula sheets disagree. One sheet multiplies by the natural log of a, the other divides. Memory alone cannot settle it; you need a way to rebuild the formula.

The key move: rewrite the whole thing as a power of e. The outer layer copies itself, and the inner layer contributes the natural log of a. So your sheet was right: multiply by the natural log of a.

Test it on two to the x: at zero the slope should be about 0.69. The roommate value, about 1.44, slices through the curve instead of touching it. Could the whole table be rebuilt like that, from just a few seeds?

First, the five rules from the course text, exactly as written. Each rule takes derivatives you already know and makes a new one. Then the basic formulas: constants, powers, and the four trigonometric rows. And the inverse trig, exponential and logarithm rows complete the list.

Something that never moves has a rate of change of zero. Any real exponent is allowed, and at 1 the slope equals the exponent. Slide along the sine curve and record the slope: you draw the cosine. That square is never below 1, so the tangent climbs on every branch. Where the sine turns flat, its inverse turns steep. The exponential is its own slope: the higher it is, the steeper. The log has slope one over x: steep near zero, nearly flat far away. Fourteen formulas and five rules, and almost none of it needs memorizing.

3 · Method

Picture a reunion photo wall, with three grandparent photos at the very top. Card one, the exponential: its derivative is itself. Card two, the sine: its derivative is the cosine. Card three, the power: bring the exponent down, then lower it by one. Below them, the five rules are the arrows that produce every other photo. Last episode promised exactly this: chain and inverse rules build the table.

First child, already met: powers of a, from the exponential by the chain rule. Next, the log is the inverse of the exponential: read the relation backwards. Flip the slope of the exponential, and the log gets one over x. The negative side agrees, so the same formula holds on both sides. Change of base: the log base a is just the natural log times a constant. Here the natural log of a lands in the denominator, the opposite of before. The constant row needs no parent at all: nothing changes, so zero.

On the trig side, first write the cosine as a sine in disguise. The outer layer gives back the sine, and the inner layer adds a minus sign. So the cosine slope is minus the sine, and it joins the wall. The tangent is sine over cosine, a job for the quotient rule. The numerator collapses to 1, leaving the secant squared. Cotangent goes the same way, landing on minus cosecant squared. For the arcsine, read it backwards, keeping only the middle range of angles. Flip the sine slope; on that range the cosine is a positive square root. The arcsine joins the wall, and the arccosine follows by the sum rule. Invert the tangent row and use one identity: everything becomes a function of x. Arctangent and arccotangent arrive, and the whole trig family is rebuilt.

On the power side, this fraction is really a power with exponent minus one. Apply the power card, then multiply by the inner slope. Any other exponent works the same way, in one motion. Three cards and five rules, and every row of the list is on the wall.

Now the reverse job: a nested function arrives. Where do you start? Like a prerequisite chart: read the top box before you sign up below. The rule: name the outermost operation before you touch the pen. First example: exponential outside, a negative square inside. Copy the outer card, then multiply by the inner slope. Forget the inner factor, and at 1 even the sign of the slope is wrong. Second example: sine outside, and the inner layer contributes a factor 2. Forget that factor, and the tangent at zero is only half as steep. Third example: a tenth power outside, a quadratic inside. Power card times inner slope: the tenth power drops to a ninth. Expanding first means eleven terms; working outside in skips all that.

Careful: the rules only work inside each piece, never at the join itself. The problem: choose the line so the two pieces join smoothly at 1. The tempting shortcut: differentiate each piece and match them at 1. That pins down the slope, but says nothing about the height. Pick the wrong height: the slopes match, yet the graph jumps at the join. A jump breaks continuity, and without continuity there is no derivative. So go back to the definition, with the one sided quotients from before. Step one, continuity: the line must arrive exactly at height 1. Step two, slopes: the left side gives 2, so the line needs slope 2 too. So slope 2 and intercept minus 1: the line is the tangent itself. Matching slopes fixed one number; only continuity could fix the other.

One habit makes this automatic: store every row with an inner layer built in. Every row ends with the inner slope, so lookup and chaining happen at once. Example: a log of a cosine, so the outer row is the log. Divide the inner slope by the inner function: minus the tangent. In one breath: hyperbolic sine and cosine come from the exponential, and each is the derivative of the other.

4 · Outlook

First use: how much of your view the board fills as you sit further back. Outermost operation first: an arctangent, with a fraction inside. Table row and chain rule fire together, and the rate comes out negative. Near the front the view shrinks fast; at the back it barely changes.

Second use: news spreading through a dorm follows an S shaped curve. The power card with exponent minus one, through the chain, gives the rate. Rewritten, the rate depends only on how many know: fastest at one half. That is why machine learning code loves this curve: its slope reuses its own value.

The chain rule episode made a promise, and today it was paid in full. A debt to chapter six: a limit theorem will let the slope formula check some joins. But continuity at the join must still be checked by hand, every time.

Every elementary function now differentiates mechanically, so exams hide the difficulty elsewhere. Break points, parameters, and proofs about the derivative instead of computing it. The next two episodes are the first places where that shift happens. Three cards and five rules: you can regenerate any row whenever you need it.
