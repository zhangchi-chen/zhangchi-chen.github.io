12-08 Abel and Dirichlet: summation by parts enters

Abel and Dirichlet: summation by parts enters

1 · Motivation

Abel and Dirichlet, and the trick behind them, summation by parts. Here's a series from the problem set. Does it converge? The terms do shrink to zero, but that alone never proves convergence. The comparison principle gives nothing, its only bound is the harmonic series. The Leibniz test needs the sign to flip every step. Here the signs travel in clumps.

And yet, watch the partial sums. They seem to calm down. Add up the sines one by one, and the total never wanders far. Meanwhile the other factor just shrinks, steadily, toward zero. It's the group project again. One member is steady, the other wavers but never wanders off. Together they get the job done, and summation by parts explains why.

3 · Method

To prove it, aim for the Cauchy criterion from the first episode. Take any block of consecutive terms, as long as you like. Inside the block, each total is a gap between two partial sums, so at most twice the bound. That's all the wandering partner has to do, stay close to base. Abel's lemma now caps the block by the first factor's largest value, times a constant. The steady factor dies out, so for any epsilon the cap soon falls below it. Every far-away block is tiny, so by the Cauchy criterion the series converges. Abel's proof is the twin. This time the other series supplies the smallness. Why insist on one direction? Here's a factor that shrinks to zero but zigzags. Pair it with a flipping sign and you get the harmonic series, which diverges.

A family reunion. Let the other factor just flip sign, its totals hop between 1 and 0. Then Dirichlet says, a steadily dying factor makes the alternating series converge. That's the Leibniz test, from the alternating series episode. These tests are nested tools, not rivals.

Now the real prize. Do the cosine sums stay bounded? Multiply by a helper factor, and each cosine splits into two sines. Neighbours cancel in pairs, and the whole chain collapses to a single sine. That is the course text's identity 21.

Between zero and two pi the helper factor is positive, so dividing is safe. So the cosine sums are bounded, with a bound that ignores how many terms. At a third of pi the bound is one and a half, and the sums stay inside. Sines get the same treatment, and their sums are bounded too.

Stay away from the ends, and one single bound serves every point. But slide toward zero, and the bound shoots up without limit. Right at zero, every cosine equals 1, and the sums just climb forever. There the cosine series becomes the harmonic one, and really diverges.

Payoff time. Split the sine series into its two partners. The steady factor decreases to zero, and the sine sums stay bounded. Dirichlet's test says it converges, our problem-set series included. The cosine series converges the same way there.

But absolutely? Try a half pi, where the sines are 1 and 0 by turns. Take absolute values, and only the odd reciprocals survive. Compare with half the harmonic series, and it diverges. So at a half pi the convergence is conditional, not absolute.

4 · Outlook

First use, a series that Abel's test settles in one line. One factor creeps up but stays bounded, the other series converges. Second use, even a factor that shrinks this slowly still does the job.

Improper integrals of a product get the very same two tests. Both tests return later in a uniform version, owed to chapter thirteen. And later, this sine series will compute special values.

The whole chapter's toolbox, on one map. First, check that the terms die out. If not, it diverges. Positive terms? Reach for comparison, ratio or root, the integral test, or Raabe. Mixed signs? First ask whether it converges absolutely. If not, and the signs alternate, Leibniz is the tool. A steady factor times a wanderer? Abel or Dirichlet. One steady, one bounded. Together, they converge.
