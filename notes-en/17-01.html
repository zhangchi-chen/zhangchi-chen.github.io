17-01 The total differential: the tangent plane takes over as the linear stand-in

The total differential: the tangent plane takes over as the linear stand-in

1 · Motivation

The total differential, where a flat plane stands in for a curved surface. Zoom into a hiking map on your phone, and the hill starts to look flat. Up close, the contour lines go straight, side by side, with nearly equal gaps. Straight contours with equal gaps: that is the fingerprint of a flat plane.

Now by hand: estimate 1.08 to the power 3.96, with no calculator. Think of it as x to the power y, a small step away from the point one, four. But which plane? With one variable, a single slope did the whole job. Here we get two slopes, one for each direction of the grid. Do two slopes always settle the tangent plane? Not always. A surface can show both slopes and still refuse a tangent plane. So today's question: what should differentiable mean with two variables?

2 · Statements

Recall 05-08: in one variable, Δy = AΔx + o(Δx), one constant A.
In the plane, a linear function of the step needs two constants: AΔx + BΔy.
Same definition, one dimension up — the promise made in 05-08 is kept.
Definition 1: the total increment Δz splits as AΔx + BΔy + o(ρ).
Then f is differentiable at P₀, and AΔx + BΔy is its total differential.
Clause by clause: start at P₀ and take a step (Δx, Δy) to P.
The total increment Δz is how much the height changes along that step.
ρ is the straight-line length of the step.
A and B are fixed by P₀ alone; they don't depend on the step.
So AΔx + BΔy is a linear function of the step.
And o(ρ) is a higher-order infinitesimal: o(ρ)/ρ → 0 as ρ → 0⁺.
In short: Δz is a linear guess plus an error far smaller than ρ.
First test, the course text's Example 1: f(x, y) = xy.
Read xy as the area of an x₀-by-y₀ rectangle.
Stretch the sides by Δx and Δy; the area grows by Δz.
The two thin strips, y₀Δx and x₀Δy, form the linear part.
What's left is the corner ΔxΔy — the last bit of topping sliding off the tray.
Is the corner o(ρ)? Its size over ρ is at most ρ/2, which goes to 0.
So xy is differentiable, with total differential dz = y₀Δx + x₀Δy.
It's the two-dimensional twin of the L-shaped strip from 05-08.
Now the picture: the graph of f is a surface over the plane.
At the point above P₀, put the plane of the linear part.
dz is how much the plane rises; Δz is how much the surface rises.
Δz − dz is the small gap between surface and plane.
Now zoom in on P₀, and keep your eye on that gap.
The closer we look, the flatter the surface: it hugs the plane.
Even divided by ρ, the gap still shrinks to 0: that is o(ρ).
Theorem 17.4: a non-vertical tangent plane exists exactly when f is differentiable.
The course text's test: Q on the surface, h its distance to the plane, d = |PQ|.
The plane is tangent when h/d → 0 as Q slides into P.

Recall one variable: the change is one constant times the step, plus a tiny leftover. In the plane, a linear function of the step needs two constants, one per direction. Same definition, one dimension up: the chapter five promise is kept. Definition one: the total increment splits into a linear part and a tiny leftover. Then f is called differentiable there, and the linear part is the total differential.

Clause by clause. Start at the base point and take one small step. The total increment is how much the height changes over that step. Rho is simply how far the step goes, in a straight line. A and B are set by the base point alone, whatever step you take. So the first part is a linear function of the step. And the leftover is a higher order infinitesimal: it fades faster than rho. In short, the change is a linear guess plus an error that is tiny next to rho.

First test case, Example one from the course text: x times y. Picture x times y as the area of a rectangle. Stretch both sides a little, and the area grows by the total increment. The two thin strips along the sides make up the linear part. What remains is the little corner, like the last bit of topping sliding off the tray. Is the corner small enough? Divided by rho, it is at most half of rho, so it fades. So x times y is differentiable, and the two strips are its total differential. It is the two dimensional twin of the L shaped strip from chapter five.

Now the picture: the graph of f is a landscape over the floor. At the point above the base, lay down the plane of the linear part. The total differential is the plane's rise; the total increment is the surface's rise. Subtract one from the other: a thin gap between surface and plane. Now zoom in toward the point, and watch the gap. The closer we look, the flatter the hillside gets, until it hugs the plane. Even measured against rho, the gap still dies out. That is the leftover term. Theorem seventeen point four: a non vertical tangent plane exists exactly when f is differentiable. The test compares two distances from a moving point: to the plane, and to the base point. Tangent means the first distance becomes negligible next to the second.

3 · Method

If the two constants exist, what are they? First, step only sideways. With the other step at zero, the ratio of changes heads straight to A. That limit is the slope of the slice along x: the partial derivative. Step only along the other direction, and the same story gives B. Theorem seventeen point one: differentiable forces both partial derivatives to exist, and to be A and B. So the total differential is built from the two partial derivatives. The two partial derivatives are just two samples of one linear map. And since the constants have no freedom, the total differential is unique. But not the other way round: partial derivatives may exist with no differentiability at all.

Example five in the course text, set to zero at the origin. Its graph is a tent, and the tip of the pole sits at the origin. Along both axes the tent stays at zero, so both partial derivatives are zero. So if it were differentiable, the total differential would have to vanish. Then the gap is the function itself; divide by rho. Here is the quotient. Does it fade to zero?

Bring back the two path test from chapter sixteen: along the axes, it is zero. Along the diagonal, the quotient is one half, every single time. Two paths, two different answers, so there is no limit, and no differentiability. In fact, the quotient depends only on the direction you come from. The moral: partial derivatives only ever look along the two axes. Differentiability must keep every direction in check, all at once. Zoom into the tip of the tent as far as you like; it never flattens out.

So when can we be sure? Theorem seventeen point two offers a sufficient condition. Theorem seventeen point two: partial derivatives that exist nearby and are continuous at the point give differentiability. Goal: the leftover after the linear part must be tiny next to rho. Walk an L shaped path, and on each leg, use the one variable mean value theorem. As the step shrinks, continuity pulls those shifted slopes back home. What is left is a vanishing factor times rho, so it is tiny, and f is differentiable. Careful: continuous partials are enough, but they are not required. The course text has one: differentiable at the origin, yet its partials are not continuous there. The routine: find both partial derivatives, then check that the leftover over rho fades. Shortcut: if both partials are continuous at the point, you are done.

4 · Outlook

Back to our estimate: replace the function by its linear approximation. Take x to the power y, at the point one, four, where it equals 1. There, the partial derivatives are 4 and 0, because the log of 1 is 0. So the estimate is 1 plus 4 times 0.08, which gives 1.32. The true value is about 1.3563, higher by about 0.036. That gap comes from second order terms; chapter seventeen, episode five will close it.

A second use, error estimates: a desk measured at 120 by 60 centimetres. Each tape reading could be off by half a centimetre. The total differential says 90 square centimetres; the exact worst case is 90.25. The missing quarter is exactly the corner from before.

Chapter seventeen, episode three will show the total differential keeps its form. Looking ahead: this linear map is the derivative of f at the point. Between higher dimensional spaces it becomes a matrix, the Jacobian, in chapter twenty three. And in functional analysis, the same idea becomes a linear operator. One idea to keep: up close, every change is a linear part plus a fading leftover.
