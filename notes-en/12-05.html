12-05 Alternating series: one step down, one offset, remainder controlled

Alternating series: one step down, one offset, remainder controlled

1 · Motivation

Alternating series: one step down, one offset, and the remainder under control. A classic formula claims pi over four is this alternating sum. The course proves it later. Today it is simply our test case.

Stop after three terms, and we land at about 0.867. But how far off is it, when the true value is still unknown? Take away one seventh, and it drops back to about 0.724. Every new term swings it back and forth, a little less each time. The true value seems trapped between two neighbouring partial sums. If that holds, the very next term caps the error. Today's theorem turns that hope into a guarantee.

2 · Statements

An alternating series looks like u₁ − u₂ + u₃ − u₄ + ⋯.
Every uₙ is positive; the signs alone do the alternating.
The course text's first example: 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − ⋯.
Second: 1 − 1/3! + 1/5! − 1/7! + ⋯.
Third: 1/10 − 2/10² + 3/10³ − ⋯.
Do they converge? One picture will answer all three.
Theorem 12.11, the Leibniz test, also called the alternating series test.
If the alternating series (1) satisfies
(i) {uₙ} is monotone decreasing
and (ii) lim uₙ = 0,
then it converges.
Condition (i): each term is no bigger than the one before.
Condition (ii): the terms shrink all the way down to zero.
The conclusion: the partial sums settle on a single number.
Two conditions, one promise: convergence, and nothing more.
If the series satisfies the conditions of Leibniz's test,
the remainder estimate of the convergent series is |Rₙ| ≤ uₙ₊₁.
Here Rₙ = S − Sₙ: everything left after the first n terms.
In plain words: the first term you leave out caps the error.

An alternating series: the signs take turns, plus then minus. Every term size is positive; only the signs take turns. The course text's first example, the alternating harmonic series. Second: odd factorials below, signs alternating. Third: one, two, three over powers of ten. Do they converge? One picture will settle all three.

Here is the Leibniz test, also called the alternating series test. If the alternating series satisfies two things: first, the terms are monotone decreasing, second, they tend to zero, then it converges. Condition one: each term is no bigger than the one before. Condition two: the terms shrink all the way down to zero. The payoff: the partial sums settle on a single number. Two conditions, one promise: convergence, and nothing more.

And a corollary: if the series meets the Leibniz conditions, the remainder is never bigger than the next term. The remainder is everything left after the first n terms. In plain words, the first term you leave out caps the error.

3 · Method

Let's plot the partial sums of the alternating harmonic series. A zigzag. So first, look only at the even ones. Pair the terms up, so each even sum is a stack of brackets. Condition one makes every bracket zero or more. So the even partial sums never go down. Now regroup the odd sums: the first term, minus a stack of brackets. Again each bracket is zero or more, and each one is taken away. So the odd partial sums never go up.

Picture settling a debt with your roommate, transfers going back and forth. After you pay, you've paid too much; after a refund, too little. The real debt is trapped between the two running totals. Every even partial sum lies below every odd one. And the gap between neighbours is exactly one term. Condition two squeezes that gap down to zero. So these intervals nest one inside the next, shrinking to nothing.

That's the nested interval theorem from chapter seven, back again. It hands us exactly one point inside every interval. Both strands close in on that point, so the series converges. The whole proof fits in two short lines. Call the sum A. Its exact value waits for episode fourteen oh four.

Now the bonus: the sum always sits between two consecutive partial sums. Those two sit one term apart, so the remainder can't be bigger. In debt terms, the next unpaid instalment is the most you can be off. For our series, the error after n terms is at most the next fraction. Check it: the true error always stays under the bound. Want the error under one hundredth? Then 99 terms will do. Slow, but guaranteed, and known before you add a single term.

Back to pi over four: that series passes both conditions. Three terms used, so the error is at most the fourth term, one seventh. The true error, about 0.081, does stay below that bound. The opening question, answered without knowing pi.

Careful: both conditions are doing real work. Drop condition one: these terms bob up and down, yet still fade to zero. Now some brackets turn negative, and the odd strand can climb. This one actually diverges: its odd-position terms alone grow without limit. Drop condition two: these terms still decrease, but only toward one. The two strands still settle, but a whole unit apart. Terms that never shrink to zero break the necessary condition from episode twelve oh one. So read the test clause by clause, and check both conditions.

4 · Outlook

Now the cliffhanger: Leibniz says the alternating harmonic series converges. Strip the signs, and the harmonic series diverges, as we proved in episode twelve oh one. So this series converges, but its absolute values do not. That kind of convergence has a name: conditional convergence. Sorting absolute from conditional is the next episode's job. Exam trap: Leibniz applies, so it converges absolutely. False. The test promises convergence, never absolute convergence. And soon this very series gets reordered to change its sum.

Drill one, the factorial series. Pause, and check both conditions. Factorials shrink fast: three terms already pin it down to about 0.0002. Drill two, the powers of ten series. Check it the same way. Each term is at most a fifth of the one before, and they fade to zero. After three terms, the error is at most 0.0004. Full workings for both drills are in the episode notes.

In numerical analysis, projection methods close in the same way, overshooting and undershooting. In probability, inclusion exclusion is an alternating sum, with the same price tag. Today's kit: two conditions, two nested strands, one price tag. One step down, one offset, and the remainder under control.
