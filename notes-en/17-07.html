17-07 Least squares: fitting a straight line to scattered points

Least squares: fitting a straight line to scattered points

1 · Motivation

Today: how to fit one straight line through a cloud of points. In your lab notebook, six readings: the load on a spring and its length. Almost a straight line. Almost. You grab a ruler and nudge it until it feels right. Your partner draws another line. It also looks fine. Two lines, two opinions. Which one is better? Eyeballing settles nothing. We need a number we can make smallest.

Measure each point's vertical gap to the line. A deviation is where the line sits, minus where the point sits. Some point up, some point down. Add them raw and they cancel out. Square each gap: no more cancelling, just one total. Now best has a meaning: the line with the smallest total.

2 · Statements

Given n points (xᵢ, yᵢ) that lie roughly on a line,
determine a line y = ax + b for which the sum of squared deviations
f(a, b) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (axᵢ + b − yᵢ)² is smallest (least squares).
The unknowns are not x and y; they are a and b.
Each choice of a and b is one candidate line through the data.
So f is an ordinary function of two variables, a and b.
Finding the best line is last episode's problem: an unconditional extremum.
Why squares? Absolute values would also stop the cancelling.
But |t| has a corner at zero, with no derivative there.
t² is smooth at zero; its derivative is 2t.
So f is differentiable everywhere, and 17-06's machinery runs.

The course text sets it up: some points that roughly line up, find the straight line whose total of squared gaps is as small as it can be. That is least squares. Here is the twist: the unknowns are the slope and the intercept. Pick a slope and an intercept, and you get one candidate line. So the total is just a function of two variables. And that is exactly last episode's job: an unconditional extremum.

But why squares? Absolute values would stop the cancelling too. Here is the catch: absolute value has a sharp corner at zero. The square is smooth there, with a tangent at every point. So the total is smooth everywhere, and last episode's tools apply.

3 · Method

The plan: at the bottom, both partial derivatives must vanish. First wiggle only the slope, just like the partial derivative drills. Then only the intercept; this time the inner factor is just one. Both are totals of the gaps; one weighs each gap by its x. Set both of them to zero, tidy up, and out comes a linear system: the normal equations. Notice: the data show up only as five numbers. Three points or three thousand, it is still two equations in two unknowns.

Solve it, and the slope comes out as a ratio of sums. And the intercept comes out the same way. Both share a denominator. Danger: could it be zero? Secretly it is a sum of squares, one for every pair of points. So it is positive, as long as the points are not all stacked at one x. Stack every point at one x, and endless lines tie for best. Otherwise there is exactly one solution, no ties.

One candidate. But is it really the bottom? The second derivatives are plain numbers, the same everywhere. And the key combination is positive. So, by last episode's sufficient condition, it is a minimum, and since the problem must have a best line, this is the lowest value. This is last episode's table meeting real data, and the first row lights up. Positive definite means a bowl, ringed by elliptic level curves. And look: it is the same denominator as before. One fact does both jobs.

Time for real numbers: three points. First the five numbers, straight from the table. Plug them in, and the normal equations are small and friendly. Each equation is a line in the plane of slope and intercept; they meet once. Subtract: slope one half, intercept seven sixths. And the closed formulas give the same two numbers. And here is the line, laid over the points. Two small gaps one way, one double gap the other: perfect balance. Weigh them by x, and they still balance: the gradient is zero.

Now take the name literally: draw each square. Add up their areas: one sixth. That is the minimum. Tilt the line away, and watch the squares swell. So the name means exactly what it says: least squares.

A bonus: the line goes straight through the points' centroid. Check it on the numbers: it fits exactly. And it is no coincidence: the second normal equation says exactly this.

Now grab the third point and drag it up. The line follows, and the squares shrink away to nothing. Now the points sit exactly on a line, and the minimum is zero. One reading changed two of the five numbers, and the line moved with them.

4 · Outlook

Back to the notebook: now the six readings get a line no one argues with. Its slope tells how much the spring stretches per unit load. Same recipe for your phone: battery level against minutes of video. The slope reads off how fast it drains. Whenever a model depends linearly on its parameters, these equations come back. Why it worked: squaring kept everything smooth, and last episode's test did the rest.

Gift for later: this is the regression line from statistics class. Every fitting button in statistics software, is solving a bigger cousin of this very minimum. From nudging a ruler by eye, to a minimum you can actually compute.
