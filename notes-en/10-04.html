Arc length and curvature: how sharply the road bends

1 · Motivation

Arc length and curvature, or how sharply a road bends. Your map app draws the walk to the library as a winding curve. But a ruler only knows straight lines; the curve escapes it. Zoom the map in, and every little piece starts to look straight. So we add up the straight pieces and watch the total.

Second question. Two roads, and both turn through a right angle. The campus path takes its time; the garage ramp turns in a blink. The same turn, yet the ramp feels far sharper. Why? Today we answer both: how long a curve is, and how sharply it bends.

First, mark points along the curve, in order, from start to end. Join each point to the next, and you get an inscribed polygonal line. Its total length is just the chords added together. We have met this grammar before, back with the definite integral. Chop, add, take the limit. Only now we chop a curve, not an interval. Refine the cut, and the polygonal line hugs the curve tighter and tighter. Add one more point, and the polygonal line can only get longer. The totals keep climbing. Definition one: if the polygon lengths have a finite limit, the curve is rectifiable. The fineness of the partition is its longest chord. A finite limit means the totals settle down at one number. Then the curve is rectifiable, and that number is its arc length. Careful. A curve can be unbroken and still have no finite length. The wiggles crowd toward the origin, and the totals run away forever.

Theorem ten point one: continuously differentiable coordinates guarantee a finite length. The curve runs once from start to finish, never crossing itself. That means the velocity arrow changes without any jumps. So the length is the speed, added up over the whole trip.

3 · Method

Why an integral? Let's stare at a single chord. Pythagoras hands us the chord's length from its two legs. The mean value theorem trades each leg for a derivative sample. Here's the snag: the two sample points need not coincide. The real work: merge the two samples, at a vanishing price. And continuous derivatives are exactly what make this swap safe. It is the same first order test as in the element method. Once matched, it is a Riemann sum of the speed, and the integral appears.

Shrink the chord into a tangent segment, and meet the arc differential. Pythagoras again, on the differential triangle. We drew this triangle with the differential; now the long side has a name. For a graph, factor out the step along x. For a parametric curve, both legs are measured per tick of t. For a polar curve, rewrite both coordinates through radius and angle. Square and add; the cross terms cancel, and something clean survives. Three ways to describe a curve, one triangle underneath.

First distance: the arch drawn by the valve of a rolling bike wheel. One full arch, as the wheel makes one complete turn. The half angle formula tidies the speed into a single sine. Integrate, and the arch comes out to exactly eight radii. Check it. Two chords through the top come out a little shorter, as they must. Walking across, then up and down, overshoots. Eight radii sits in between.

Second distance: the catenary, the shape of a chain between two hooks. Under the root sits a perfect square, and the root simply vanishes. So the length comes out in closed form. Check: a bit more than the straight run, since the chain starts almost flat.

Back to our two roads. Their tangents turn by the same angle. This angle is the tangent's inclination, the way you're heading. The turned angle alone cannot tell the two roads apart. What differs is how much road each one uses to make the turn. Turning per unit of road: that ratio is the average curvature. Shrink the arc to a point, and you get the curvature there. Angle over length: curvature carries one over length. The dimension check approves. A circle bends the same everywhere: one over its radius. So the ramp, with its tiny radius, has the bigger curvature. Mystery solved. A straight road never turns, so its curvature is zero.

To compute curvature, first read the heading off the velocity arrow. Divide the turning rate by the speed, and the general formula appears. For a graph, only the second derivative survives on top. The second derivative is back, telling how fast the slope changes. Check: at the top of a semicircle, the graph formula agrees. An ellipse bends hardest at its sharp tips and gentlest on its flat sides. With half axes two and one, the curvature ranges from two down to a quarter.

One circle fits best at each point: the osculating circle. It passes through the point and shares the tangent there. It sits on the inner side, bending the same way the curve bends. Its radius is the radius of curvature, its center the center of curvature. It hugs the curve to second order: same tangent, same side, same bend. Ride around the ellipse: the circle shrinks at the tips and swells on the sides.

4 · Outlook

Heading home on the high speed train, the track leaves a straight for a bend. The sideways push you feel is set by speed and curvature together. Bolt the circle straight onto the line, and the curvature jumps at once. The rails have no corner, yet every coffee cup lurches sideways. The fix is a transition curve, where the bending grows gradually. A simple cubic arc does the job. Its curvature starts at zero and ends almost exactly at the bend's value. What passengers feel is whether the curvature is smooth, not just the rails. That is why high speed lines never rush a turn.

Your running app drops a GPS point every few seconds and adds the chords. That's an inscribed polygonal line, so on a winding route it comes up short. The definition borrowed the Riemann sum skeleton; the proof reran the element method test. The differential gave us the triangle, and the second derivative now sits on top of curvature. And the formula self check habit passed curvature too.

In space, curvature gets two companions: torsion, and a frame rolling along the curve. Curvature remains the first invariant of the differential geometry course. And in physics it returns, as the normal part of acceleration. Length is a limit of polygons, and bending is turning per unit of length.
