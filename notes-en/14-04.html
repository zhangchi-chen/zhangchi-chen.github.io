Taylor series: a function's life written in its coefficients

1 · Motivation

Taylor series: a whole function's life, written into its coefficients. A campus card holds just a few numbers, yet it stands for a whole person. A function carries a card too: all of its derivatives at one point. Line those numbers up, and they turn into a power series.

Take the cosine at zero, and read its card. Its derivatives at zero just cycle: one, zero, minus one, zero. The partial sums wrap around the graph, farther out every time. Does every card bring its owner back this faithfully?

Back in chapter six, Taylor's formula already split the function in two. The polynomial part is exactly a partial sum of our series. So everything hangs on the remainder. If the remainder fades away, the card gives the function back.

Here is the definition: the Taylor series of a function at a point. The first demand: derivatives of every order must exist at that point. Each coefficient is a derivative, divided by a factorial. Centered at zero, it gets its own name: the Maclaurin series. Notice what the definition never promises: convergence, or landing on the function.

Theorem fourteen eleven gives the exact condition. It talks about a whole interval around the center. If and only if: each side guarantees the other. At every point inside, the remainders must shrink to zero. The proof takes one line: what the partial sum misses is the remainder. Could every derivative exist, and the remainder still refuse to fade?

The course text's first example says yes, such a function really exists. Meet the function from that example, the star of today's story. Away from zero it is always positive; at zero we simply set it to zero. Zoom in near zero, and the graph presses closer to the axis every time. At one tenth, the value has forty three zeros after the decimal point. Read its card at zero: every derivative is zero. Why? The exponential decay crushes any power that tries to blow up. So its Taylor series is nothing but zeros, and it converges everywhere. A perfectly well behaved series, about the wrong function. The remainder is the whole function, every single time; it never fades. Her card is completely blank, and yet there she is, sitting in the room. Having every derivative is not enough; the fading remainder decides.

3 · Method

Now for the happy cases, armed with the Lagrange remainder from chapter six. For the exponential, each derivative is itself. So the remainder sits under a fraction with a factorial below. At three, the fraction first climbs, and then the factorial takes over. Factorial outruns exponential, the speed ladder from chapter twelve. So the exponential equals its Maclaurin series at every real number.

For the sine, every derivative stays between minus one and one. One line does it: the same factorial fraction, without the extra factor. The cosine comes for free: differentiate the sine series term by term. The first three rows hold for every real number. The logarithm row arrives from the ladder of the previous episode. Put in one, and the row spells out the logarithm of two. The constant from chapter twelve now sits in the table, in public.

Next row: the binomial series, as the course text states it. Taming this remainder is messy, so the course text takes a smarter road. Neighbouring coefficients have a ratio tending to one, so the radius is one. Frame one: give the sum function a name, without knowing it yet. Frame two: that product splits into two columns. Add the two columns: the parts with n cancel, and only alpha remains. Frame three: out comes a differential equation. Frame four: divide by the hoped for answer, and call it F. Differentiate it: inside sits exactly our equation, so the derivative vanishes. So F stays at one, and the sum is exactly our function. The trick: recognize the sum function without adding anything up. Termwise differentiation from the last two chapters, cashed in as a proof.

4 · Outlook

From here the table grows like a group chat, every reply quoting an older message. Reply one: flip the sign of the variable in the geometric series. Reply two: feed in a negative square instead. Integrate term by term, and the arctangent series lands. It holds up to both ends, and at one it hands us a quarter of pi. Reply three quotes the binomial row: alpha at minus one half, then a sign flip. Reply four: swap the variable for its square. Integrate term by term once more, and the arcsine series arrives. Every entry wears its tag: whom it quoted, and what it changed. The family table trick from chapter six: never differentiate from scratch.

One cell stays conspicuously blank: the endpoints of the binomial row. The course text lists three cases by alpha, and sends the proof to another book. We refuse to leave it there: the next episode builds the whole proof. Its tools are ready: Raabe, Leibniz, and Abel's endpoint continuity.

From the alternating row, the error shrinks only like one over the number of terms. For four decimals, the bound asks for about ten thousand terms. Subtract two logarithm rows, and every even power cancels. Pick one third, and the inside of the logarithm becomes exactly two. Four terms are enough, already past four decimals. Ten thousand terms against four: that is what a good substitution buys.

Over the complex numbers, being differentiable on a whole disc forces the series back home. Our blank card function fails that test: along the imaginary axis it explodes. That contrast is the doorway to complex analysis, and episode six stands beside it. The coefficients tell the whole life story only when the remainder fades.
