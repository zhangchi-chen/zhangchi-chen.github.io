04-08 Composition: one bad point, two patches

Composition: one bad point, two patches

1 · Motivation

Today's question: when can a limit pass through g of f of x? One bad point of the outer function can spread into many bad values of x. A homework limit: this expression as x tends to 0.

Set u equal to x times sine of 1 over x. Then the expression is just sine u over u. Since sine u over u tends to 1 as u tends to 0, the answer is 1? Lin's phone app says 1. Zhou's phone, on the newer version, refuses: undefined arbitrarily close to 0. Same app, two versions, two answers. Which one has the bug?

Here is the graph of u equals x times sine of 1 over x, near 0. Since the absolute value of u is at most the absolute value of x, the graph is squeezed to 0. But u is exactly 0 at every x equal to 1 over n pi. These zeros pile up at 0: every interval around 0 holds infinitely many. At each of them, sine u over u becomes 0 over 0: undefined. One bad point of sine u over u became a whole patch of bad values of x.

Think of a call chain: x goes into f, then into g. g crashes on exactly one input: u equal to 0. And f keeps sending exactly that input, again and again. Lin's version quietly patched g: it sets g of 0 equal to 1. A limit at 0 needs values at every point near 0, except 0 itself. Here that fails, so strictly the limit is not even defined. Not both right: Lin computed the patched function. The patch is called a continuous extension; without it the limit does not exist.

To see the mechanism, switch to a smaller example: the inner function is the constant 0. Note its ending differs from the opening example. Outer function: g of u equals 1 whenever u is not 0. But at the single point 0, g of 0 equals 2. f tends to 0 and g of u tends to 1 as u tends to 0, so substituting predicts 1. Unexpectedly the composite is 2 everywhere: the limit exists and equals 2. Unlike the opening example, where no limit existed, here it merely differs from the substituted 1. Moral: when the inner function keeps landing on u 0, the composite sees the value of g at u 0, not its limit.

2 · Statements

The textbook offers two patches for this bug.
Patch A is Theorem 3.8, on composite limits.
Its first two clauses: f tends to u₀, and g tends to A.
The limit of g looks only near u₀, never at u₀ itself.
A hollow (punctured) neighbourhood: an interval around x₀, with x₀ removed.
Third clause: on some hollow neighbourhood of x₀, f(x) ≠ u₀.
That is Patch A, block the input: f never sends u₀ there.
Conclusion: lim g(f(x)) = A; g(u₀) plays no role, may not even exist.
Patch B starts from Theorem 4.5: continuity survives composition.
Here the limit walks straight inside g.
The note after §4.2 Example 1 stretches this further.
The inner function only needs a limit a at x₀.
f(x₀) may differ from a, or not exist: a removable discontinuity.
What matters is the outer g: continuous at u = a.
Then lim g(f(x)) = g(lim f(x)) = g(a).
Patch B makes g robust: f may hit a as often as it likes.

Columns: is the outer continuous at u₀? That is Patch B.

3 · Method

Patch A's proof is left as Exercise 3.2, number 10; here is its skeleton. Start with any epsilon greater than 0 for the output. Link 1, from the limit of g: if u is within delta 1 of u 0 but not equal to it, then g of u is within epsilon of A. Link 2, from the limit of f: some delta 2, at most delta, forces f of x to be within delta 1 of u 0. But link 1 needs f of x to differ from u 0, not just to be close to it. Link 3, the third hypothesis, supplies exactly that f of x is not u 0. Chain closed: whenever x is within delta 2 of x 0 but not equal to it, g of f of x is within epsilon of A. Without link 3, the constant function 0 slips through the gap and breaks the chain.

Only A: take the inner function f of x equal to x near 0. Outer: the same jumpy g, with g of 0 equal to 2. f avoids 0 away from x equal to 0, so A holds; g jumps, so B fails. The composite is 1 away from 0: the limit is 1, the limit of g, not g of 0.

Only B: the inner function is our friend x times sine of 1 over x, outer g of u equals u squared, continuous: A fails, B holds. The composite, x squared times sine squared of 1 over x, is at most x squared, so it tends to 0, which is g of 0.

Neither: keep the inner function x times sine of 1 over x, and take g of u equal to 1 for u not 0, but g of 0 equal to 0. g has limit 1 at 0, so 0 is a removable bad point. The composite is 0 at every x equal to 1 over n pi, and 1 everywhere else. Two sequences tending to 0 give values 0 and 1: no limit. So A and B are independent: neither implies the other.

How do you detect an inner function that keeps hitting u 0? Recycle the sequence tool of episode zero three zero four as a bug detector. Hunt a sequence x n tending to x 0, never equal to x 0, with f of x n equal to u 0. Here x n equals 1 over n pi, which tends to 0, and f of x n equals sine n pi over n pi, which is 0. One such sequence kills Patch A; only Patch B can save you. If no such sequence exists, Patch A holds.

Before any substitution in a limit, ask two questions out loud. One: can the inner function keep attaining its limit value? Two: is the outer function continuous at that value? On the opening example, question one gets a yes. Question two: sine u over u is undefined at 0. Bug cell. Patch it with g of 0 equal to 1, as Lin's phone did: B holds, and the limit is 1. Second test: the square root of 2 minus sine x over x, as x tends to 0. The inner function, 2 minus sine x over x, tends to 1; the outer square root is continuous at 1. Patch B applies, so the limit is the square root of 1, which is 1.

4 · Outlook

Use one: the natural log of 1 plus x, divided by x, as x tends to 0. Rewrite it as the natural log of 1 plus x, raised to the power 1 over x. The inner function tends to e and is undefined at 0; natural log is continuous at e: Patch B, limit 1. Use two: e to the x minus 1, divided by x, as x tends to 0. Substitute u equal to e to the x minus 1, so x equals the natural log of 1 plus u. The fraction becomes u divided by the natural log of 1 plus u, whose limit at 0 is 1. Here g is undefined at 0, but e to the x minus 1 is not 0 when x is not 0: Patch A.

A preview we owe to episode zero nine zero two: the same question for integrability. There, an integrable inner function plus a continuous outer function makes g of f of x integrable. Its proof runs on uniform continuity, from episode zero four zero five. On a closed interval, Cantor's theorem guarantees it, as episode zero four zero six showed. Outer: that same g; inner: Riemann's function R. Both are integrable. Between 0 and 1, R is positive at rationals and 0 at irrationals. So g of R of x is 1 at every rational and 0 at every irrational. Dirichlet's function is not integrable: the bad point spread everywhere. Slogan: outer continuity is the universal patch.

Programmers know this anatomy as a call chain bug. One level up the stack, a caller passes exactly the input that crashes yours. Patch A, a local fix: never pass the bad input. Patch B, a robust wrapper: make the callee tolerate it. Same two strategies, in limits and in code.
