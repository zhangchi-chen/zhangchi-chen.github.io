08-06 The universal substitution: the fallback card when nothing else fires

The universal substitution: the fallback card when nothing else fires

1 · Motivation

Some integrals shrug off every trick you know. Here is one, straight from tonight's problem set. First try, glue a differential. Nothing lines up. Second try, split the fraction. One piece still looks stuck. The partial fraction pipeline only takes rational functions. This is not one. What we want is a converter. Trigonometry goes in, a rational function comes out.

Think of the power bank buried at the bottom of your backpack. It always rescues you, and it is never light. The half angle substitution, also called universal, is that power bank. It always works. Every trigonometric rational function can be converted. The price is weight. What comes out can be a bulky fraction. So we learn to carry it, and learn when to leave it at home.

2 · Statements

Here is the statement, in the course text's words.
One new variable: t = tan(x/2), the tangent of half the angle.
The line from (−1, 0) through P meets the y-axis at height t.
That corner angle is x/2, half the central angle.
P's height, sin x, becomes 2t/(1 + t²): a fraction in t.
Its horizontal position, cos x, becomes (1 − t²)/(1 + t²).
Spot check: t = ½ lands on (3/5, 4/5), right on the circle.
And the differential: dx = 2/(1 + t²) dt.
Each x in (−π, π) has exactly one t, and back again.
Swap all three, and R(sin x, cos x) dx becomes rational in t.
Why? Combining fractions in t only ever gives fractions in t.
That is exactly the input the partial-fraction pipeline takes.
It promises a route every time, not a short one.

Here is the statement, exactly as the course text puts it. One new variable, the tangent of half the angle. From the circle's leftmost point, aim through the moving point; you hit height t. That angle on the left is exactly half the central angle. The height of the moving point becomes a plain fraction in t. Its sideways position is a fraction in t as well. Quick check. One half lands on three fifths and four fifths, right on the circle.

Even the differential becomes a fraction in t. From negative pi to pi, each angle matches exactly one t. Swap all three pieces, and the whole integrand turns rational in t. Why? Adding, multiplying, or dividing such fractions just gives another one. And that is exactly what the partial fraction pipeline was built to take. It promises a route every time. It never promises a short one.

3 · Method

Where do those fractions come from? Nothing here is pulled from a hat. Use the double angle rule, and slip a hidden one underneath. Divide top and bottom by the same square, and t appears everywhere. Careful. That division only works where t itself is defined. The little triangle says it at a glance. Its legs are one and t. Cosine takes the same two moves, and comes out just as clean. Both share one denominator, the square of the hypotenuse. For the differential, glue it the way we learned, then flip the fraction. Nothing new was invented. Every line used a tool you already own.

Back to tonight's integral. Let the converter run. Drop in the three fractions, one slot at a time. Simplify, and all the clutter cancels, leaving something tiny. Heavy going in, light coming out. This time, at least. Integrate term by term. One line does it. Put the half angle tangent back, and the answer is in x. Don't trust it yet. Differentiate, and see whether we get back home. One line of chain rule, and the original integrand walks back in. The picture agrees. At a quarter turn the slope is 2, exactly the integrand's value.

The course text adds a caution, and it matters. Take a similar integral, and race two routes against each other. Like the short walk to class you only discover after a whole semester of detours. The half angle route turns the bottom into a degree four polynomial. The tangent route. Divide through, glue twice, and you are done. Same finish line. A very different commute. That is the caution, made visible. It always works, but it is not always the easy way.

Why did the tangent route work? Flip both signs and watch. The integrand repeats every half turn, exactly like the tangent. Moving half a turn flips both signs, and the tangent stays exactly the same. When the integrand ignores a double sign flip, try the tangent first.

But sometimes there is simply no shortcut. Here sine and cosine are mixed, and nothing is squared. Glue? The top is not the derivative of the bottom. The tangent route? Half a turn later the bottom is different. Partial fractions directly? It is not a rational function of x. Every cheaper route has stalled. Time to reach for the power bank. Under the half angle swap, the bottom collapses into something tidy. Multiply by the differential, and all that is left is one logarithm. Where the shortcuts stall, the half angle swap still gets you there.

4 · Outlook

Same idea, new territory. This time a root stands in the way. Here is what the course text says about this kind of root. Why the condition? Without it, the fraction is just a constant, and t is frozen. When c is zero, plain roots of a linear expression fall under it too. An example. Call the whole root t. Solve back, and the old variable becomes a rational function of t. The differential is rational in t as well. Before, a root. After, a purely rational integrand. The root has vanished. Hand it to partial fractions, and it splits into three simple pieces. Integrate, and you get a logarithm minus twice an arctangent. One exercise, and the whole chapter lights up along the way.

Step back. Chapter eight now has five stations. The first substitution, the second substitution, and integration by parts. Then partial fractions, and the half angle substitution that feeds them. Everything made here lands in the partial fraction episode. Together they close the rational world. And the differential step leaned on gluing, once again. The honest boundary. For the bell shaped curve, no station yields an elementary answer. Yet an antiderivative exists, as the first episode showed. It just has no elementary formula.

Later, with limits over a full period, symmetry often wins outright. Why do tables of integrals look finite? Whole families of integrands fold into one rational core. Beyond lie integrals with no elementary answer at all. Elliptic integrals live out there. The definite integral chapters reach them by other means. Carry the power bank. Just look for the short walk first.
