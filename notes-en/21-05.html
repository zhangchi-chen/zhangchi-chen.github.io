21-05 Green's formula: one lap around the boundary sees the whole interior

Green's formula: one lap around the boundary sees the whole interior

1 · Motivation

Green's formula: walk once around the edge, and you see everything inside. Back in episode 3 of chapter 10, we met an area formula that walks around a curve. But the proof only covered curves built from an upper graph and a lower graph. A bean shaped loop breaks that split: one vertical line hits it four times. So that episode ended with a promise to finish the proof later.

You only walk the edge. How could that tell you the inside? Picture yourself taking one lap around the campus lake. Keep the water on your left, the whole way round. A breeze blows across the lake and nudges you at every step. Count only the push along your direction, and keep a running total. Today's surprise: that one lap total equals a sum over the whole lake. And the old promise from chapter 10 is finally kept.

2 · Statements

Positive direction: walking along the boundary, D always lies on your left.
The opposite direction is the negative direction, written −L.
On an outer boundary, positive means counterclockwise.
Around a hole, keeping D on the left forces clockwise.
Theorem 21.11: P, Q have continuous first-order partial derivatives on closed D.
Left side lives on the inside; right side lives on the edge.
L may have corners: finitely many piecewise smooth pieces are fine.
Memory form: a 2×2 determinant, derivatives on top, P and Q below.
Take a concrete breeze: P = −y³, Q = x³, over an oval lake.
The integrand ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y works out to 3x² + 3y².
Paint the lake with it: bright where the breeze turns hard.
Sum the paint over the whole lake: the double integral.
The paint totals 15π/2, about 23.56.
Now walk one positive lap, adding the push along your path.
The lap total is 15π/2 too, to the last digit.
The boundary reads the interior.

The positive direction: as you walk the boundary, the region stays on your left. Walk the other way and you get the negative direction, minus L. On the outer edge, that rule means counterclockwise. But around a hole, keeping the region on your left means going clockwise.

The hypothesis: both functions have continuous first partial derivatives on the closed region. The left side lives inside the region; the right side lives on its edge. The boundary may have corners; a few smooth pieces glued together are fine. To remember it: derivatives on top, the two functions below, then cross-multiply.

Let's take a concrete breeze, swirling around an oval lake. Inside, the integrand measures how hard the breeze turns at each spot. Paint the lake by that amount: brighter where the breeze turns harder. Now add up all the paint on the lake, column by column. In total: fifteen pi over two, about 23.56. Now forget the inside: walk one lap and add the push along the way. And the lap lands on exactly the same number. The slogan: the boundary reads the interior.

3 · Method

Why does it work? Begin with a region you can slice either way. Split the formula in two, and first prove the half with Q. Integrate across first: one horizontal strip at a time. Along the strip, the derivative undoes itself: right end minus left end. It's the Newton Leibniz formula from chapter 9, one floor up. Walk the boundary the positive way: up the right side, down the left. So the loop counts the right end as plus, the left end as minus. Same two terms on both sides, so the first half holds. Slice the other way, and the half with P comes out the same way. Where does the minus come from? Along the top, positive means right to left. Add the two halves, and there is Green's formula.

For a messier region, cut it into pieces of that friendly kind. Each piece obeys Green's formula, walked around its own boundary. That cut is walked twice, in opposite directions, like two classmates passing in a corridor. Their footsteps cancel out, and only the outer boundary is left. The most general region needs more tools; the course text says so too.

Now put an island in the lake. The water has a hole. Add one cut from shore to island, and the hole is opened up. Walk it with the water on your left: shore counterclockwise, island clockwise. The cut cancels again, so the total is the outer lap minus the inner lap.

Here is the course text's favorite test: a breeze swirling around one point. Away from the origin, the two cross derivatives agree exactly. So on any loop that leaves the origin outside, Green gives zero. But on a circle around the origin, everything collapses to the angle itself. One lap gives two pi, not zero. The hole makes itself heard. Apply Green to the whole disk and you'd get zero. That's the trap. At the origin the two functions aren't even defined, so the hypothesis fails. Cut out a tiny circle: any simple loop around the origin, walked positively, gives two pi.

Now pick the simplest swirl of all. The integrand is a flat two everywhere: perfectly even paint. Paint two per unit area, and the lap reads off twice the area. Back in chapter 10, this was proved only for curves with an upper and a lower graph. Now Green's formula proves it for every region's edge, corners and dents included. The promise from chapter 10 is kept.

4 · Outlook

First job: the area between a tilted parabola and the horizontal axis. Along the axis nothing happens: that stretch adds zero. On the arc, walking back from right to left, the integrand shrinks to a single root. Half the lap integral: the area is a squared over six. Check it the old way: again a squared over six.

Second job: an integral along a quarter circle, computed without walking it. Close the loop along the two axes; both straight pieces add nothing. But this loop runs clockwise, region on the right: the negative direction. So the answer is minus a quarter disk's area; the minus is the walk's direction. One theorem, two jobs: read the area from the edge, or the edge from the area.

A map app can measure the area you outline, using only the corners. Each edge adds one cross product of its ends; halve the total: the shoelace formula. A planimeter does it with gears: trace the edge once, read off the area.

The right side is chapter 20's second type line integral, now with a shortcut. Next episode asks a sharp question: when does every loop give zero? Later, Gauss lifts this idea to solids, and Stokes gathers the whole family. In a flowing fluid, that integrand is the vorticity: how hard the water spins. One lap around the boundary, and you've seen the whole interior.
