21-03 Polar coordinates and change of variables: the stretch rate of an area element

Polar coordinates and change of variables: the stretch rate of an area element

1 · Motivation

Polar coordinates, and changing variables. Our task: integrate a round bump over a whole disk. In the usual coordinates, the bounds become square roots. The inner step needs an antiderivative no elementary formula can give. The same wall as last time, and swapping the order does not help.

In the language of distance and angle, a disk is easy. In these terms the disk is just a plain rectangle. And the integrand only cares about the distance. The real question: what becomes of the tiny patch of area? Think of the two coordinate systems as two currencies. And the exchange rate depends on where you stand. We will earn it twice, once with a picture, once with algebra.

2 · Statements

The course text's polar transform:
Turn to angle θ, then walk out a distance r along the ray.
r runs from 0 outward, while θ sweeps one full turn.
Fix r and you get a circle; fix θ and you get a ray.
And the text already announces the answer: J = r.
Theorem 21.14 puts that factor to work:
First, the region D is redrawn as Δ in the rθ plane.
Second, the integrand is rewritten with x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ.
Third, the step people forget: multiply by r.
Three chores: redraw the region, rewrite the integrand, add the r.

The polar transform, from the course text. Turn to the angle, then walk out the distance. The distance starts at zero; the angle sweeps a full turn. Hold the distance: a circle. Hold the angle: a ray. And the text already tells us: the factor is the distance itself.

Theorem twenty-one fourteen puts it to work. First, the region is redrawn in the plane of distance and angle. Second, rewrite the integrand in the new coordinates. Third, the step people forget: one more factor of distance. Three chores: redraw the region, rewrite the function, add the factor.

3 · Method

Cut the plane with circles and rays, and you get a polar grid. Pick one cell, between two nearby circles and two nearby rays. Zoom in, like on a photo, until the curves look straight.

Its straight sides are one small step of distance. Two curved sides, arcs cut out by that small angle. Arc equals radius times angle, so distance sneaks in. So the cell is almost a rectangle, one side scaled by distance. The area element: distance times both small steps. What we dropped is a sliver that vanishes faster than the cell.

Keep both small steps fixed, and push the cell outward. The straight sides stay the same, while the arcs keep growing. The arc grows in step with the distance. That is the factor. The farther out you shop, the more area each step buys. That is the exchange rate, and it rises with distance.

Now let us get the same factor by pure algebra. Put the four partial derivatives into a small determinant. Expand it; the squares add to one, and the distance is left. Algebra and geometry agree on the same factor.

Watch the map itself, starting from tiny rectangles. They become curved cells, their area multiplied by the factor. The rate depends on the point; near the origin, cells shrink.

Using the theorem starts with reading the bounds off a sketch. Origin outside: each ray enters the region, then leaves it. Origin inside: every ray starts at the origin and runs to the edge. Origin on the edge: the angle no longer makes a full turn.

Back to the opening disk. The origin is inside, so the bounds are easy. The extra factor is exactly what makes the antiderivative elementary. Multiply by a full turn, and the answer drops out. Remember this number; a later episode of this chapter harvests it.

When should you switch to polar coordinates? Look for two signs. Sign one: the region is round, a disk, a ring, or a slice of one. Sign two: the integrand only cares about distance from the origin. When both show up together, it is a gift.

Polar is just one map among many. Here is the general theorem. First, the map is one to one: no two points land on the same spot. Second, the map is smooth: its first partial derivatives are continuous. Third, the determinant never vanishes, so no cell is crushed flat. Then its absolute value is the local exchange rate for area. Chapter eighteen called it an exchange rate; today it pays rent. With one variable, it is the old substitution rule, rescaling length.

A new example: an awkward exponent over a small triangle. The exponent begs for the difference and the sum as new variables. Solve back for the old variables, and the factor is one half. Each edge of the triangle becomes an edge of a new triangle. Integrate across first, and each slice comes out clean. Finish the last integral, and the answer is about 0.59. Quick check: the integrand is trapped between two bounds, and the answer fits.

An honest footnote: the polar map breaks hypothesis one. The entire left edge of the rectangle collapses into one point. And the factor is zero right there, so hypothesis three fails too. The fix: cut away thin strips of width epsilon, then let epsilon shrink. Both sides settle down in the limit: a real limit, not hand-waving.

Last, the classic bug: forgetting the extra factor. Test it on a disk, with the function equal to one everywhere. Without the factor you get the length around the circle! With the factor back, the school formula appears. Even the units warn you: an area must be a length squared. That is the habit from chapter ten: check a school formula first.

4 · Outlook

First use: generalized polar coordinates measure an ellipse. The factor picks up both semi-axes, and the area follows. Second use: densities that look the same in every direction.

Next comes height, with cylindrical and spherical coordinates. In statistics, changing variables in a density pays the same rate. In one sentence: little areas stretch, and the determinant says how much. In polar coordinates, that rate is the distance itself. Never drop it.
