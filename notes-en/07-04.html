07-04 Upper and lower limits: a sequence's two shadow lines

Upper and lower limits: a sequence's two shadow lines

1 · Motivation

Upper and lower limits: the two shadow lines of a sequence. After a whole season of ups and downs, the river wall keeps two marks. Not one freak flood, but the height the water kept returning to.

Now a sequence straight from the homework. Its dots hop up and down, and never stop. The even terms creep up toward one. The odd terms slide down toward negative one. So is the limit one, or negative one? Neither. Two subsequences disagree, so there is no limit. But throwing both answers away wastes real information.

First, draw a line above every single dot. Lower it, as long as only finitely many dots poke above. Push it below one, and infinitely many dots break through. So the ceiling comes to rest at one: the upper shadow line. Mirror it from below, and the lower shadow rests at negative one. Two shadow lines: the sequence's own pair of water marks. Their proper names are the upper limit and the lower limit.

2 · Statements

The building block is the cluster point.
Definition 1: every neighbourhood of a holds infinitely many terms.
Shrink the neighbourhood of 1: still infinitely many dots inside.
So 1 is a cluster point, and so is −1.
But a small neighbourhood of 0 catches no dots at all.
Repeated values count as repeated terms.
sin(nπ/4) returns to 1 forever, so 1 is a cluster point.
Equivalently, cluster points are limits of convergent subsequences.
Must every bounded sequence have a cluster point?
Theorem 7.4: yes, and a largest and a smallest one exist.
Definition 2: the largest is the upper limit, the smallest the lower.
Here the upper limit is 1 and the lower limit is −1.
sin(nπ/4) has five cluster points: its five repeating values.
The largest is 1, the smallest −1: the course text's Example 1.
Theorems 7.5 and 7.6: liminf ≤ limsup, equal exactly when convergent.
When both lines meet at A, the sequence converges to A.

Everything is built from one idea: the cluster point. Definition one: every neighbourhood holds infinitely many terms. Shrink the neighbourhood around one, and infinitely many dots stay inside. So one is a cluster point, and so is negative one. But a small neighbourhood of zero catches no dots at all. A value that repeats counts as many separate terms. This sine sequence keeps returning to one, so one is a cluster point. Put another way, cluster points are exactly the limits of convergent subsequences.

Must every bounded sequence have a cluster point? Theorem seven point four: yes, and a largest and a smallest one exist. Definition two: the largest cluster point is the upper limit, the smallest the lower. For our sequence, the upper limit is one and the lower is negative one. The sine sequence has five cluster points, its five repeating values. The largest is one, the smallest negative one, as in the course text's first example. Two theorems: the lower limit never exceeds the upper, and equality means convergence. When the two lines meet at one height, the sequence converges there.

3 · Method

But why must a largest cluster point exist at all? Bolzano and Weierstrass: every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence. So cluster points exist: chapter two's compactness theorem pays off. The cluster points sit inside a bounded set, so a supremum and an infimum exist. And that supremum is itself a cluster point. Any neighbourhood of it holds a cluster point, and so infinitely many terms. That supremum and that infimum are exactly the upper and lower limits. The course text takes another road: cut the range in half. Keep the upper half whenever it still holds infinitely many terms. The nested halves close in on the largest cluster point.

There are three equivalent ways to state the upper limit, and exams want all three. Theorem seven point seven says it with epsilon and N. First clause: from some point on, every dot stays below the raised line. Second clause: some subsequence stays above the lowered line. Eventually capped from above, yet touched again and again. Theorem seven point seven prime says it by counting. Above any level higher than the upper limit, only finitely many terms. Above any level lower than the upper limit, infinitely many terms. The third form takes the limit of the suprema of the tails. Drop the early terms and take the supremum of what is left. As the tail shrinks, its supremum can only step down. And it steps down onto the upper limit, one.

Now translate between the forms in both directions; exams love this. Raise the line by epsilon: eventually below means only finitely many above. Lower the line by epsilon: a subsequence above means infinitely many above. For the tail suprema, the first clause caps them and the second props them up. Check every row on our first sequence: all three land on one. On the sine sequence the rows agree too: one and negative one. Careful: the upper limit need not be a term; no dot here ever equals one.

Use theorem seven point six as a detector: do the two lines meet? If both equal the same number, the upper clause caps the tail just above it. The mirror clause for the lower limit props the tail from below. Trapped in a band of any width around it: that is convergence. With two separate cluster points, the lines never meet. Notice: this proves convergence without ever naming the limit first.

4 · Outlook

A limit may fail to exist, but for a bounded sequence the upper limit always exists. Any line above it eventually caps the tail; no line below it ever does. First use: much later, the reach of a power series is computed from exactly this. Second use: Stolz's theorem is proved by pinching exactly these two lines. Theorem seven point eight: eventual order passes to the upper limits. Leave it in plain sight; that proof leans on it.

Later on, this pair becomes the standard way to define generalized limits. For sequences of sets, of functions, even of random events. And the Cauchy Hadamard formula makes the upper limit the exact gauge of a power series. Back at the river: two marks drying into one line means the level has settled. Two shadow lines, three equivalent forms, and one way to detect convergence.
