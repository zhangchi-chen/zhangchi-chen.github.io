09-04 The variable-limit integral: when the upper bound moves, the integral becomes a function

The variable-limit integral: when the upper bound moves, the integral becomes a function

1 · Motivation

When the upper limit starts to move, the integral turns into a function. A homework problem: differentiate this integral. Your homework answer takes one quick line. The answer key has an extra integral in it. Both seem to rest on the theorem from the last episode. One theorem, two different answers. So which one is right?

To settle this, picture a download progress bar. The edge keeps moving, and the filled part is what you read off. Now let the download speed rise and fall over time. Everything downloaded so far is the area under the speed graph. The right edge of that area is a wall we can drag. Every position of the wall gives one number. That is a function.

2 · Statements

That is 09-03's machine; this episode drives it.

Such an integral is called a definite integral with variable upper limit. Likewise, integrate from x to the right end. Together they are called the variable limit integrals. In both formulas, the variable of integration must not be called x, otherwise it gets mixed up with the x sitting in the limit.

Read it slowly. The letter x marks where the wall stands. Meanwhile t runs along the floor, from the left end to the wall. Once the sweep is over, t is used up. Only the wall position is left. Drag the wall, and the integral draws its own graph.

Theorem nine point nine: if f is integrable on the interval, then this integral is a continuous function. Why? Push the wall forward by a tiny step. The new sliver is exactly the change. An integrable function is bounded, so the sliver fits inside a short box. So a small push can only cause a small change. That is continuity.

It survives even when f jumps. The integral still has no gaps, but it bends sharply at the jump. At that point the left and right derivatives exist, but they disagree.

Theorem nine point ten: if f is continuous on the interval, then the integral is differentiable everywhere, and its derivative is f. When f is continuous, the thin sliver is nearly a rectangle. Its height is f at the wall, and the rate becomes that height. The last episode built this machine. This one takes it for a drive.

3 · Method

First drive: what if the wall position is itself a function? That area is simply the old integral, evaluated at the moving wall. A composite function, so the chain rule from chapter five takes over. The outer derivative is the height at the wall. Then multiply by how fast the wall is moving. Height times speed: that is how fast the area is growing.

Now let the left end move as well. Cut it at the fixed left end, using additivity over intervals. The left piece is taken away, so its term comes with a minus sign. Apply the chain rule twice, once for each wall. Two moving walls give two contributions, with opposite signs.

A test drive with a simple integrand, where we can check the answer by hand. The two wall terms leave one over x. Compute it directly, and the derivative is again one over x. They agree.

Now back to the warning hidden in the definition. Picture two roommates with the same name sharing one mailbox. So mail keeps going to the wrong roommate. Same thing here: one letter doing two jobs at once. The fix is easy: give the sweeping variable its own name. The letter t is a dummy variable. Rename it, and nothing changes. But x is free. Move it, and the value moves with it.

Back to the homework. Here x is hiding inside the integrand. Your homework froze that x as if it were a constant. But it moves with the wall, so pull it out of the integral first. Now the product rule gives two terms. So the answer key was right.

Now the exam favourite, where x hides even deeper. Freeze x, and the answer collapses to zero, which is clearly wrong. Split it first. By linearity, x comes out of the first piece. Product rule on the first piece, the old theorem on the second. The two matching terms collide and cancel out. Once more, and f itself comes back.

Next, a limit built from one of these functions. The area explodes while the exponent shrinks. A tug of war. Take logs: infinity over infinity. L'Hôpital's rule from chapter six, with our theorem on top. Almost all of that area is crammed into the last thin strip by the wall. One more pass, and the ratio settles at one. The logarithm tends to one, so the limit itself is e.

Last drive: every antiderivative of f is this integral, shifted up or down. It starts at zero, so the shift is F at the left end. So F is the integral plus F at the left end. The family from chapter eight now has a member we can write down.

4 · Outlook

First use: that exam favourite is secretly a position function. On the 800 metre test you start from rest, pushed by an acceleration f. Zero position, zero speed, acceleration f: exactly that run.

Second use: a nonnegative density, and the area to the left of the wall. This function collects all the probability up to x. The far left end stays informal for now. And its derivative is the density: the very same pattern. The probability course calls this the distribution function.

Third use: chapter eleven defines infinite integrals as limits of these functions. Next episode, each formula with limits can be checked by differentiating in the upper limit. Further down the road, the integrand may depend on a second variable as well. Swapping the derivative with the integral is not automatic. Uniform convergence comes in. That story belongs to a later chapter on integrals with parameters. The upper limit moved, and the integral came alive as a function. Walls can move, the dummy stays a dummy, and you split before you differentiate.
