18-03 The Jacobian determinant: the exchange rate of a coordinate change

The Jacobian determinant: the exchange rate of a coordinate change

1 · Motivation

Meet the Jacobian determinant, the exchange rate of a coordinate change. Homework: two equations, four letters, and one derivative to find. But which two of the letters are secretly functions? Two equations can pin down two letters, leaving two free. Four letters give six ways to choose the pair, six doors out of the room. A single determinant will tell us which doors open.

Recall the one equation case: a nonzero partial in y means we can solve. Where that partial vanishes, the curve stands vertical and solving breaks down. With two equations, who takes over that partial's job?

Polar coordinates are easy one way, but at the origin the angle has no single value. Change your money and change it back, and you should end up exactly where you began. Changing coordinates and changing back ought to keep that same promise.

Warm up with a linear system: each equation is a straight line. The lines cross at one point, and the determinant is not zero. Parallel lines give a zero determinant: no solution, or far too many. When the equations bend, partial derivatives step in for the coefficients.

3 · Method

Back to the homework, the course text's Example 1, at its starting point. Both equations hold at that point, so the initial conditions are met. First, all eight partial derivatives at the point, in one table. Pick any two columns, and they give that door's Jacobian. Open them one by one: seven, six, zero, five, seven, six. Only one door reads zero, and that door stays shut. Let us walk through one door and find two of its partial derivatives. Differentiate both equations, and out comes a linear system: our warm up again. The denominator is seven, giving five sevenths and minus six sevenths. And that seven is no accident: it is exactly this door's Jacobian. No need to memorise the quotients: differentiate, then solve a small linear system.

Here is Theorem 18.5, the inverse function group theorem. A coordinate transformation runs forward; can we run it backwards? The trick: move everything to one side, and inversion becomes an implicit system. The two minus signs cancel, leaving the transformation's own Jacobian. So a nonzero Jacobian guarantees a local inverse function group.

And the two Jacobians are reciprocal: multiplied together, they give exactly one. Just like exchange rates: seven one way, one seventh back, and the product is one. Underneath sits linear algebra: inverse matrices have reciprocal determinants. Its single variable ancestor: the derivative of an inverse function is a reciprocal. Read it backwards: a product equal to one can never contain a zero factor.

Now the classic: polar coordinates, and their Jacobian. And it collapses to something wonderfully short: just r. Here is what it means: a tiny cell gets stretched, more the farther out it sits. At the origin it dies: every angle lands on the same point. The way back has Jacobian one over r, so the rates multiply to one. Spherical coordinates give a similar factor, and it vanishes along the vertical axis. Careful: the Jacobian is nonzero away from the origin, and yet the angle wraps around. One full turn lands on the same point, so no inverse works on the whole plane. The theorem is local: it promises an inverse near each point, never everywhere at once.

4 · Outlook

Course text Example 5: a slanted coordinate change, with nonzero Jacobian. In the new coordinates, the vibrating string equation collapses to almost nothing. The solutions are two waves running in opposite directions, bought by one change of coordinates. That Jacobian is the receipt of the exchange: nonzero, so we can always change back.

Everything today was the implicit function theorem, one size up. A debt we leave behind: three Jacobians will build the tangent of a curve cut by two surfaces. Another debt: in double integrals, this factor r will measure how areas stretch. Looking ahead, the size of the Jacobian becomes the local area multiplier of a substitution. The same exchange rate that converted slopes will soon convert areas. And the proofs, along with the general version, wait for chapter 23.

To solve for a pair, check that its Jacobian is not zero. A transformation and its inverse: their Jacobians multiply to one. And everything here is local: near a point, not across the whole plane. The Jacobian determinant: the exchange rate between two coordinate systems.
