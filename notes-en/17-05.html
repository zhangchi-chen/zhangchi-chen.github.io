17-05 The multivariable Taylor formula: the approximation climbs to second order

The multivariable Taylor formula: the approximation climbs to second order

1 · Motivation

Today the approximation climbs one rung higher. Back in the total differential episode, the tangent plane guessed 1.32. The true value is about 1.3563, so the plane is off by 0.036.

Why? A plane is flat, so it is blind to the bend. Along our step, the surface curves upward, away from the line. First order is used up, and we need the next rung. Think of a map app with a flat view and a terrain view. Same route, plus one new layer, the bend of the ground. Our goal is a second-order approximation that can see the bend. Two tools will get us there, convex regions and a one-variable trick.

2 · Statements

Convex region: the segment joining any two points of D stays in D.
A disc is convex, and so is a rectangle.
A horseshoe is not: this segment leaves the region.
Why care? The proof will walk along the segment PQ.
On a horseshoe the walk leaves D, and the conclusion can fail.
So convexity is a hypothesis, not decoration.
Theorem 17.8, the mean value theorem, in the course text's words.
Clause one: f is differentiable on a convex open region D.
Pick any P(a, b) and Q(a + h, b + k) in D.
The change in f equals partials times steps, added up.
But the partials are read at one point on the segment, θ of the way.
θ lies in (0, 1), but the theorem does not say where.
It is the two-variable twin of the one-variable mean value theorem.
Theorem 17.9, Taylor's theorem, in the course text's words.
Hypothesis: continuous partials up to order n + 1 near P₀.
The Taylor polynomial: rungs 0 to n, all read at P₀.
The remainder: one rung higher, read part way along the segment.
This is the Lagrange remainder, just as in one variable.
If o(ρⁿ) is enough, continuity up to order n will do.
ρ = √(h² + k²) is the step size; the remainder dies faster than ρⁿ.
So the remainder comes two ways: an exact formula, or just o(ρⁿ).

A convex region keeps the segment between any two of its points inside. A disc passes the test, and so does a rectangle. A horseshoe fails, because this segment runs outside. Why care? Because the proof walks along that segment. On a horseshoe the walk leaves the region, and the conclusion can fail. So convexity is a real hypothesis, not decoration.

Here is Theorem 17.8, the mean value theorem. First, f is differentiable on a convex open region. Pick any two points, one step apart. The change in f is partials times steps, added up. But the partials are read at one point along the segment. Theta sits between 0 and 1, and the theorem won't say where. It is the two-variable twin of the one-variable theorem.

Now Theorem 17.9, Taylor's theorem. The hypothesis: partials stay continuous, one order beyond n. The Taylor polynomial climbs rung by rung, all read at the start. The remainder sits one rung higher, read part way along the segment. This is the Lagrange form of the remainder, as in one variable.

If a small-o remainder is enough, we need one order less. Rho is the step size, and the remainder dies faster than that power of it. The remainder comes two ways, an exact formula or a promise that it's small.

3 · Method

Now the heart of the episode, a one-variable trick. Walk the straight corridor from the dorm to the cafeteria, as t runs from 0 to 1. Write down the height along the way, and call it phi. Phi is an ordinary one-variable function. Milestone one, its two ends are the values at the two points. Milestone two, borrow the one-variable mean value theorem. Somewhere inside, at theta, the tangent is as steep as the chord. Milestone three, the chain rule translates the slope of phi into partials. Stop the walk at theta, and the theorem appears. Convexity was used right here, the whole corridor must lie inside.

For Taylor, borrow the one-variable Taylor formula from episode six seven. Each derivative of phi applies the operator once more. Expand at the start, step to the end, and Taylor's theorem drops out. A neighbourhood is a disc, so the corridor stays inside for free.

Careful, squaring an operator is not squaring a number. Squaring means apply it once, then apply it again. Four terms come out, just like expanding a binomial. Continuous mixed partials are equal, so the two mixed terms merge. The coefficients go one, two, one, the binomial pattern. And don't forget the half in front of the second-order term. 4 · Outlook

First use, if both partials vanish on a convex region, f is constant. The mean value theorem makes every pair of values equal. Second use, the right side is the gradient of episode seventeen four, meeting the step. So a capped gradient caps how much f can change between the two points.

Looking ahead, numerical software solves systems of equations with Newton's method. Linearize at the guess, solve, step, and go around again. It's the total differential's linear approximation, run in a loop. Today the approximation climbed from the plane to second order. A convex corridor, a one-variable engine, and the chain rule to translate.
