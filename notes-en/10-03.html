10-03 Area of closed parametric curves: the sign follows the direction of travel

Area of closed parametric curves: the sign follows the direction of travel

1 · Motivation

Today, the area inside a closed curve, and a sign that follows the way you travel. Two roommates set out to find the area of one and the same ellipse. Roommate one plugs the ellipse into the formula and gets minus six pi. Roommate two writes it a little differently and gets plus six pi. Same ellipse, same formula, and yet the signs disagree. The only difference is the direction each of them walks around it.

Picture yourself running one lap on the campus track. Now imagine a strange pedometer: one direction counts plus, the other counts minus. Today we find out where that sign comes from, and how to tame it.

2 · Statements

First the easier case: an arc that does not close.
Let the curve be given by x = x(t), y = y(t), t ∈ [α, β],
with y continuous, x continuously differentiable and x′(t) ≠ 0 on (α, β);
writing a = x(α), b = x(β),
the area of the region bounded by the curve, the lines x = a, x = b and the x-axis
is A = ∫_α^β |y(t)x′(t)| dt.
Now close the curve: the dot returns to where it started.
If the parametric curve is closed — x(α) = x(β), y(α) = y(β) —
and does not intersect itself on (α, β),
then the area of the enclosed region is A = |∫_α^β y(t)x′(t) dt|
(equally |∫_α^β x(t)y′(t) dt|).
This formula follows from (1) and (3).
The sign inside the absolute value is determined by the orientation of the curve.
A closed curve without self-intersection is called a simple closed curve.

First, the easier case: an arc that never comes back to its start. A curve is traced as t moves along an interval. The height only has to be continuous, while the shadow on the axis never stops or turns back. Mark where the shadow starts and ends. Take the region fenced in by the arc, two vertical lines, and the axis. Its area is one integral in t.

Now let the curve close up, so the dot comes home. Suppose the curve is closed, ending exactly where it began. and never crosses itself along the way, then the enclosed area is one integral, wrapped in absolute value bars. Swapping the two letters works too. It is built from formula one and formula three. And the sign under the bars depends on which way the curve is travelled. A closed curve that never crosses itself has a name: a simple closed curve.

3 · Method

Back to formula three: it is just the substitution rule wearing a costume. Let the parameter drive the position, and the step changes form. The limits travel along with it, exactly as in chapter nine. If the arc runs from right to left, the integral quietly turns negative. Our half ellipse would report minus three pi. A continuous speed that is never zero can never switch direction. The absolute value simply absorbs the reversal, and the area is three pi. Exactly half of six pi, and the referee from the last episode agrees.

Back to the closed loop, where the real danger is the sign. Send a dot counterclockwise and watch the running total. The total only sinks, and stops at minus six pi. Now run the very same ellipse clockwise. This time the total climbs to plus six pi. For this reading: counterclockwise gives minus, clockwise gives plus. The other reading has the opposite habit: counterclockwise comes out plus. The sign is a fact about you, not about the region. Write the warning on your formula card: drop the bars, and you get a sign. Put the bars back, and both laps report six pi. Just like the track: a lap is a lap, whichever way you run it.

Worked example one: a general ellipse. The parameter goes once around, counterclockwise. The key move: the integrand is a negative multiple of sine squared. Sine squared averages one half over a full turn, so the integral is minus pi a b. The bars turn it into pi a b. Make it a circle, and the referee from the last episode nods again.

Worked example two: one arch of the cycloid, drawn by a rolling wheel. The wheel's point always moves forward inside the interval, so formula three applies. Height and forward speed are the same expression, so the integrand is a square. The result: three times the area of the rolling wheel. A quick check: the arch fits inside a box of four pi a squared, and it holds a triangle of half that, so our answer sits comfortably between.

One loop, and two ways to read its area. They always come out opposite, because together they add up to nothing on a closed loop. So subtract and halve, and both coordinates get an equal say. Averaging the two gives a form that does not care where the origin sits. Test it on a circle: the integrand collapses to a constant. Over a full turn: pi r squared, once more.

Now slide the whole loop, as if the origin had moved. The integrand picks up two extra terms. Around a closed loop the total rise and the total run are both zero, so the extras vanish. On an open arc they would stay: only the round trip cancels them. Each tiny piece is a thin triangle, fanned out from the origin. Move the origin outside: some triangles now count negative, yet the total stays.

Now an honest question: how does the course text actually prove formula four? It cuts the loop at its leftmost and rightmost points, leaving an upper graph and a lower graph, and formula one takes their difference. Going counterclockwise, the upper branch runs backwards, and that is where the minus sign is born. This only works if every vertical line meets the loop at most twice. A curve shaped like the letter C breaks this: some vertical lines cross it four times. The formula is promised for every simple closed curve, but this proof covers fewer. The general proof is a debt we leave open for now.

4 · Outlook

Green's theorem, in chapter twenty one, turns the loop into an integral over the region. That pays the debt in full, curves shaped like a C included. Stokes' theorem, in chapter twenty two, lifts the idea from flat loops to curved surfaces. Both of those later episodes will point back to this one.

First use: a running watch records your lap, point by point. On a polygon the integral becomes a sum over the corners: 1450 square metres. Run it the other way and the raw sum reads minus 1450, so the bars return. Second use: font files draw the hole in a letter the opposite way round. The sign of the area tells the software which loop is the hole.

Looking ahead: this expression is the course's first line integral, in disguise. A planimeter finds an area just by tracing its boundary, running on this very identity. The takeaway: the sign tells your direction, and the absolute value tells the area.
