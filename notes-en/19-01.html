19-01 Integrals with a parameter: the parameter moves, the integral follows

Integrals with a parameter: the parameter moves, the integral follows

1 · Motivation

An integral can quietly carry a parameter. Homework hands us this integral. Try simple numbers, and the curve looks harmless. The area underneath is plainly finite. But every attempt at an antiderivative stalls. We park it for later. Here is the twist: the exponents can slide. Every position of b produces one number. So the integral is secretly a function. And functions come with limits, slopes and areas. That idea is the key to the homework.

2 · Statements

Start with a function f(x, y) on a rectangle R.
Freeze x, and f(x, ·) is one curve in y.
The area under it is a number, called φ(x).
Slide x, and the curve and its area both move.
Record every area, and φ becomes a new function of x.
Each area is 09-01's definite integral, run once per x.
This object is an integral with a parameter.
Do nearby values of x give nearby areas?
Theorem 19.1 (continuity) says yes.
Hypothesis: f is continuous on the whole closed rectangle.
Conclusion: φ is continuous on [a, b].
Read it as a swap: limit and integral commute.
This is the limit under the integral sign.
Now break continuity at just one corner.
Take this f, which misbehaves only at the origin.
As x → 0⁺, each bump's area tends to 1/2.
Yet at each fixed y, f(x, y) → 0.
Limit inside gives 0; limit outside gives 1/2.
So the continuity hypothesis is doing real work.

Start with a function on a rectangle. Hold x still, and f becomes an ordinary curve. The area under it is one number, phi of x. Slide x, and the curve and its area move together. Write down every area, and a new function appears. Each area is the definite integral of episode nine one. This object is called an integral with a parameter. Do neighbouring curves have neighbouring areas?

The continuity theorem answers yes. The hypothesis is continuity on the whole closed rectangle. The conclusion: the area function has no jumps. Read it as a swap of limit and integral. This move is called taking the limit under the integral sign.

Now spoil continuity at a single corner. Take a function that misbehaves only at the origin. Squeeze x toward zero, and the area heads for one half. Yet at every fixed spot, the height dies away. Swap the order, and zero turns into one half. So that continuity hypothesis really earns its place.

3 · Method

Why is continuity enough? Two pictures tell it. Recall sixteen four: on a closed rectangle, continuity is uniform. A single delta works for every y simultaneously. So the two areas differ by at most a thin strip.

Next question: can the derivative slip inside as well? With fixed ends, differentiate inside, then integrate. Differentiate first, and integrate second. The mean value theorem trades each quotient for a nearby slope. And uniform continuity of the partial carries the limit inside.

Now let both ends of the integral move as well. Theorem nineteen point four handles moving ends. We need f and its partial continuous, and ends that move differentiably. The derivative comes in three pieces.

Look at the area between the two moving ends. Nudge x, and the curve itself shifts: term one. The upper end slides, adding a thin strip: term two. The lower end slides too, and its strip is subtracted. Think of a parcel: the contents, and two flaps. The chain rule delivers the same three pieces in one go.

Here is the classic slip: keeping just the first piece. Test it on an example we can also do by hand. By hand, we get the true derivative first. Both ends slide right, the upper end twice as fast. The first piece alone gives just a third of the truth. Add the two flaps, and it matches the hand answer. Both ends move, so neither flap may be dropped.

In this example, alpha hides in three places. The theorem makes it differentiable, hence continuous. So put alpha to zero, and the limit is pi over four.

Example four resists a direct attack. The trick: bring in a parameter and let it slide. At one end the area is zero; we want the other end. Differentiate first, and the logarithm melts into a fraction. Partial fractions, and the derivative comes out clean. Then integrate the derivative back across the slider. And the twist: the last piece is our target again. Solve for it: pi over eight, times the log of two. Bring in a parameter, differentiate, then integrate back.

4 · Outlook

The third swap: two integrals, and which goes first? Theorem nineteen point six: the order of integration may be swapped. Both sides are iterated integrals, sliced in different directions.

Now we bring back the homework we parked. The key move: the integrand is itself an integral. So the homework becomes an iterated integral. Because a is positive, the integrand is continuous everywhere on it. Swap the order, and the inner integral turns easy. And the answer is one clean logarithm. So the shaded area from the start was this number all along.

Three theorems, and three swaps. Each one runs on continuity over a closed rectangle. Stretch the rectangle to infinity, and this is no longer enough. That upgrade needs uniform convergence, which episode nineteen two takes on.

A familiar exam question is just a special case. Nothing moves inside and the bottom is fixed, so one flap remains. The Gamma and Beta functions ahead are integrals of this kind too. And chapter twenty one will swap the order inside double integrals. Later, in probability, expectations and distribution functions carry parameters too. Each time a parameter slides and gets differentiated, today’s theorems are at work. The parameter moves, and the integral follows.
