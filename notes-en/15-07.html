15-07 From square waves to MP3: Fourier's compression magic

From square waves to MP3: Fourier's compression magic

1 · Motivation

From square waves to MP3: the magic of Fourier compression. Here is a square wave. Flat, flat, and then a sudden jump. A sine curve is smooth everywhere. No corners, no jumps. So how could adding smooth curves ever build a jump?

Picture the equalizer in your music app. Each slider looks after one frequency: one harmonic. Each slider plays a sine, faster and faster as you go right. Somewhere in these sliders hides a preset: the square wave. And the songs on your phone lean on these very sliders. Today we build the wave, read its recipe, and then squeeze it.

2 · Statements

3 · Method

Now let's build it, pushing one slider at a time. Push slider one. A lone sine: far too round. Slider three to a third. The sides stand up straighter. Slider five to a fifth. The top flattens, the shoulders sharpen. The even sliders never move. Only the odd ones do. Keep going, seven then nine, and it hugs the wave tighter. Now skip ahead to twenty five odd harmonics. Flat tops and steep walls. The course text's figure, now in motion. But look closely at the walls. Small ripples cling to every jump.

Remember those ripples. But first, why only the odd sliders? We can read the recipe off the wave itself, before computing anything. First check: rotate it about the origin, and it lands on itself. An odd function wipes out every cosine, as the extensions episode showed. Second check: slide it half a period, and it turns upside down. And that flip wipes out every even harmonic: the fingerprint from the period episode. An even harmonic never flips over, so its two halves cancel out. Odd harmonics flip right along with the wave, so their halves add. Before a single integral, the prediction is in: odd sines only. And the integrals merely confirm it: odd sliders get their reciprocals, even ones get zero. That strange height, a quarter of pi, was picked to keep the coefficients clean.

Now, back to those ripples at the jump. Add more harmonics, and the ripples crowd in toward the jump. But the spike never gets shorter: the Gibbs phenomenon, back from the convergence episode. Sharp corners are paid for in high frequencies. The smoother the wave, the faster its coefficients die away. Theorem 15.1: if the coefficient sizes add up, then it converges absolutely and uniformly, everywhere. It is the M test again: one convergent fence holds every tail. And our square wave fails it: the odd reciprocals add up to infinity. And no fence ever could: smooth partial sums cannot converge uniformly to a jump. Its convergence, point by point, was settled long ago by Dirichlet's test.

4 · Outlook

Here's a twist: this toolkit was first built for heat, not music. Warm half a metal ring, cool the other half. That is our square wave. Each sine piece cools at its own pace, and the fast wiggles die first. Which starting profiles split this way? That question became the convergence theorem.

Now the payoff. Keep only the first few harmonics. With just three sliders, the shape is already unmistakable. Throw the rest away. That is lossy compression in one sentence. Like sending a song with only the bass and middle sliders set. And the ear is remarkably forgiving about the high frequencies we dropped. The eye forgives too, block by block, inside every JPEG photo. MP3 and JPEG are the household names of exactly this trade.

But why is cutting safe? And why sines, not polynomials? As the coefficients episode showed, they meet at right angles: drop one, nothing else moves. With slanted directions, drop one and the other must change, from one to three. Powers of x lean on one another. Sines and cosines never do.

Later on, the Fourier transform stretches this to signals that never repeat. Its discrete version runs inside every phone call and every photo. And eigenfunctions, the natural vibration modes, come back in differential equations and machine learning.

Look back over the chapter: coefficients, convergence, extensions, periods. Then the special values, and finally, today, the signals. The constants in the middle of the chapter and the songs at its end come from the same five formulas. Same tools, two worlds: numbers and sound. That is the chapter.
