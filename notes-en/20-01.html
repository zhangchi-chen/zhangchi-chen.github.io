20-01 Line integrals of the first type: weighing a curve

Line integrals of the first type: weighing a curve

1 · Motivation

Today we learn to weigh a curve. Picture a necklace arc, beads growing heavier toward the top. Its density simply equals its height. So what does the whole arc weigh?

The first instinct: integrate the density along the x axis. But that is just the area of a quarter disk, about 0.785. Yet the careful weighing we will do later gives exactly one. Here is the catch: we measured the shadow, not the wire. Where the arc is steep, a big piece leaves only a tiny shadow. The shadow loses length, so that first answer comes out too light.

So let's be honest and weigh it one piece at a time. Each piece weighs roughly its density times its length. Add the pieces up, then keep cutting them finer. It is the definite integral's recipe, with the interval bent into a curve. Cut, estimate, add, and take the limit: four familiar steps.

2 · Statements

The course text's definition of the line integral of the first type.
It is called the line integral of the first type of f along L, written ∫_L f ds.
Clause one: L is a rectifiable plane curve; it has a length.
And f is a function defined on L.
A partition T cuts L into small arcs of lengths Δsᵢ.
On each arc, choose any point (ξᵢ, ηᵢ).
Weigh and add: the sum Σ f(ξᵢ, ηᵢ)Δsᵢ.
The fineness ‖T‖ is the length of the longest small arc.
Now let the fineness ‖T‖ shrink to zero.
If the limit exists, independent of T and of the chosen points,
then this limit is written ∫_L f ds.
For a wire with density f, this limit is its mass.
Notice: every Δsᵢ is a length, so it is never negative.
Walk the curve backwards: the same arcs, the same lengths.
So the sum, and its limit, come out exactly the same.
The line integral of the first type does not depend on direction.
Remember this spot: the second type, next episode, flips sign.

Here is how the course text writes it down. That limit gets a name: the line integral of the first type. First, the curve must have a length we can measure. Second, we need a function living on that curve. Then we cut the curve into small arcs and note each length. On each little arc, pick any point you like. Weigh each arc at its point, and add everything up. The fineness tracks the longest arc of the cut. Keep cutting until the longest arc shrinks to nothing. Suppose every way of cutting and picking lands on one number. That shared number is the integral along the curve. For a wire, that number is simply its mass.

Notice that every piece is a length, and lengths are never negative. Walk it backwards and you meet the same arcs, just as long. So the sum, and the final answer, do not change at all. This integral simply has no sense of direction. Remember this spot: next time, reversing the walk will flip a sign.

3 · Method

The theorem turns that limit into an ordinary integral. For a smooth curve and a continuous function, the formula holds. That square root is a speed: how fast the point runs. Speed times a sliver of time gives a sliver of distance. It is the same arc length piece from our chapter on curves. So the theorem is just a substitution, plus that length piece. Key move: each arc is the speed at one moment times its time slot. Now the sum is an ordinary integral sum, and the limit finishes it.

Careful: the function is only read at points on the curve. Both coordinates turn into functions of the parameter. Back to the necklace, riding the unit circle. On the circle, the density is the sine, and the speed is one. Integrate over a quarter turn and the mass is exactly one. The shadow's answer was smaller because it ignored the tilt.

When the curve is a graph, the variable x itself is the parameter. Then one square becomes one, and only the slope is left. In space, the third coordinate adds one more square.

First drill: integrate the squared distance along a half circle. Let the angle sweep from zero to half a turn. On the circle, the squared distance never changes. Its speed is just the radius. Constant times length: the answer is pi times a cubed. Spot the constant early, and the grinding disappears.

Second drill: weigh a piece of parabola by its height. Which hand to write with: across, or up? Pick the horizontal, and the slope blows up at the origin. Pick the vertical, and everything stays smooth: the easy hand. Height times stretch, from bottom to top. One substitution, and the integral falls open. About 2.438: choosing the right hand saved half the work.

Third drill: a plane slices a sphere, leaving a circle. We want x squared along it, and that circle is awkward to write down. But the sphere and the plane treat all three coordinates alike. Turn it a third of a turn: the axes trade places, the circle stays put. So the three squares carry exactly the same integral. Add them up, and on the sphere the sum is a constant. The plane runs through the center, so this is a great circle. Each one takes a third: two thirds of pi times a cubed. Look for symmetry first, and average before you integrate.

4 · Outlook

First use: the mass of any wire, whatever its density. Second use: moments of inertia, distance squared times density. For a uniform half ring, the two moments add up to drill one. Every bit sits at the same distance, so it is mass times radius squared.

Third use: put up a garden fence whose height follows the function. Each fence panel is height times width: the very same sum. And our necklace's fence covers an area of exactly one.

Before you go, two traps. Trap one: the lower limit is always the smaller number. Write the limits backwards, and the mass turns negative. Lengths are never negative, so the limits cannot carry direction. Trap two: both coordinates go into the function, not just one.

Weigh a surface instead, and the same story repeats one level up. Same four steps, with little patches of area instead of arcs. In probability, a curve can carry its own spread of mass. Cut, weigh, add, refine: and the curve is weighed.
