10-01 The element method: slice thin, then pass one o(Δx) test

The element method: slice thin, then pass one o(Δx) test

1 · Motivation

Today's method: slice thin, then pass one test. Every application chapter repeats the same three-step rule. It comes like a rule card, and the back is blank.

Say you want to cover your dorm lampshade with fabric. Its side is a straight line, spun around the axis. Plan A slices it into a stack of thin cylinders. Plan A asks for about 9.42 square decimetres. Plan B slices it into thin frustums. Plan B asks for about 13.33 square decimetres. Same lampshade, and the two answers are almost four apart.

Both slices look thin enough, so which one is wrong? Today the rule card turns into a real theorem. The price is one honest check per formula, like a unit test. And this very test is what exposes Plan A.

2 · Statements

Here is the method in the course text's own words.

Clause one: the quantity Φ is spread along [a, b].
Φ(x) is the part over [a, x]; Φ(b) is the target.
Clause two: on a thin piece [x, x + Δx], choose an approximation Δ′Φ.
Clause three: it must look like f(x) times Δx.
And f must be continuous.
Clause four: the error Δ′Φ − f(x)Δx must be o(Δx).
That is: error divided by Δx tends to 0.
Then write dΦ = f(x)dx, and integrate from a to b.
The course text adds three cautions.

Let's repackage the method as three hypotheses.
(i) Additivity: the whole equals the sum of its pieces.
And an empty interval carries nothing: Φ[a, a] = 0.

3 · Method

Now the proof, and it fits in two lines. Step one: the piece becomes a change in the running total. Shrink the width: the derivative is the coefficient. In pictures: the running total climbs at exactly that pace. Step two: the fundamental theorem pins it down, up to a constant. At the start the total is zero, so the constant goes. And there it is: the target is exactly an integral. Two lines, and the rule card was a theorem in work clothes. Continuity is spent exactly once, in step two. The notation is chapter 5's differential, invariance and all.

Now let's match the three cautions to the hypotheses. Caution one is the additivity hypothesis, word for word. A failure: the peak height over a stretch just does not add up. Caution two: pick the estimate by the strict definition. Arc length is defined by sums of chords, so the chord it is. Caution three is the entrance test for the approximation. Pass the test, and the formula can ship.

Now four elements in a row, derived, not memorised. Here is the course text's list, which we now earn. First element, area: a thin strip under the curve. Up close, the strip is almost a rectangle. The leftover sliver is boxed in by how little the curve moves. Continuity makes that box vanish, so the area element passes. Where the curve dips below the axis, take the absolute value.

Second element, polar area: a thin slice of pie. Up close, it is the sector from school. Or picture a thin triangle: the same answer to first order. The radius barely changes across the sliver, so it passes too.

Third element, volume: a thin slab, like a slice of bread. If the cross-sections change continuously, the slab passes. Spin a curve, and every cross-section is a disc. So disc area times thickness is the volume element.

Fourth element, arc length: an old triangle from chapter 5 returns. The tiny triangle obeys Pythagoras. Pull out the width, and the arc element appears. With a continuous slope, chord and tangent agree to first order.

Now the big moment: the surface of revolution, our lampshade. Candidate A, the lazy one: treat the band as a cylinder. Candidate B, the honest one: a frustum hugging the sliver. Its area is the average circumference times the slant height. Run the test on B: what is left shrinks faster. Pass. Zoom in, and the frustum hugs the curve; only crumbs are left. Now test A: the gap keeps a whole first-order term. Per unit width, the error settles at a fixed number, not zero.

At the rim of the lampshade, shrink the slice tenfold each time. The honest column melts away; the lazy one stalls near 2.6. So the lazy error is first order, not a higher-order infinitesimal. Pile up those errors, and they never go away: a wrong formula. That is exactly the missing fabric from our lampshade. The honest element wins: circumference times arc element. This is the unit test that catches the bug before release.

The same test also catches a shortcut for arc length. Take the shadow as the element, and every curve is as long as its shadow. A diagonal of a unit square would come out as just 1. The test sees it at once: the ratio settles above zero. Only a perfectly flat curve passes everywhere.

4 · Outlook

Five elements on one card, and each one passed its test. Next episode, each one faces the formulas from school. One use: measure a bottle's cross-sections, then stack the slabs. Later, the same checklist handles work, pressure and mass.

Spread the same three hypotheses over a flat region, and you get double integrals. Lay them along a curve with vector rates, and you get line integrals. Slice thin, pass one test, and that is the whole method.
