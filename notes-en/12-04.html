12-04 The integral test: series and integrals, one bloodstream

The integral test: series and integrals, one bloodstream

1 · Motivation

The integral test, where a series and an integral share one bloodstream. Last time, both of these series gave a ratio that crawls up to one. Yet their fates split: one converges, the other diverges. Right at one, the geometric ruler is simply too coarse.

Picture your monthly budget, tracked in two different ways. Way one: add up what you spend each day, one bar at a time. Way two: measure the area under the curve of how fast you spend. The app's bar chart hugs that smooth curve. Could one total stay finite while the other runs off to infinity? Today's answer: as long as the curve goes downhill, they always agree. And it finally settles the dead zone from last episode.

2 · Statements

Here is the integral test, as the course text states it.
Clause one: f is decreasing on [1, +∞).
As in every positive-term series, f stays nonnegative.
The series samples f only at the whole numbers.
The integral takes in the whole area from 1 onward.
“If and only if”: they converge together or diverge together.
Same fate, different values: about 1.645 versus exactly 1.
The trade: a sum we can't add, for an integral we can compute.

Here is the integral test, in the course text's own words. First clause: from one onward, the function only falls. As in every positive-term series, the values never dip below zero. The series only looks at the heights above whole numbers. The integral swallows the whole area from one onward. If and only if means they converge together, or diverge together. Same fate, but not the same value. The trade: an endless sum, swapped for an integral we can actually compute.

3 · Method

Let's zoom into a single strip. Because it decreases, the graph never rises above the left edge. And it never sinks below the right edge. So the area of this strip is trapped between two bars. Do it on every strip, and the tall bars cover the whole area. So the area can never beat the total of the tall bars. Slide the bars one step left, and they hide under the curve. The partial sum is now pinned between two areas. Finite area means the partial sums hit a ceiling, so they converge. Infinite area drags the partial sums off to infinity as well. The series and the integral converge as one single event.

Now drop the word decreasing, and let the graph spike inside the strips. The bars only see the heights at whole numbers, and miss every spike. Inside the strip the graph breaks out, and the sandwich falls apart. Thin spikes can hide plenty of area while the bars stay tiny. Positive and shrinking is far from enough; decreasing is needed.

Two episodes ago, we used the p-series benchmark on credit. Today the integral test settles that debt, with an integral from the same family. Chapter eleven drew the watershed: past one, the integral converges. So the p-series outcome is now proved, not borrowed. Fine print: when the exponent is not positive, the terms never shrink. There, the necessary condition from an earlier episode decides at once: divergence.

Second withdrawal: the logarithmic series. Key move: let the logarithm itself become the new variable. One substitution, and the familiar p integral walks right back in. Same watershed, one logarithm deeper: it converges only past one. One rung down the ladder: the denominator picks up one more logarithm.

Now back to the series stuck in the dead zone. Ratio test, one. Root test, one. Neither can help. The integral test sees an exponent of exactly one: divergence. Its integral is a logarithm of a logarithm, creeping toward infinity. So slow that climbing past five takes more than a billion trillion terms. For any decay like a power of n, both tests can only read one. The integral test reads the exponent straight off the term. That closes the dead zone from last episode, just as promised.

An optional segment: a finer ruler, called Raabe's test. The ratio hugs one; what matters is how far it falls short. Test it on the p-series: this reading is exactly the exponent. So this reading is the series' effective exponent. Raabe's test, in the course text's own words. Above one converges, below one diverges, just like p.

Example thirteen: odd numbers multiplied, over even numbers multiplied, all raised to a power. Neighbouring terms differ by just one new odd over one new even. Raabe's reading comes out to exactly half the power. Power one: the reading is one half, so it diverges. Power three: the reading is one and a half, so it converges. Power two lands right on one, and the limit form goes silent. The inequality form steps in: the reading always stays under one, divergence. Remember this reading: chapter fourteen uses it at the binomial endpoints. Raabe's test has its own dead zone, right at one. There is no slowest convergent series, so no ruler ever finishes the job.

4 · Outlook

Bonus: the same picture puts a price on the leftover tail. That is how numerical courses decide how many terms are enough. The debt to an earlier episode is paid: the p-series benchmark is proved. A mirror of chapter eleven: one watershed governs both worlds.

In numerical analysis, these slivers become correction formulas. In probability, the integral of the tail decides whether the average is finite. Decreasing glues the bars to the curve: one bloodstream.
