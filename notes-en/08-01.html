08-01 The antiderivative: differentiation played backwards, plus a constant that refuses to die

The antiderivative: differentiation played backwards, plus a constant that refuses to die

1 · Motivation

Today we play differentiation backwards. On the ride to class, your phone logged your speed. Speed tells how fast position changes. Can we rebuild the position? The same log fits many trips, one for every place you could have started. Tell me where you started, and the whole trip is pinned down.

Differentiation turns a function into its derivative. Now we walk back. We are handed the slope at every point and must find the curve. Does this candidate give back arc tangent? First the product rule, then the chain rule, one term at a time. The two extra terms cancel, and only arc tangent is left. The check passes, but nothing told us to try this one. Checking is easy. Finding it is the real problem.

2 · Statements

Definition 1: F is an antiderivative of f on I.
First clause: everything lives on one interval I.
At every point of I, F's slope equals f's height.
Notice the word “an”: there will be more than one.
Theorem 8.1: continuity on an interval guarantees an antiderivative.
It promises that F exists, not how to write it down.
Theorem 8.2: antiderivatives come as F plus a constant.
Part one: lift F by any constant, and it still works.
Part two: any two of them differ only by a constant.
Find one, and you have found them all.
Definition 2: all antiderivatives together form the indefinite integral.
First the integral sign ∫, then the integrand f(x).
f(x) dx is the integrand expression; x is the variable of integration.
For short, we write ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C.
That C is the constant of integration.
Same design, every size: that is the family F + C.

Here is the official name for what we are hunting. First condition: everything happens on one unbroken interval. At every point, the slope on the left equals the height on the right. Notice it says an antiderivative. It will not be alone. Theorem eight point one: continuous on an interval means an antiderivative exists. It promises existence, and says nothing about a formula.

Theorem eight point two tells us what the whole crowd of answers looks like. Part one: slide the graph up or down, and it still qualifies. Part two: any two of them are just a constant apart. Find one, and you have found them all.

Definition two gives the whole crowd one name: the indefinite integral. First the integral sign, then the integrand, the function inside. The piece with its differential is the integrand expression; x is the variable of integration. For short: one antiderivative, plus a constant. That constant is the constant of integration. One design, printed on shirts of every size.

3 · Method

Part one takes one line: differentiation wipes the constant out. Part two: their difference has zero slope everywhere. The corollary of Lagrange's mean value theorem says zero slope means constant. Chapter six left us exactly the tool we needed. But the interval matters. Watch this function on both sides of zero. Lift only the right branch, and the derivative does not notice. Two pieces, two constants, so no single constant works.

Differentiate the whole family, and the original function comes right back. Go the other way, and a constant tags along. These two moves undo each other, up to a constant.

For this simple slope rule, the integral curves are a stack of parabolas. Each one is the same curve, just shifted up or down. Stand at any x, and every curve has the same tilt there. One initial value picks exactly one curve out of the stack. The constant is pinned at two, and the curve is found. The initial condition picks your size from the rack.

Back to motion, as in the course text: the acceleration never changes. Undo once: many possible speeds, and the starting speed picks one. Undo again, and a second constant appears. Each constant is pinned by one starting value. Undo twice, and you need two starting facts.

Every elementary function is continuous on each interval where it is defined. So on every such interval, an antiderivative is guaranteed. The proof is a debt we leave for chapter nine. And it never promises a formula you can write down.

Now the tempting reverse question: does having an antiderivative force continuity? Meet a function that wiggles faster and faster near zero. Squeezed between two parabolas, the curve leaves zero perfectly flat. Away from zero, the usual rules give a two-part formula. So this derivative exists everywhere, and it has an antiderivative by construction. But near zero, the cosine part never stops swinging. So it has no limit at zero, and continuity breaks right there. A discontinuous function can still have an antiderivative. Continuity is enough, but it is not required.

Differentiation comes with a recipe: work out one limit. An antiderivative comes with only a target to hit. So our toolbox is the derivative table, played in reverse like a rewound playlist.

4 · Outlook

First use: your homework answer and the answer key do not match. Differentiate both, and both come back to the same function. Their difference is a constant one half, exactly as promised. Before you panic, differentiate. Two different-looking answers can both be right.

Second use: your club prints posters, and you know what each extra one costs. Total cost is pinned down only up to a constant. That constant is the setup fee, paid before printing anything.

Chapter six already did its part: zero slope means a constant gap. Still owed: chapter nine will prove that continuity guarantees existence. Next, the differential table becomes our first real technique.

Looking ahead: this bell curve is continuous, so an antiderivative exists. Yet no formula from the table can express it. The probability course meets it again, over the whole line or by number crunching. Its antiderivative is a perfectly good function. It just is not in the table. Check by differentiating, and never forget the constant.
