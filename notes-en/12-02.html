12-02 Positive-term series: find a benchmark and compare

Positive-term series: find a benchmark and compare

1 · Motivation

Positive term series. Find a benchmark, then compare. Last time, two kinds of series let us write the running total exactly. Now take the inverse squares, and no neat formula appears. Its terms fade away, but so do the terms of the harmonic series. Yet the harmonic series diverges, as the previous episode proved. So fading terms settle nothing. Where else can we look?

Picture the 800 metre test, beside a friend whose pace you know. Suppose each stretch of yours takes no longer than your friend's. Then you cross the finish line no later than they do. Their finish time caps your running total at every moment. Positive terms act like split times. The total can only grow. Today's plan is to measure an unknown series against a benchmark we trust.

With no negative terms, each new total only adds a step. So the partial sums form a staircase that never steps down. A staircase like this has only two possible endings. Either it settles below some ceiling, or it climbs past every ceiling you could ever draw. That's the monotone bounded principle, back from chapter two. Theorem 12.5 says exactly this, for positive term series. Positivity trades the question of convergence for a question of bounds.

Now line up two positive series, one term against another. Past some point, each term stays under its partner. The first few terms may misbehave. Finitely many never decide convergence. This is the comparison principle, Theorem 12.6. Why does it work? Past that point, each step of the smaller series is no taller. So its total stays under the first few terms plus the benchmark total. Bounded. Then Theorem 12.5 hands us convergence, and part one is done. Part two is the same fact, read in reverse. If the smaller one runs off, the bigger one cannot settle. First payoff. Each inverse square sits under a telescoping term from last time. That series adds up to 1, so the inverse squares stay under 2. Convergent.

With the smaller and bigger series fixed, there are four cases. Smaller than a convergent series is safe. It converges too. Bigger than a divergent series is safe too. It diverges. Bigger than a convergent series? That tells us nothing. Above the inverse squares, one series converges and another diverges. Exam trap. A convergent smaller series says nothing about the bigger one. Smaller than a divergent series? Also nothing. Both kinds fit underneath. Convergence flows down to the smaller series. Divergence flows up.

Exact inequalities can be fiddly. A limit is often easier to get. Divide one term by the other, and watch where the ratio settles. This is the limit form of the comparison principle. When the limit is a positive number, both terms fade at the same speed. Past some point, each term is trapped between two multiples of its partner. So the two series converge together, or diverge together. That's clause one. If the limit is zero, the term ends up below its partner. Only smaller than convergent is safe, so clause two needs a convergent benchmark. If the ratio runs to infinity, the term ends up on top. That's clause three. Careful. A zero limit against a divergent benchmark tells you nothing.

3 · Method

First, a course text example: a series of small sines. Each term sits just under the matching harmonic term. Smaller than a divergent series. The plain inequality is stuck. The limit form comes to the rescue. The ratio heads to 1. A limit of one against the divergent harmonic series. So it diverges.

Second, a geometric series with a small dent. This time each term sits above its partner. Bigger than convergent. Once more, the obvious inequality points the useless way. The ratio tends to 1. The power of two dwarfs n. A limit of one against a convergent geometric series. So it converges. The lesson. With a positive finite limit, which side is bigger no longer matters.

To pick a benchmark, first rank how fast each piece of the term grows. It's the order comparison from episode two oh eight, now aimed at terms. Logs lose to powers, powers to exponentials, and those to factorials. At twenty, the factorial already has nineteen digits. Keep only the fastest piece on top, and the fastest below. It behaves like a geometric series with ratio two thirds. So it converges. A log over a cube, against inverse squares, gives zero. By clause two, the benchmark converges, so this one does too. Now a lone log downstairs. The ratio runs to infinity. By clause three, it diverges along with the harmonic series.

Every verified benchmark earns a punch on our loyalty card. Punch one, the geometric series, convergent when its ratio is below one in size. Punch two, the harmonic series, paying off last episode's promise as our divergence anchor. Benchmark three. The p series converges exactly when its power exceeds one. Its proof comes in episode twelve oh four. Until then, the punch is in pencil. One half shows already. Below one, the terms beat harmonic, so it diverges. Every card keeps two things. The outcome, and why it holds.

Can this card ever be complete? Honestly, no. No convergent series is the slowest. Another one always converges more slowly. So no comparison test, however clever, decides every series. Episode twelve oh three will automate the geometric comparisons.

4 · Outlook

Quick fire drill. Pause, choose a benchmark and a form, then decide. The first sits under the inverse squares. Smaller than convergent, so it converges. The second, against a geometric series, has ratio tending to pi. It converges. The third sits under the harmonic terms. A dead end. With the limit form the ratio tends to 1, so it diverges with harmonic. The pattern. When the easy inequality points the wrong way, take a limit.

This reflex, find a benchmark and compare, runs through all of analysis. In numerical courses, errors are measured against powers of the step size. In machine learning, a training loss is measured against a geometric decay. The move never changes. Pin the unknown under a benchmark you know. Positive terms, a good benchmark, and the right direction. That's the whole game.
