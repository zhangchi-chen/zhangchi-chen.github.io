06-05 L'Hôpital's rule: powerful, not a master key

L'Hôpital's rule: powerful, not a master key

1 · Motivation

L'Hôpital's rule: the shortcut every student hears about. Plug in zero, and all three look like zero over zero. Yet their limits are zero, one and infinity. That is an indeterminate form: the shape alone decides nothing.

A classmate whispers a shortcut: differentiate top and bottom separately. Try it on a first limit: the shortcut answers three halves. Factoring gives three halves too, so the shortcut agrees. Now try a second limit, near zero. Plugging in gives one. The shortcut gives zero. Two answers for one limit. One of them must be wrong.

And on a third limit, the shortcut just walks in a circle. So when can we trust it, and when does it lead nowhere? Today: why it is powerful, but not a master key.

Chapter three's quotient rule needs the bottom limit to be nonzero. Facing zero over zero, it has nothing to say. We need a new tool. Here is the course text's theorem for the zero over zero form.

Condition one: top and bottom both shrink to zero. Condition two: differentiable near the point, with the point itself left out. And the bottom never stalls there: its slope is never zero. Condition three: the ratio of the two derivatives settles at A. A may be a real number or infinite. Then the original ratio has the same limit. A note extends it to one-sided limits and limits at infinity.

Read it as three questions you ask before borrowing a classmate's notes. First: is it really zero over zero? Notes for this course? Second: no pages missing nearby, and the bottom's slope is never zero. Third: does the ratio of derivatives settle anywhere? Three yeses, and the limit carries straight over.

3 · Method

Why should differentiating help at all? The engine is Cauchy's mean value theorem. First fill the hole: give both functions the value zero there. On that small stretch, Cauchy swaps the ratio for a ratio of derivatives. The new point xi is trapped in between. Slide toward the point, and xi gets dragged along; the ratio follows. That is the whole idea of the proof: the last episode's promise, kept.

Now remove the conditions one at a time, and watch the rule break. Without condition one: our second example was never zero over zero. Top and bottom both head to one, so the limit is just one. Blind differentiation gave zero: wrong, and said with a straight face. Mystery solved: the shortcut was taken where it does not apply. Without condition two, the Cauchy step might divide by zero. If the bottom ever came back to zero, Rolle would force a flat tangent between. So a nonzero slope also keeps the bottom off zero: the ratio makes sense. Without condition three, nothing can be concluded. An example is coming.

Now a fair use: the course text's Example three, near pi. Checklist first: top and bottom both vanish at pi. The bottom's slope is zero only at pi itself, which is left out. One round, and it simplifies to minus half the cube of cosine. Near pi, cosine heads to minus one, so the limit is one half. The graph agrees: it rises to one half, with a hole at pi.

Next, an example that needs two rounds. Simplify first, as the course text does: swap the log for its simple equivalent. Round one, and it is still zero over zero. Before round two, run the checklist again. Every round, every time. Round two gives one half. The graph confirms it: the hole sits at height one half. Skip that first swap, and round one already turns messy. Lesson: simplify before every round, then differentiate what is left.

Now the trap: a wobbly line divided by a straight line, out to infinity. Their ratio hugs the line at height one, and tends to one. But the derivative ratio swings between zero and two, forever. The derivative ratio has no limit, condition three fails, and the rule falls silent. Yet the original ratio still tends to one. The rule runs one way only. Never argue backwards: a missing derivative limit says nothing about the original. Nor the other way: a limit for the original says nothing about the derivatives.

The infinity over infinity form has its own theorem in the course text. Look at condition two: only the bottom has to blow up. The top may do anything, even stay bounded. Checking only the bottom is why it works at infinity so often.

Last trap: top and bottom both blow up. Round one flips it upside down. Round two flips it back. Exactly where we started. A playlist stuck on repeat: the rule chases its own tail. Escape by algebra: divide top and bottom by x. What is left heads straight to one. The rule is a tool for indeterminate forms, not a replacement for algebra.

4 · Outlook

Use one: ranking how fast things grow, which later chapters lean on. Log over any positive power: one round, and the limit is zero. Power over exponential: n rounds, and the limit is zero. Logs crawl, powers walk, exponentials sprint: a ranking you will reuse. Use two: once the slope settles, the ratio to x settles at the same value. Add a log to a line of slope two: the long-run slope stays two.

Five other indeterminate forms must first be rewritten into these two honest ones. That rewriting is the next episode's job. At the end of chapter seven, Stolz's theorem plays this game for sequences. And when the rule loops, Taylor's formula, later this chapter, breaks the cycle. Check the list every round, and the shortcut gets you home.
