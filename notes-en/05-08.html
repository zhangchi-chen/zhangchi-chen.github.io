05-08 The differential: a linear stand-in, and why the formula survives a change of variable

The differential: a linear stand-in, and why the formula survives a change of variable

1 · Motivation

The differential: a linear stand in that survives a change of variable. Two lines in your notes seem to argue with each other. Line one says the Leibniz symbol for the derivative is one whole symbol, not a fraction. Line two: the parametric formula looks exactly like cancelling the small change in time. If it is not a fraction, why does cancelling never go wrong? Today the two differentials become two real numbers, and the fraction bar earns its place.

A square photo for your dorm wall, ten centimetres on each side. Its area is the side squared, here one hundred square centimetres. Enlarge the print a little: the side grows by a small step, say one centimetre. The extra area: two long strips plus one small corner. Together, the two strips are twice the side times the step. The corner is the step times itself, the step squared. With ten and one: the strips give 20 square centimetres, the corner just 1. Halve the step: the strips halve, but the corner drops to a quarter. Divide the corner by the step: you get the step, which goes to zero. So the corner is an infinitesimal of higher order than the step. The strip part, proportional to the step, is the differential of the area.

2 · Statements

Here is the definition, as the course text states it.
Give x₀ a step Δx and watch the change Δy.
Suppose Δy can be written as a constant A times Δx…
…plus a remainder that is o(Δx).
o(Δx) means: even divided by Δx, it still tends to zero.
Then f is called differentiable at x₀.
The term A·Δx is the linear principal part of Δy.
It is the differential, written dy at x = x₀.
Fix x₀: then dy depends only on the step Δx.
Plot the true change Δy against the step Δx.
Every line through the origin is a linear rule; which one hugs Δy?
A wrong slope, like 1.5, leaves an error about half of Δx.
Slope 2 leaves an error of exactly (Δx)², which is o(Δx).
So the linear stand-in, when it exists, is unique.
Now pinch-zoom into the graph at x₀, like a map on your phone.
Ten times closer, the curve already looks nearly straight.
A hundred times closer, curve and tangent can no longer be told apart.
What survives the zoom is the tangent's rise over Δx: that rise is dy.
The gap Δy − dy is (Δx)², shrinking much faster than Δx.
Theorem 5.9: f is differentiable at x₀ if and only if f′(x₀) exists.
And then the constant A is exactly f′(x₀).

Here is the definition, as the course text states it. Give the point a small step and watch the change of the function. Suppose the change is a constant times the step, plus a remainder that is small even compared with the step. Higher order small means: even divided by the step, it still tends to zero. Then the function is called differentiable at that point. The constant times the step is the linear principal part. It is the differential of the function at that point.

Theorem 5.9: differentiable at a point if and only if the derivative exists there. And then the constant is exactly the derivative at that point.

3 · Method

Why is this theorem almost free in one variable? From differentiable to derivative: divide both sides by the step. As the step goes to zero, the quotient tends to the constant, so the derivative equals it. Conversely, if the derivative exists, look at what is left over. Divided by the step, it is quotient minus limit, which tends to zero. A linear function of one variable is just multiplication by a constant. One number decides it, and that number must be the derivative. In two variables, having slopes no longer guarantees a linear stand in. So there, differentiable is defined by the linear stand in itself.

Now take the simplest function of all: the output equals the input. Its derivative is 1, so its differential is exactly the step. For the independent variable, its differential is the step itself. So the differential of the function is the derivative times the differential of the variable. At each point, both differentials are ordinary numbers. Divide them: the derivative is a genuine quotient of two differentials. That is why the derivative is also called the differential quotient. Line one in your notes is upgraded: the fraction bar is now a real division.

Every derivative rule times the differential of the variable becomes a differential rule. For a product: each factor times the differential of the other, added. For a composite: outer derivative times inner derivative times the differential of the variable. The last two factors together are exactly the differential of the middle variable. So the formula has the same shape as if the middle variable were independent. This is the invariance of the first order differential form. Like a group project: whoever holds the pen, the result reads the same.

Demo one: the unit circle. Take the differential of both sides, without picking an independent variable. Twice each coordinate times its own differential, added, gives zero. Read it one way: where the vertical coordinate is not zero, the slope is minus horizontal over vertical. The other way: where the horizontal coordinate is not zero, it is minus vertical over horizontal. At the rightmost point the tangent is vertical: the first reading fails, the second gives zero. One equation, two readings, and nobody chose who was independent.

Demo two pays the debt from last episode: parametric curves. Take the cycloid from last episode, traced by a point on a rolling wheel. The horizontal differential is one minus the cosine, times the differential of the parameter. The vertical differential is the sine times the differential of the parameter. Both are numbers, so divide: the differential of the parameter cancels honestly. At a quarter turn of the wheel the slope is 1, just as last episode found. The ratio of two rates from last episode is a true quotient of differentials.

Demo three: an exponential of a sine of a linear function, three layers. Outer layer: copy the exponential, times the differential of the sine part. Middle layer: sine becomes cosine, times the differential of the linear part. Inner layer: the slope times the differential of the variable, and we are done. Whatever stands in front of the differential of the variable is the derivative. Each step used the same formula with a new middle variable; nothing was declared.

4 · Outlook

First use, linear approximation: the new value is about the old value plus the derivative times the step. If one yuan buys 1.02 units of a foreign currency, what does one unit cost? Use the reciprocal function near 1, where its derivative is minus 1. Linear estimate: one minus 0.02, that is 0.98. The true value is about 0.98039, slightly larger. The reciprocal curve stays above its tangent, so the linear estimate runs low.

Second use: how a measuring error spreads. You cut the photo by hand: the side is 10 centimetres, off by at most a millimetre. The area error is about twice the side times the side error: 2 square centimetres. That is the opening strip again; the corner adds only one hundredth of a square centimetre. A one percent error in the side becomes about two percent in the area.

One warning: invariance belongs to the first order only. The second order differential loses it; the next episode shows why. In chapter 8, substitution in integrals runs this invariance backwards. In two variables, the differential combines the two input differentials, each with its own constant. A linear map of the plane; a remainder small compared with the distance will define differentiable. Later: a matrix between spaces, then a linear operator in functional analysis. The idea never changes: up close, change is almost linear.
