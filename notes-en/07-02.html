07-02 The equivalence ring: pass the theorem around the circle

The equivalence ring: pass the theorem around the circle

1 · Motivation

The equivalence ring: we pass one theorem all the way around a circle. In the previous episode, six theorems went up on our shelf. Each one is a different way of saying the real line has no holes. And we heard that any single one of them would be enough. But why should one theorem give us the other five for free?

The brute force plan: prove every theorem from every other one. Thirty separate proofs, one per ordered pair. Not before finals week. At the dorm party, someone suggests a game of pass the parcel. Each player only ever hands it to the next person. Yet after one full lap, every single player has held it. The parcel is the property itself: the line has no holes. Six hand passes end up doing the work of thirty proofs. But wait, isn't arguing in a circle a classic mistake? No. Each arrow is a separate proof that assumes only where it starts. What the ring proves is that the six stand or fall together.

First clause: start from any one, and the other five follow. Second clause: the proof walks once around the circle, in order. The first three arrows are Theorems 2.9, 7.1 and 7.3. The next two are exercises, and the closing arrow is Example 3.

Want four to imply two? Walk the long way round. Every ordered pair is connected, so one lap gives all thirty implications. Each theorem needs at least one arrow coming in, so six is the floor. Building everything around theorem one would cost ten arrows, there and back. Proving each from a single outside axiom still leaves them unlinked to each other.

3 · Method

Slow motion for the group photo. First pass: supremum to monotone. It is Theorem 2.9, the chapter two proof we postponed until now. Take an increasing sequence that is bounded above. Its terms make a nonempty set that has an upper bound. So the Supremum Principle hands us the least upper bound, xi. Pick any positive epsilon, and shade a thin band under the wall.

Here comes the key move: some term has to enter the band. Otherwise the lower edge of the band would be a smaller upper bound. Impossible. Because the sequence increases, every later term sits at least that high. And none can climb past the wall, since the wall is an upper bound. So from that index on, every term lives inside the band. Shrink epsilon: the band narrows, the starting index moves right, the terms still fit. That is convergence to xi: the sequence pressed against the wall it built. A decreasing sequence works the same way, resting on its infimum instead.

Pass two is a shortcut straight across the ring, not one of its six arrows. Together with the first two arrows, it closes a small loop of its own. Take a set that is nonempty and bounded above. Start with one point inside the set, and one upper bound. Every interval in this story carries two duties. Duty one: the right end is always an upper bound. Duty two: the set still reaches into the interval. Cut it in half. If the midpoint is an upper bound, keep the left half. If not, the set has a point beyond it, so keep the right half. Either way, both duties survive the cut.

Watch the halving bounce back and forth between the two duties. The lengths halve toward zero, so the Nested Interval Theorem gives one point. Is that point an upper bound? Suppose some member of the set beat it. But the right ends close in on that point, so one drops below the intruder. An upper bound sitting below a member of the set? Impossible. Duty one has been inherited: the point is an upper bound. Is it the least one? Take any number slightly smaller. The left ends also close in, and soon pass that smaller number. The set reaches past it, so the smaller number is no upper bound. Duty two is inherited too: the point is the least upper bound. Reading tip: the two duties are exactly the two halves of least upper bound.

The arrow that closes the ring is worked out in the course text’s Example 3. Here is its skeleton, just as the course text writes it. It finishes with a Cauchy sequence and its limit. Lay down a ruler with evenly spaced fine marks. Lambda n is the first mark that clears the whole set. One mark back, it no longer clears the set. The quiet extra ingredient is the Archimedean property: it guarantees such a mark. It comes from chapter one: tiny equal steps eventually pass any number.

Two rulers, two marks, and each one pins the other in place. So the two marks differ by less than the coarser spacing. As the spacing shrinks, the marks crowd together: a Cauchy sequence. Now theorem six, the Cauchy Convergence Criterion, gives it a limit. Every mark is an upper bound, and so is their limit. The marks one step back crowd in too, so nothing smaller works. So the limit is exactly the least upper bound, and the ring is closed.

Two to three is Theorem 7.1: the endpoints move one way only, so they settle. Three to four is Theorem 7.3: suppose it fails, then keep halving. Four to five is homework: starting from finite covers, find a cluster point. Five to six is homework too: from a convergent subsequence to the Cauchy Criterion. Now you know exactly where the homework sits on the ring.

4 · Outlook

So why go to the trouble of proving they are equivalent? Because for each task, one of the six makes it a two-line job. Take the nested square root sequence: it increases and stays below two. Monotone Convergence finishes it in two lines, and the limit is two. Do it with the Cauchy Criterion instead, and you must bound every gap. Painful. Now flip it: the alternating sums zigzag, so they are not monotone. Here the Cauchy Criterion takes two lines: late sums sit within one small step. Equivalence means whichever one fits the task is fair to use. Any one of the six can be taken as the axiom of the real numbers. The other five then arrive for free, delivered around the ring.

Later, in real analysis, the same ring gets drawn in metric spaces. There is no ordering there, so the Supremum Principle no longer makes sense. But the Cauchy property survives, and becomes the very definition of completeness. With the ring closed, the old debts from chapters two and four can finally be paid, each one paid with whichever key fits it best.
