17-04 Directional derivatives and the gradient: the steepest uphill direction

Directional derivatives and the gradient: the steepest uphill direction

1 · Motivation

Directional derivatives and the gradient: which way is steepest uphill? An evening run: your map draws the campus hill as rings of equal height. You pause at a point halfway up. Your fitness app shows the slope straight ahead. Turn slowly on the spot, and the reading keeps changing.

Partial derivatives, all we used up to the chain rule, check only east and north. But a runner can set off along any bearing at all. Every bearing comes with its own slope number. Among these eight, southwest wins, at 2.12. But some bearing in between might climb even more steeply. Two questions, then: the slope along a ray, and the steepest ray.

3 · Method

Why does it hold? Remember the total differential from chapter 17, episode 1. Put in the steps along the ray, then divide by the distance. The leftover fades away: the linear part did all the work.

Example 1: one function, one point, one direction. Careful: this vector has length 3, so shrink it first. Multiply partials by cosines and add: one third. Heading that way, the height rises one third per unit walked. Skip the shrinking, and you report 1, three times the true value.

If every directional derivative exists, must the function be continuous? Example 2: the function is 1 between the axis and the parabola, 0 elsewhere. Every ray from the origin starts out in the zero zone. Even a ray hugging the axis stays at zero for a short stretch. So every directional derivative at the origin is zero. Now walk in along a flatter parabola instead. Along it the value is 1 all the way, yet zero at the origin. So it is not continuous at the origin, let alone differentiable. As in chapter 16, episode 2, one curved path beats every straight ray. Moral: differentiability is sufficient, not necessary, for directional derivatives. And no number of directional derivatives brings continuity back.

Without differentiability, the formula itself can go wrong. Bring back the troublemaker from the chain rule episode. Both partials at the origin vanish, so the formula predicts zero. But along the diagonal, the true slope is about 0.35, not zero. Partials alone can't predict other directions; that takes differentiability.

Back in the good case, the formula is a dot product. The second vector is just the direction we chose. The first depends only on the function and the point: the gradient. Definition 2 gives this vector its name: the gradient. So the directional derivative is gradient dot direction. With length one, it's the gradient's size times a cosine. The course text concludes: largest at angle zero, smallest when reversed.

Back on our hill, the gradient at our point is one arrow. Now dial the direction once around the circle. The directional derivative is the gradient's shadow on that direction. Line up with the gradient: the top reading, about 2.24, the fastest climb. At a right angle, walking across the slope, the reading is zero. Turn fully around: straight downhill, the lowest reading. Every bearing in between is the top reading times a cosine. So the winner wasn't southwest: it is the gradient's bearing.

Example 3: find the gradient of a function in space. The three partials there come out as 1, minus 3, minus 3. So the gradient's length is the root of 19. The fastest increase runs along the gradient, at rate root 19.

Now walk along the level curve through our point. Height never changes there, so the tangent direction reads zero. A zero reading means a right angle: gradient and tangent are perpendicular. The fastest climb wastes nothing sideways along the curve.

Lift the map back into the hill it describes. Each level curve is the hill sliced at one height. On the map, gradient arrows cross every level curve at right angles. Where curves crowd, arrows grow: arrow length is steepness. It's the contour map reading every hiker already knows.

For two variables, just drop the third term. For three variables, the gradient is normal to the level surface. The equations built on that come in chapter 18, episode 4.

4 · Outlook

Fill the map with gradient arrows: every one points uphill. Which way climbs fastest? Follow the arrow. How fast? Read the arrow's length. Those are the runner's two questions, both answered.

Use one: rain on the hill runs off the steepest way down. Use two: on a dorm heat map, the gradient points where it warms fastest. Walk along a level curve instead, and the temperature holds steady.

All of this ran on the differentiability of chapter 17, episode 1. Chapter 18, episode 4 makes the gradient the normal of level surfaces. Chapter 18, episode 5 finds constrained optima where level curves touch. And in chapter 23, the gradient becomes one row of a matrix derivative.

Outlook: machine learning trains models by walking against a loss's gradient. Gradient descent is the reverse setting of our dial, run millions of times. Every bearing has a slope; one arrow sums them all up.
