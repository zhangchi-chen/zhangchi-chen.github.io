12-01 Watch the partial sums, not the single terms

Watch the partial sums, not the single terms

1 · Motivation

Watch the running totals, not the single terms. A shopping list that never ends: plus one, minus one, again and again. One classmate pairs them up, and every pair cancels to zero. Another keeps the first one aside, pairs the rest, and gets one. Same terms, same order, and yet two different totals. So which one is the real sum? Adding infinitely many numbers has no meaning yet. That is the real problem.

2 · Statements

A calculator only ever adds finitely many numbers.
Picture the cafeteria checkout screen while friends keep adding dishes.
The screen never shows one dish; it shows the running total.
The running total after n items is the partial sum Sₙ.
Definition 1 gives the endless sum its formal name.
uₙ is the general term; the whole chain is the series.
Definition 2: the series converges exactly when its partial sums do.
So the sum S is a limit, nothing more and nothing less.
If the partial sums diverge, the series diverges and has no sum.
Our list's totals go 1, 0, 1, 0, …: the series diverges.
So the puzzle never had an answer: this series has no sum.
First real example: the geometric series a + aq + aq² + ⋯.
For q ≠ 1 its partial sum has a closed form.
If |q| < 1, then qⁿ → 0 and Sₙ → a/(1 − q).
Check: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ⋯ fills the unit bar exactly.
If |q| ≥ 1, the partial sums never settle: divergent.
Second example: 1/(1·2) + 1/(2·3) + 1/(3·4) + ⋯.
Key move: split each term as 1/n − 1/(n+1), and neighbours cancel.
What survives: Sₙ = 1 − 1/(n+1), which tends to 1.
The series converges, and its sum is exactly 1.
Series and sequences are two views of one object.
From series to sequence: take the partial sums.
From sequence to series: use the differences a₁, a₂ − a₁, a₃ − a₂, …
Its partial sums telescope right back to aₙ.
So every question about a series is a question about a sequence.

A calculator only ever adds finitely many numbers. Picture the cafeteria checkout screen while your friends keep adding dishes. The screen never shows one dish. It shows the running total. The running total after n items is called the partial sum. Definition one gives the endless sum its formal name. The n th term is the general term; the whole chain is the series.

Definition two: the series converges exactly when its partial sums converge. So the sum is a limit, nothing more and nothing less. If the partial sums diverge, the series diverges, and there is no sum. Our list's totals just hop between one and zero, so the series diverges. So the puzzle never had an answer. This series simply has no sum.

First real example: the geometric series. When the ratio is not one, the partial sum has a ready formula. When the ratio is less than one in size, the sums converge. Each term half the one before, and the total is exactly one. If the ratio is at least one in size, the partial sums never settle.

Second example: each term is one over a product of neighbours. Here is the key move: split every term, and neighbours cancel in pairs. Only the first and last pieces survive, and the totals head to one. The series converges, and its sum is exactly one.

Series and sequences are two views of the same object. From series to sequence: take the running totals. And back again: line up the steps between neighbours as a series. Its running totals fold right back into the original sequence. So every question about a series is really about a sequence.

3 · Method

But a formula for the partial sum is rare luck. Usually there is none. We need a test that never asks where the limit is. Theorem twelve point one: the Cauchy criterion for series. That block is just the gap between two partial sums. So this is the Cauchy criterion for sequences, worn by a series.

Read it like this: go far enough, and every block is small. Picture a window framing a few terms, sliding out along the tail. What must shrink is everything in the window together, not one term. And it must hold however wide the window gets. First you pick a tolerance, then you find how far out to start. Now squeeze the window down to a single term. Then every far-away term is small: the terms must tend to zero.

The corollary: if the series converges, the general term tends to zero. Run it backwards as a five-second check: terms that stay big mean divergence. Our shopping list fails too: plus one, minus one, never shrinking. Exam trap: terms shrinking to zero is only a necessary condition. Small terms come for free. They never buy you convergence.

Now meet the harmonic series. View one: the terms do shrink to zero, so the corollary has nothing to say. View two: let the Cauchy criterion frame one block. Each term in the block is at least as big as the last one. So the whole block adds up to at least one half. However far out you go, some block still adds up to at least one half. The course text's Example three says it in one line. With a tolerance of one half, the criterion fails, and the harmonic series diverges.

View three: cut the first sixteen terms into groups that double in size. In each group the last term is smallest, so every group gives at least one half. Every doubling adds at least another half to the total. The totals march past every bound, while each term fades away. But slowly. Passing four takes thirty-one terms; passing ten takes over twelve thousand.

Back to the opening puzzle, with one more fact. Theorem twelve point four: brackets in a convergent series keep the same sum. But put brackets on a divergent series, and an answer can appear out of nowhere. Each friend kept only half of the partial sums: all zeros, or all ones. Whether it converges is settled by the partial sums, before any bracket moves.

4 · Outlook

Use one: what does point nine repeating mean? It is a geometric series with ratio one tenth, and its sum is exactly one. Every infinite decimal is a limit of partial sums, our definition at work. Use two: a program that stops adding as soon as the terms get tiny. On the harmonic series it prints about nineteen, a sum that does not exist. The patch: tiny terms never prove convergence. Watch the totals instead.

Looking back a chapter: improper integrals asked the same question about the far tail. The two Cauchy criteria are twins, and they meet again in episode four. Next: to prove convergence, we need benchmarks to compare against. The harmonic series is the first benchmark, on the divergent side. Later it returns word for word as the uniform Cauchy criterion.

One last thought: defining the sum as a limit of partial sums is a choice. Later courses meet Cesàro summation, which gives our list the value one half. Ordinary analysis declines that value and sticks with the partial sums. Watch the running totals, not the single terms.
