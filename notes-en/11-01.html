11-01 Infinite integrals: the area bill of an endless tail

Infinite integrals: the area bill of an endless tail

1 · Motivation

Infinite integrals, and the area bill of a tail that never ends. A rocket wants to leave Earth and never come back. Gravity keeps pulling back, weaker and weaker, but never zero. Each short stretch costs a little work, like the elements of the physics episode.

Add up the pieces from the surface out to a far wall. Push the wall out forever, and the total settles on a finite number. The road never ends, but the bill does.

Pay the bill with the energy of the launch. Swap in surface gravity, and the needed speed appears. About eleven point two kilometres a second, the escape velocity. So when can an endless trip cost only a finite amount?

Picture a subscription whose monthly charge keeps getting smaller. Shrink fast enough, and the total you ever pay is finite. Shrink too slowly, and the bill keeps climbing forever.

Step one: an ordinary definite integral, from a up to a wall. Let the wall move, and this is chapter nine's variable limit integral. Step two: let the wall run off toward infinity. If those values settle down on one number, that number is the answer. No new kind of integration happens; a limit of familiar things gets a name.

Definition one: the function lives on an endless ray. and integrable on every finite stretch of it. If those integrals settle on one limit as u grows forever, then that limit is called the improper integral over the infinite interval, the infinite integral for short, with this compact notation, and we say the integral converges; if there is no limit, the integral is called divergent.

That infinity on top is a reminder of a limit, not a shortcut around it. Infinity cannot be plugged in; the wall can only keep moving. The running total can settle, climb forever, or keep swinging. Only settling down counts as convergent; the other two are divergent. So the rocket's bill is a convergent infinite integral. Two steps, always in this order: integrate to the wall, then let it go.

3 · Method

Now the key family: one over a power of x. Picture a shelf of phone batteries, each fading at its own pace. When p is not one, chapter nine's Newton Leibniz formula does it at once. Everything rides on one term, that power of u at the far end. If p is above one, that power fades away to nothing. The integral converges, to a clean finite value. Below one, that power runs away, and the integral diverges. Exactly one gets its own line: a logarithm that never stops climbing.

So the family is decided: above one it converges, to a tidy value, and at one or below, it diverges. Turn up p, and the curve dives toward the axis sooner. Faster decay means a smaller tail: two, then one, then one half. The flattest batteries never finish; their total keeps piling up. The dividing line sits exactly at one. The logarithm crawls, needing twenty two thousand to reach ten.

Here is the same family in disguise, a logarithm tucked inside. Let t be the logarithm: the gluing substitution from chapter eight. The limits move along with the variable, as in chapter nine. It turns back into the same family, so it converges exactly above one. The logarithm squeezes the axis, but the measuring stick is unchanged.

For a nonnegative function, the integral measures an endless region. Convergence: the region never ends, but its area is finite. For a nonnegative function, the running area can only grow. So here divergence just means infinite area, and that is a real answer.

When the sign keeps changing, divergence can also mean endless swinging. The classic slip: writing a number, like zero, beside a divergent integral. The honest line just says divergent; there is no value to report.

With infinity at both ends, cut the line at any point into two halves; it converges only when both halves converge, each on its own. Two walls run off separately; neither waits for the other. Where you cut changes nothing, neither convergence nor the value. Move the cut, and a finite piece simply changes sides; the total is untouched.

Try this gentle hill, cut right at zero. The right half is an arctangent, climbing to half of pi. The left half mirrors it: the whole hill holds pi. Cut at one: the pieces change, the total is still pi.

4 · Outlook

First use: a capacitor drains, its current fading but never quite stopping. Yet the total charge that ever flows is finite. And it equals exactly the charge stored at the start, not a bit less. Second use: pull a charge away from another, all the way to infinity. Another finite bill, and physics calls it potential energy.

Everything today ran on old tools from chapters eight and nine. Some tails won't yield to direct computation; episode eleven three builds rulers. Others explode at a finite point instead, which is the next episode's job.

Later, probability densities are nonnegative curves with total area one. The bell curve from chapter eight returns; its area waits for a later trick. Every distribution's normalizing constant is an infinite integral. An endless tail can carry a finite area, as long as it fades fast enough.
