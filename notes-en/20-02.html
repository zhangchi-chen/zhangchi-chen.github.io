20-02 Line integrals of the second type: work done along a path

Line integrals of the second type: work done along a path

1 · Motivation

Line integrals of the second type, and the work done along a path. Last time we weighed a wire, and the direction never mattered. Today a force pushes a small particle along that very curve. Picture a courier on a delivery round, on a campus of one way streets. The odometer only ever adds, whichever way the street is driven. But the parcel balance cares a great deal about direction. So does work care about the route we take, or the way we walk it?

Start small, with a constant force over one short straight step. The work of this step is the dot product of force and step. Split the step into two shadows, one on each axis. So the work splits into one piece per axis. Signed components carry the story here, not lengths. The projection habit from the gradient episode comes back.

2 · Statements

Here is the definition, as the course text states it.
Chop the directed curve from A to B at points Mᵢ.
Each little step has signed projections Δxᵢ and Δyᵢ.
On each small arc, pick a point (ξᵢ, ηᵢ) and read P, Q there.
Add up P Δxᵢ + Q Δyᵢ over all the steps.
If the limit exists, independent of the partition and the points,
it is called the line integral of the second type.
It is also called the line integral with respect to coordinates.
Compare type one: Δsᵢ is a length, always positive.
Now walk the same curve backwards, from B to A.
Every Δxᵢ and Δyᵢ changes sign.
So the whole integral changes sign.
The course text states the orientation property like this.
Type one weighs, type two works: same curve, opposite habits.
Drive a street backwards: mileage still adds, parcels flip sign.
Careful: dx here is not a length; it carries a sign.

Here is the definition, word for word. Chop the directed curve into small steps, from start to end. Each little step has two signed projections. On each small arc, pick a point and read the force there. Add up the work of every little step. If the limit exists, no matter how we cut and where we sample, it is called the line integral of the second type. It is also called the line integral with respect to coordinates. Compare type one, where every piece is a length, always positive.

Now walk the very same curve backwards. Every projection switches its sign. So the whole integral flips its sign. The course text puts it like this. Type one weighs, type two works; same curve, opposite habits. Drive a street the other way: mileage still adds, parcels flip sign. Careful, the little increment here carries a sign; it is not a length.

3 · Method

To compute, we first describe the curve by a parameter. Step one, feed the curve into the force itself. Step two, each increment becomes velocity times time. And that gives the course text's computation formula. The bottom limit marks the start, the top limit the end. The bottom limit may be the larger one; that is where direction lives. Type one always counts upward; type two follows the walk. Check it: reversing the walk swaps the limits, and the integral flips.

Drill one: one integrand, the same two endpoints, two routes. Route one is the straight segment, walked from 0 to 1. Substitute, and it all collapses into one small polynomial. Along the segment, the work is twenty five sixths. Route two bends along a parabola, and x itself serves as parameter. Shift the variable, and again it all becomes a polynomial. Along the parabola, the work is ten thirds. Same endpoints, and yet the work is different. When work ignores the path is a question for a later episode.

Drill two, a new integrand, put to three tests. Along the parabola, it all comes down to a work of 2. Along the straight line, the work is 2 once more. Now a closed loop: right, then up, then straight back home. The pieces give zero, two and minus two, so the loop gives zero. Every route we tried gave the same work. Two data points, one open mystery, and no theory yet.

A curve that comes back to where it began is a closed curve. Its integral gets an integral sign with a little circle. Start anywhere, follow the direction around, and add the pieces. Back to drill one, this time around a triangle. Along the bottom edge, the work is three halves. Up the right edge, the work cancels out to zero. The last edge is a return trip, like sending a parcel back for a refund. It is the old segment walked backwards, so its work just flips. In total, minus eight thirds. The sign flip saved us a whole edge of work.

Space curves work the same way, with one more term for height. Now take this force along one full turn of a helix. Describe the helix by the angle turned, one full turn. The first two terms shrink to a constant drag that always pushes back. Add the height term, and that is the work along the helix. Now go straight up instead, between the same two endpoints. Only the height term survives, giving a new value. Same endpoints, and once again different work. In vector form, the course text packs it all into one line. Force dotted with each step, added along the walk: that is work.

4 · Outlook

First use: comparing two routes is just walking one loop. Out along one route, back along the other: the loop measures the gap. For drill one, that loop gives minus five sixths. Second use: a constant force only cares where you start and finish. The formula turns it into one big step from start to finish. Looking back, last episode weighed the curve, and today it did work. The sign flip divides them, and the limits are where direction is kept.

Looking ahead, a loop integral will turn out to see the region inside. That is Green's theorem, in the next chapter. A field whose work never depends on the path is called conservative. Type one weighs, type two works, so always watch the sign.
