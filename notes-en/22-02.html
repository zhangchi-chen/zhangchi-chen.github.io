22-02 Sides of a surface: the warning of the Möbius band

Sides of a surface: the warning of the Möbius band

1 · Motivation

Sides of a surface, and the warning a twisted band gives us. Picture your desk fan, blowing straight at a badminton racket. So how much air gets through the strings every second? Your roommate counts air moving left to right, and gets plus three. You count right to left, and get minus three. Nobody slipped. You simply picked opposite meanings of through.

But a bare surface comes with no arrow. So before any flux integral, we have to choose a side. Last episode, weighing a curved shell never asked about sides. Mass is mass, whichever side you look from. Flux is different. Flip the side, and the answer flips sign. But here is the catch: can every surface be given a side?

2 · Statements

Back at M₀, the same direction: S is two-sided.
Had it come back opposite, S would be one-sided.
Two-sided means every such loop brings the normal home unchanged.
One loop that flips it is enough for one-sided.

It returns still pointing outward: two-sided.
Test two: the same walk on a band with a half twist.

Same walk, opposite ending: the Möbius band is one-sided.
No jump anywhere: the normal tips over a little at every step.

For two sided surfaces, the course text fixes which side is positive. For a graph surface, the upper side counts as positive. Upper side means the normal leans upward, at an acute angle to the vertical axis. For a closed surface, the outer side is positive, the inner one negative.

3 · Method

Why would an integral care about sides? Look at shadows. When the normal leans up, its shadow on the floor counts positive. Turn the patch to face away, and the same shadow counts negative. The other two shadows get their signs the same way, from the other two axes.

Chop the surface into small pieces. Each one casts three signed shadows. Weight each shadow by the matching component of the flow, then add. Shrink the pieces, and the limit is the second type surface integral. For a moving fluid, this is the flux through the side you chose.

Now flip to the other side. All three shadows change sign together. So the whole integral simply changes sign. The first type integral never flips. Its area pieces carry no sign. Back in chapter twenty, reversing a curve flipped the line integral. Same drama, one dimension up. This time a whole side flips. Even in chapter ten, going counterclockwise was quietly choosing a side.

Theorem 22.2 says: on the upper side, integrate over the shadow region. Just plug the height of the surface in. On the lower side, it is the same integral with a minus sign.

An example: a quarter of the unit sphere, taken on the outer side. It is not one graph, so cut it at the equator. On the upper piece, outer means upper, so it comes in with a plus. On the lower piece, outer means lower, so it comes in with a minus. And on the lower piece, z itself is negative: a second minus sign. The two minus signs cancel, and the two halves come out equal. Below the equator, negative height times negative shadow comes out positive. Polar coordinates finish the job: two fifteenths. Those two signs are really the whole story here.

For a closed surface, all the normals point outward. That is the positive side. Normals pointing inward mark the inner side, the negative one. Gauss's formula will stand on exactly this convention.

First trap: giving the pieces of one surface mismatched sides. Correct: upper piece taken up, lower piece taken down, total two fifteenths. Wrong: take both pieces from above, and the halves cancel to zero. Second trap: the other side flips all three terms together. Flipping just the last term is the classic slip.

4 · Outlook

Back at the racket: once a side is chosen, there is one answer. Heat leaking through your dorm wall in winter needs a side first, too. In 3D graphics, a mesh whose normals all agree has chosen a side. One sided surfaces lead into topology, to shapes like the Klein bottle. And Stokes's formula will pair a side with a direction around its edge, by the right hand rule. The bridge between the two types, with its direction cosines, waits for a later episode. So before any flux integral, ask the twisted band's question: which side?
