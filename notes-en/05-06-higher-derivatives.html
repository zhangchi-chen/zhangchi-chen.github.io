Motivation

  1. Higher derivatives: differentiate, then queue again.
  2. The homework asks for the 100th derivative of x²eˣ.
  3. By hand, that is a hundred rounds of the product rule.
  4. Round one gives eˣ(x² + 2x).
  5. Two more rounds, and the bracket keeps changing.
  6. Down the columns: 2, 4, 6 and 0, 2, 6 — a pattern?
  7. But guessing the hundredth row is a gamble.
  8. Puzzle two: x² sin(1/x), squeezed flat near 0.
  9. It is differentiable everywhere, yet f″(0) does not exist.
  10. Puzzle three: x|x| looks like one smooth S-curve.
  11. Its first derivative is fine; at 0 the second is missing.
  12. Today's question: what does it take to differentiate again?

What the statement says

  1. Picture d/dx as one cafeteria counter.
  2. A function goes in on a tray; its derivative comes out.
  3. Second derivative: take what came out, and queue again.
  4. Each pass fully differentiates whatever came out last.
  5. So f⁽ⁿ⁾ is the same counter, passed n times.
  6. The course text's definition of the second derivative:
  7. and the n-th derivative is built from the (n−1)-th.
  8. Clause one: f′ itself must be differentiable at x₀.
  9. Take f = sin x: its slopes trace out cos x.
  10. f″(x₀) is the slope of the f′ graph at x₀.
  11. A slope of slopes: here f″(x₀) = −sin x₀.
  12. Clause two: the same difference quotient, now built from f′.
  13. Hidden demand: f′ must exist near x₀, not only at x₀.
  14. Clause three: each order is defined from the one before.
  15. Notation dⁿy/dxⁿ: apply d/dx, n times.
  16. Read it as one symbol, not as a fraction.
  17. The differential d²y is another object; 05-09 handles it.
  18. Now send three classics through the counter.
  19. x³ comes out as 3x², 6x, 6, then 0 forever.
  20. Each pass brings one exponent down: 3·2·1 = 3! = 6.
  21. In general xⁿ reaches n! after n passes, then 0.
  22. sin x turns a quarter of a dial on each pass.
  23. cos x, −sin x, −cos x — four passes bring it home.
  24. Why a quarter turn? cos x is sin x shifted left by π/2.
  25. So sin⁽ⁿ⁾x = sin(x + nπ/2): n quarter turns.
  26. cos x rides the same dial, one quarter ahead.
  27. eˣ is the fixed point: no pass changes it.
  28. The course text records all three in one line each.

Method / proof core

  1. Back to products: what do n passes do to uv?
  2. One pass: each term differentiates exactly one factor.
  3. Two passes: four paths, each choosing u or v twice.
  4. The two mixed paths land on one term: 2u′v′.
  5. Three passes: eight paths, merging as 1, 3, 3, 1.
  6. A term with v⁽ᵏ⁾: choose which k of the n passes hit v.
  7. There are C(n,k) such choices: that is the coefficient.
  8. Now put the binomial theorem right above it.
  9. Same shape: powers have become orders of derivatives.
  10. Expanding (u + v)ⁿ picks u or v from each bracket.
  11. Differentiating n times picks u or v at each pass.
  12. Same counting, so the same C(n,k).
  13. This is the Leibniz formula.
  14. Careful: u⁽⁰⁾ means u itself, not 1.
  15. Check n = 4: the coefficients read 1, 4, 6, 4, 1.
  16. Back to the homework: (x²eˣ)⁽ⁿ⁾.
  17. Put x² in the v slot: from the third pass on, it is 0.
  18. eˣ sits in the u slot and never changes.
  19. In the full sum, only k = 0, 1, 2 survive.
  20. The survivors add up to eˣ(x² + 2nx + n(n − 1)).
  21. Check: n = 1, 2, 3 give back our three rounds.
  22. And the 100th: eˣ(x² + 200x + 9900), in one line.
  23. Tip: give the dying polynomial the v slot, so the sum stays short.
  24. Now we pay 05-02's debt: puzzle two.
  25. Set f(x) = x² sin(1/x) for x ≠ 0, and f(0) = 0.
  26. The graph is trapped between x² and −x².
  27. At 0 the quotient h sin(1/h) is squeezed to 0: f′(0) = 0.
  28. Away from 0, product and chain rules give f′(x).
  29. The first term fades; the second keeps swinging.
  30. Arbitrarily close to 0, f′ hits both 1 and −1.
  31. So f′ has no limit at 0; it is discontinuous there.
  32. If f″(0) existed, f′ would be continuous at 0 (05-02).
  33. It isn't, so f″(0) does not exist.
  34. Two roommates: one always calm, one wild only at midnight.
  35. The calm one tames the wild one for one pass, not two.
  36. Puzzle three: x|x| is x² on the right, −x² on the left.
  37. First order by the definition: the quotient is |h|, so f′(0) = 0.
  38. Altogether f′(x) = 2|x|: a V with a corner at 0.
  39. Second order: back to the definition, this time with f′.
  40. The quotient 2|h|/h is 2 on the right, −2 on the left.
  41. Two different one-sided limits: f″(0) does not exist.
  42. The blind shortcut: differentiate the right piece twice, get 2.
  43. That 2 is only the right-hand half of the story.
  44. Rule: at a join, every new order goes back to the definition.
  45. Each step uses quotients of the previous derivative.

Applications and outlook

  1. Where it shows up: the dorm lift, height h = t²/2 metres.
  2. h′ = t is its speed; h″ = 1 m/s² is its acceleration.
  3. Your stomach feels the second derivative, not the speed.
  4. Back to the dial: two quarter turns flip the sign, sin″ = −sin.
  5. The simplest spring obeys y″ = −y, and sine fits.
  6. 06-07's Taylor formula is built from these values f⁽ᵏ⁾(x₀).
  7. Counting surviving passes sorts functions into classes C¹, C², …
  8. x|x| is in C¹ but stops short of C².
  9. x² sin(1/x) is differentiable, yet not even C¹.
  10. In function series: may d/dx pass through an infinite sum, again and again?
  11. One counter, queue again: that is every higher derivative.
  12. Leibniz's coefficients count which passes hit which factor.
  13. And at a join, every order goes back to the definition.
