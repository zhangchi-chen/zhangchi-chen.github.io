Techniques for definite integrals: change the limits with the variable, and let symmetry halve the work

1 · Motivation

Definite integrals today: the limits move with the variable, and symmetry halves the work. Tonight's homework is one innocent looking definite integral. The square root begs for a sine substitution. First try: the integrand changes, but the old limits stay put. The calculator says roughly 0.727.

Yet the picture is simply a quarter of a circle of radius one. Its area is about 0.785, so the first answer must be wrong. That number measures the wrong stretch of t. As the old variable goes zero to one, the new one goes a quarter turn. The rule of the day: new variable, new limits. And some integrals will not even need an antiderivative.

2 · Statements

Theorem 9.12: substitution in definite integrals.
First, f is continuous on [a, b]: the function we integrate.
Second, φ′ is integrable on [α, β], the new variable's interval.
Third, φ(α) = a and φ(β) = b: the endpoints land on the endpoints.
Fourth, φ([α, β]) ⊆ [a, b]: x never leaves the interval of f.
Then integrand, differential and limits all change at once.
A definite integral is a number: compute one side and you're done.
Flashback to 09-03: both sides equal the same number F(b) − F(a).
Theorem 9.13: integration by parts for definite integrals.
u v|ₐᵇ simply means the value of uv at b minus its value at a.
The moves of 08-02, 08-03 and 08-04 now come with limits attached.
And one strange formula we will earn by the end: the Wallis formula.
π/2 appears out of nothing but even and odd whole numbers.

Here is the substitution theorem for definite integrals. First, the function we integrate is continuous on the old interval. Second, the derivative of the substitution can be integrated. Third, the new endpoints land exactly on the old ones. Fourth, along the way the old variable stays inside the old interval. Three things change together: the integrand, the differential, the limits. The payoff: the answer is a number, so there is no trip back to x. Flashback to the Newton Leibniz formula: both sides give the same number.

And here is integration by parts, now with limits attached. It simply means the right end's value minus the left end's value. The tricks from chapter eight are back, now carrying limits. And a strange formula we will earn at the end: the Wallis formula. Pi appears, built from nothing but whole numbers.

3 · Method

Now redo it properly, with the new limits from the start. On this stretch cosine is never negative, so the root opens cleanly. The root and the differential merge into cosine squared. Split off a wave whose positive and negative parts cancel. What is left is a quarter of pi, exactly the quarter disc. And notice we never switched back to the old variable.

Your selfie camera quietly flips left and right. An even function is its own mirror image across the vertical axis. So compute one half and simply double it. An odd function's graph is symmetric about the origin. The left half is the right half with a minus sign, so they cancel. One substitution flips the left half onto the right, limits and all. In this example, the scary odd part simply disappears. The even part doubles a third into two thirds, and we are done.

On the campus shuttle, a full loop takes the same time from any stop. For a periodic function, any full period holds the same area. The slice you lose on the left comes back on the right. One hundred identical humps, each of area two: two hundred.

On the walk back from the library, every uphill turns into a downhill. Now walk the same stretch backwards, from the far end to zero. Read backwards, sine becomes cosine, so the two integrals match. The two tile a rectangle in equal halves, and each half is our homework. Pairing each point with its mirror, x may be replaced by the midpoint. Quick check: the answer is pi, and integration by parts agrees.

Here is the course text's own showpiece integral. Its antiderivative is not elementary, yet the exact value is within reach. A tangent substitution clears the denominator and moves the top limit. One trig identity, and the logarithm breaks into three pieces. Walk this stretch backwards and the two cosine logs trade places. The cosine terms cancel, and only a constant survives. About 0.272, found without ever touching an antiderivative.

With limits, the constant shows up at both ends and cancels itself. Let the logarithm be differentiated and the power integrated. At the bottom the logarithm is zero, so only the top end counts. Subtract the leftover piece and land at about 4.57. The two pieces tile one rectangle: the picture behind the formula.

Now the powers of sine: the higher the power, the lower the curve. One round of parts, and the boundary piece vanishes at both ends. Swap the cosine square away, and the original integral comes back. Solve for it, and each integral reaches down two steps at a time. Both staircases end on easy floors: a rectangle and a sine hump. Even powers keep a factor of pi; odd powers give plain fractions. Walk it backwards once more, and the cosine powers give the same values. Since sine sits between zero and one, higher powers are smaller. Integrate all three, and half of pi gets trapped between two ratios. The two bounds close in on each other, and the Wallis formula drops out.

Here is the order: symmetry first, then reversal, then the heavy tools. Each integral from today lands on its own branch of this chart. Only when all of these fail do we open the table.

4 · Outlook

First use: the same sine substitution hands us the area of an ellipse. Second use: a squared wave averages one half over any full cycle. So the 220 volt socket in your dorm actually peaks near 311 volts. Third use: a symmetric bell balances exactly on its axis.

Every move today will work again for the improper integrals of chapter eleven. This staircase grows into the Gamma function's product formulas later on. Where antiderivatives give up, symmetry often still wins. Later, the probability and series chapters solve whole families like this. So: new variable, new limits, and always look for symmetry first.
