06-07 Taylor's formula, part one: a polynomial doing an impression

Taylor's formula, part one: a polynomial doing an impression

1 · Motivation

Taylor's formula, part one: a polynomial doing an impression. Here is an old limit from chapter three that never got settled. Swap the sine for x, as we used to, and the answer comes out zero. Three rounds of L'Hôpital give minus one sixth instead. Two methods, two answers, so one of them is lying.

A quick numerical check settles it: the ratio hugs minus one sixth. Substitution lied; it threw away the one piece that decides the answer. That swap is the tangent line at zero standing in for the curve.

Recall the differential: the tangent line plus something of higher order. The tangent copies where the curve is and where it heads, but never how it bends. Its error is smaller than the distance to the point, and that is all it promises. When the bending matters, the copy has to match more than direction.

Picture karaoke night, and a classmate doing an impression of a famous singer. Nailing the first note is like the tangent: right place, right direction. Breath and vibrato are the higher orders: the bend, and how the bend changes. Near that note, the audience simply cannot tell the two voices apart. Whatever difference the audience can still hear is called the remainder.

Here is the demand: one polynomial of degree n, glued to f near the point. Its error must die faster than the n th power of the distance. Match the value and every derivative up to the top order: one more condition than the degree. And the polynomial has exactly that many coefficients to spend.

3 · Method

Why does the theorem hold? Watch one ratio. Top and bottom both vanish at the point, along with all their lower derivatives. So L'Hôpital can run round after round, one short of the top order. But the last step can't be another round; the top derivative lives only at that point. Instead, the very definition of that derivative closes the argument: the ratio goes to zero. The proof spends exactly what the theorem assumed, not a drop more.

Back to the old limit; now we can see what the swap threw away. Expand the sine all the way to its cubic term. Subtract x: the big terms cancel, and the cubic piece is left standing. It's like splitting a dinner bill: big items cancel, and the small change decides. Divide by the cube: the limit is minus one sixth, and L'Hôpital was right. The swap threw away exactly the term that survives the cancellation.

Rule one: inside products and quotients, the small tails may be dropped. Rule two: inside sums, keep expanding until something survives the cancellation. Substitution keeps a single term; Taylor keeps as many as the problem demands. Taylor's formula is substitution in its complete form, and chapter three's debt is paid.

Now a trap: could we promise one order more, a fifth order tail? The true error behaves like a fifth power over one hundred twenty. Divide by the fifth power, and the ratio refuses to reach zero. So the fifth order claim is false; fourth order is honest. A remainder is a precise claim, never a sloppy and so on.

Note two: only one polynomial of degree n can do this job. So any valid expansion, however you found it, is the Taylor expansion. Read it backwards: each derivative at zero is a factorial times a coefficient. The course text's example: plug a composite into the exponential row. Only even powers appear, and not a single derivative was computed. Want the ninety eighth derivative at zero? Just read one coefficient. Multiply by ninety eight factorial; the answer is negative. And every odd derivative at zero vanishes, since odd powers never appear.

4 · Outlook

First use: the equivalence borrowed last episode comes straight from this line. Second use: a limit where two expansions must race to the fourth power. The first two terms cancel, and the fourth powers decide: minus one twelfth. Third use, quick estimates: the cosine of 0.1 is about 0.995. It is very close, but the little o never says how close.

Little o says eventually small, never how small; the Lagrange remainder will price it. Looking ahead, with error control added, calculators compute sines and logs this very way. And this very table returns in full, much later, when power series arrive. Match n derivatives, and near that note, the impression is flawless.
