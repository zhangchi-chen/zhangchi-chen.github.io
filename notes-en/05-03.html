05-03 Sum, product, quotient: the extra little patch of area

Sum, product, quotient: the extra little patch of area

1 · Motivation

Sums, products, quotients, and the little patch of area behind them. Tonight's homework is a single product to differentiate. The tempting move: differentiate each factor, then multiply. At the point one, the shortcut says two.

But the graph disagrees: right there, the tangent lies flat. A slope of two would cut steeply across instead. The safe road is slower: multiply it all out first. Term by term, and at the point one the answer is zero. Zero, not two. The tempting move is simply wrong.

And a product like this one can never be multiplied out. We need a real rule for products. Picture a dorm room where the rent is split by floor area. Measured again, each side turns out a little longer. A quick guess: just multiply the two small extras. The truth is ninety one times bigger than the guess. So where did all that extra area hide? That's our question.

Theorem 5.4: for sums and differences, derivatives just add or subtract. In plain words, when two pieces change, the total changes by both. Theorem 5.5, the product rule: the two factors take turns. In one term only the first factor moves; in the other, only the second.

Theorem 5.6, the quotient rule: the bottom must not be zero. The minus sign and the square underneath look random. We will earn both. Here is the course text's list, all the rules side by side. Two special cases hide inside: a constant factor, and a reciprocal.

Each rule talks about one single point, nothing more. The only thing assumed: both functions are differentiable at that point. The quotient rule asks one extra thing: the bottom stays away from zero.

3 · Method

The sum rule comes free: split the fraction, and limit arithmetic finishes it. Sums were the warm-up. Products are the real story. At the start, the area is simply one side times the other. Nudge x a little, and both sides grow a little. All the extra area lives in this L-shaped border.

First, a thin strip down the right side. Then a second strip along the top. And one tiny tile left in the corner. So the extra area is two strips plus a corner. Divide by the step: each strip becomes a rate.

Here comes the dangerous step: does that corner tile really vanish? Last episode pays off: differentiable means continuous, so the extra height shrinks away. So the corner is negligible: it shrinks faster than the step itself. Shrink the step: only the two strips survive. That's the product rule. So your extra rent is the two strips; the corner is pocket change. Forget the formula? Sketch the rectangle and read it straight off.

Now the classic mistake: multiply the two derivatives. It's the roommate who pays for the corner tile and walks away. First test, both factors x: the true answer is two. The mistake says one. Half the answer just went missing. Where did it go? Into the square's two strips.

Second test, now with a sine: the product rule says negative pi. The mistake says negative one. Wrong again. The graph settles it: near pi the curve falls about three times faster. The reflex forgets both strips, and the strips are the whole answer.

Quotients next, and we won't memorize anything: rewrite it as a product. Then only one new piece is missing: the reciprocal. When the bottom grows, its reciprocal shrinks. That's the minus sign, right there. Put it over one denominator, and the minus sign shows up on its own. Continuity pulls the bottom to a safe, non-zero value. That's the reciprocal rule, minus sign included. Sanity check with the simplest bottom: it matches the power rule exactly.

Now the product rule stitches the two pieces together. One common denominator later, the whole quotient rule falls out. That minus sign in the numerator? It came from the reciprocal, not from magic. So the minus always rides with the bottom's derivative. Two steps, one new idea, and nothing to memorize twice.

Back to tonight's homework, armed with the product rule. Two terms, one for each strip, and no fifth-degree expansion needed. Plug in one: the two strips cancel to zero, just like the safe road.

Second exhibit: the tangent function, which no one can multiply out. The quotient rule gives a numerator we've seen a thousand times. The old friend equals one. Three lines, done. The graph agrees: gentle in the middle, steeper and steeper toward the edges.

And the product we couldn't expand at the start? One step. Constants just ride along: a side that never grows adds no strip. Make it a habit: pull the constant out first, and save a step every time.

4 · Outlook

First use: the class T-shirt order, with a setup fee and a price per shirt. The average cost per shirt is a quotient. The quotient rule says the average only ever goes down. In the picture, the average is the slope from the origin, and it keeps flattening. Each extra shirt costs less than the average, so the average drops.

Second use: paying abroad, where cost is price times exchange rate. Both drift: one strip from the price, one from the rate. Price up two percent, rate up one percent: cost up about three. Exactly, it's a hair more, and that hair is the corner tile.

Remember: last episode's continuity is what made the corner vanish. Next episode adds the chain rule and inverse functions, and the toolbox is complete. After that, the derivative table is just a lookup. Repeat the product rule many times, and it comes back as the Leibniz formula.

Enjoy this picture: it's the last time a derivative rule is this easy to see. From the chain rule on, the pictures turn into gears and trees. And when partial derivatives arrive, the product rule survives untouched. Because along any one direction, it's the very same rectangle. Remember the room: the two strips count, and the corner fades away.
