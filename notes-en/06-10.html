Convexity and inflection: the shape of a function

1 · Motivation

Convexity and inflection: the shape of a function. Two graphs climb from the same start to the same finish. Both rise all the way, so their derivative signs match exactly. Yet one bends up, and the other bends down. The first derivative tells up or down, but not the bend.

Picture a cup that holds water, and a dome that lets it run off. In the course text, the cup that holds water is called convex. Careful: some English books use the opposite word for this shape. And the dome is concave: water runs off it. Today we pin down the bend, learn to detect it, and find where it flips.

3 · Method

Why are chords and tangents one story? Watch the slopes. The lemma: convex exactly when the left chord's slope never beats the right one's. From the left chord to the right chord, the slope climbs. Slide the middle point around, and the order never breaks. Squeeze the short chords at either end, and they turn into tangents. Left tangent, chord, right tangent: slopes in rising order, so the derivative increases. And the left half, rearranged, is exactly the tangent below statement. That's the sketch: three views, tied together by climbing slopes.

Now the engine room: who can quickly tell that the derivative increases? It's the monotonicity dictionary from episode six three, moved one floor up. This theorem: convex exactly when the second derivative never goes negative. This pays episode five nine's second promise: the second differential's sign reads the shape. Episode six nine used it for extrema; here it decides the shape.

Let's try it on the arctangent. Differentiate twice; the bottom stays positive, so the top decides. Left of zero it's a cup; right of zero it's a dome. Convex all the way up to zero, concave from zero onward. Watch the tangent turn upward, then, past zero, turn back down.

There the shape flips, like a spoon turned from bowl up to bowl down. Here is Definition two: the inflection point. Two ingredients: a tangent that crosses, and a genuine shape change. Two theorems: first a necessary condition, then a sufficient one. Careful: a zero second derivative only makes the point a candidate.

First trap: x to the fourth, with zero second derivative at zero. But the second derivative never turns negative: a cup on both sides. No shape change, so no inflection at the origin.

Second trap, from the other side: the cube root of x. At zero there's no derivative at all; the tangent stands straight up. Yet left of zero it's a cup, right of zero a dome: the sign flips. The vertical tangent cuts across the curve, so by Definition two it's an inflection point. List spots where the second derivative is zero or missing, then test for a sign change.

4 · Outlook

Before we sketch, one more scan: where does the graph run off to? Vertical asymptote: the function blows up near one finite point. Horizontal asymptote: far out, the function settles to a fixed height. Oblique asymptote: find the slope first, then the offset. A horizontal asymptote is simply the oblique one with zero slope.

Test run on the course text's example: a cube root of a cubic. Factor first: it splits into a squared factor times a plain one. Defined everywhere, no symmetry, crossing zero at one and minus one. Rise and fall: the only stationary point sits at minus one third. At one and minus one the derivative blows up; the tangents stand vertical. A local maximum at minus one third, and a local minimum of zero at one. Convexity: the second derivative's sign is set by the factor x plus one. A cup left of minus one, a dome right of it: an inflection there. At one it's a dome on both sides: a sharp point, not an inflection. Asymptote: slope one, offset minus one third, at both ends. The payoff: for a convex function, a stationary point is the global minimum. Why? The tangent there is flat, and the curve sits on top of it. No endpoint scan needed: that's why optimization loves convexity.
