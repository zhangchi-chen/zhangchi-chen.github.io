17-06 Unconditional extrema: the quadratic form behind AC − B²

Unconditional extrema: the quadratic form behind AC − B²

1 · Motivation

Today's question: where does a surface reach a peak, or a lowest point? In one variable: find where the slope is flat, then check the bend. That's Fermat's theorem from chapter six, plus the sign of the bend.

Two variables bring three second partials. Who gets the final say? The natural guess: if both axis slices bend upward, we have a minimum. Try it on this innocent-looking function at the origin. Along each axis we get a plain parabola, and both open upward. But along the other diagonal, the parabola flips over. Both walls of the study corner curve in, and still there's no minimum. Two directions aren't enough; we need a test that sees every direction at once.

2 · Statements

First: what exactly is a local maximum or minimum?
Definition: compare f(P) with f(P₀) on a whole neighbourhood.
Only interior points: the whole disc must fit inside the domain.
Example: f = 2x² + y² has a minimum at the origin, a bowl.
Every slice through the origin curves up: f ≥ 0 = f(0, 0).
And g = √(1 − x² − y²) has a maximum at the origin, a dome.
And h = xy: up along one diagonal, down along the other.
It looks like a saddle: neither a maximum nor a minimum.
Suppose P₀ is an extremum point. Freeze y = y₀ and take a slice.
The slice has an extremum at x₀, so Fermat gives f_x(x₀, y₀) = 0.
Freeze x = x₀ instead, and the same argument gives f_y = 0.
Theorem 17.10, the necessary condition: grad f(P₀) = 0.
The gradient from 17-04 shrinks to zero: the tangent plane lies flat.
Points with grad f = 0 are called stationary points.
But a stationary point need not be an extremum point: see h = xy.
So stationary means candidate; deciding needs second-order information.
Collect the second partials into the Hessian (Hesse) matrix.
Continuous second partials make f_xy = f_yx: the matrix is symmetric.
Theorem 17.11, the sufficient condition: read the Hessian's definiteness.
In practice it becomes four rules in A, B, C.
Positive definite: the quadratic form is positive in every nonzero direction.
Negative definite: negative in every direction. Indefinite: both signs occur.

First, let's pin down what a local maximum or minimum really is. The definition compares values over a whole neighbourhood, not along one line. And only interior points: the little disc must fit entirely inside the domain. First example: a bowl, lowest right at the origin. Every slice through the origin bends upward; nothing nearby sits lower. Second example: a dome, highest right at the origin. Third example: along one diagonal it climbs, along the other it falls. It looks like a saddle, and the origin is neither a top nor a bottom.

Suppose there's an extremum here. Freeze one variable and take a slice. That slice is a one-variable function with an extremum, so Fermat flattens it. Freeze the other variable, and the same story flattens the other slope. Here it is officially: Theorem 17 point 10, the necessary condition. The gradient from episode four shrinks to nothing, and the tangent plane lies level. Points where the gradient vanishes get a name: stationary points. Careful: a stationary point is only a candidate, and the saddle shows why. So the gradient only shortlists; to decide, we need second-order information.

Pack the four second partials into one square table: the Hessian matrix. With continuous second partials the two mixed ones agree, so the table is symmetric. Theorem 17 point 11, the sufficient condition, asks whether the Hessian is definite. In practice: four rules from three numbers. Positive definite means the form comes out positive whichever way you step. Negative definite: negative every way. Indefinite: some ways up, some ways down.

3 · Method

Why definiteness? The answer was prepared last episode, in the Taylor formula. At a stationary point, the first-order term vanishes completely. What's left is half a quadratic form, plus a remainder that shrinks faster. Close to the point, the quadratic part outweighs the leftover, so it sets the shape. This is the second differential promised in chapter five; today the debt is paid. It's the one-variable second derivative, upgraded to one value per direction.

Now the key move: read that table as the sign pattern of a quadratic form. Linear algebra recognizes this number: it's the determinant of the Hessian. Complete the square, and that very combination steps out on its own. Determinant positive and A positive: positive definite, a bowl, a local minimum. Determinant positive but A negative: negative definite, a dome, a local maximum. Determinant negative: indefinite. Up one way, down another, so no extremum. Stand on the pass between two dorm hills: climb toward a peak, or drop into a valley. Determinant zero: only semidefinite, and the tiny leftover can take over. In this case the test says nothing at all; silence is not an answer.

Back to the opening trap: the determinant comes out negative five. Indefinite: the mixed term is strong enough to overpower both walls. Example six from the course text: time for a real computation. Setting both partials to zero lands us on a single stationary point. The determinant is twenty, positive, and A is positive too. Positive definite, so a local minimum, with value negative eight. Example seven: the origin is a stationary point. The determinant is negative one: indefinite, no extremum at the origin. Check by hand: positive along one axis, negative along a steeper line.

Now the dangerous row, where the determinant is exactly zero. Example eight from the course text, again at the origin. The origin is stationary, and the determinant comes out exactly zero. The table goes quiet, so we ask the function itself. Between the two parabolas the function is negative; outside, positive. That valley squeezes right into the origin, so any small disc catches both signs. The value there is zero, so it's neither a maximum nor a minimum. Stranger still: restricted to any straight line, the origin looks like a minimum. In the flat direction the quadratic form says nothing, and higher terms take charge. The trap hides on a curved path that no straight line can follow. Yet another function also scores zero, and it has a genuine minimum. Same zero, opposite endings: that row is silence, not an answer.

First caution: an extremum can hide where the partial derivatives don't even exist. The cone is lowest at its tip, yet no partial derivative exists there. So the candidate list has two kinds: stationary points, and points without partials. Second caution: on a closed region, the biggest value may sit on the edge. The recipe compares three groups: stationary points, points without partials, and the boundary. Searching the boundary is a constrained problem; that debt is paid in chapter eighteen.

4 · Outlook

One use: the least-squares fit, coming up, is confirmed as a minimum by this table. In physics, a marble settles for good at a minimum of potential energy. Balanced on a pass, with no extremum, one tiny nudge sends it rolling off. In short: the gradient shortlists, the quadratic form decides. Unless the determinant is zero or partials are missing; then study the function directly.

A glimpse ahead: suppose the Hessian is positive semidefinite at every point. Then the function is convex, and any stationary point is automatically the global minimum. That single fact quietly supports the loss functions behind machine learning. Two smiling walls were never enough; the test reads the whole quadratic form.
