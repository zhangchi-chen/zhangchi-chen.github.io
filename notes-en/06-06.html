06-06 The five disguises: first rewrite into 0/0 or ∞/∞

The five disguises: first rewrite into 0/0 or ∞/∞

1 · Motivation

Five limits wear disguises, and our rule only fits two shapes. Last episode's rule needs a fraction, and none of these three is one. Look at the first one: a product, with no fraction bar to work on.

Next, the famous power. First guess: its base is heading to 1. And 1 to any power is still 1, so the answer must be 1. Second answer: chapter three's famous limit says the value is e. The graph settles the argument: it climbs toward e, well above 1. The same shape can end at 1, at e, or blow up completely. That is the meaning of indeterminate: the shape alone decides nothing.

Besides last episode's two quotients, there are five more disguises. Think of the rule as a charger that fits only two kinds of socket. Each disguise is a foreign plug, and each needs a single adapter. Today we fit one adapter to each disguise and run an example through it. Then the real skill: choosing the cheapest tool before you write a line.

The engine itself has not changed since last episode. All we have to do is hand it a genuine fraction. Its checklist still has to pass, every single round.

The course text rewrites each indeterminate form with a single move. For zero times infinity, send one factor downstairs, flipped over. Flip one factor and you get zero over zero; flip the other, infinity over infinity. For infinity minus infinity, bring both terms over one denominator. Or, when a square root is causing the clash, rationalize it. For power forms, rewrite through e and the logarithm. Check all three: the exponent is always zero times infinity. So three power indeterminate forms collapse into one we already know. The outer exponential is continuous, so the limit passes straight through. One condition: the base has to be positive, or the logarithm makes no sense.

Here is the whole map: five rows, each with a first move and a landing spot. Every row finishes in one of the two basic forms. Five indeterminate forms, two basic forms, and nothing else to memorize.

3 · Method

First adapter at work, on the product from the homework. One factor shrinks to zero, the other plunges without bound. Who wins? Flip x downstairs, and the product becomes infinity over infinity. Check the list: the denominator blows up, and its derivative is never zero. One round, and the messy ratio collapses to a single term. So the product tends to zero. The graph agrees: the shrinking factor beats the exploding one. Careful, though: which factor you flip really matters. Flip the logarithm instead, and one round leaves a messier product. Rule of thumb: keep the logarithm on top, where one derivative tames it.

Second adapter, on the difference from the homework. Both terms shoot upward together, and the only question is their gap. Bring them over one denominator, and the difference becomes zero over zero. The adapter has done its job, and the engine can take over. Pure L'Hôpital works, but it takes two full rounds to reach zero. Correct, but every round means another check and a longer denominator. Cheaper: the denominator is a product, so swap it for its simple equivalent. And the numerator, as a whole, has a known cubic equivalent. Swap the whole numerator at once, never one piece inside a difference. So the fraction shrinks like a small multiple of x: the limit is zero. On the graph, the gap shrinks right along a gentle straight line. Substitution wins this one: a single line against two rounds of work.

The third adapter serves all three power forms. Back to our opening puzzle. Rewrite through e: everything moves into the exponent. The exponent is zero times infinity, and one flip makes it zero over zero. The engine works toward positive infinity as well, so go ahead. One round of differentiating, and the exponent settles at exactly 1. So the limit is e, and the opening puzzle is settled. An honest footnote: the logarithm's derivative was itself built on this limit. So this is a consistency check on the famous limit, not a brand new proof.

Now the zero to the zero form: its exponent is example one again. The exponent dies, so the power tends to 1. The curve dips a little, then climbs back to 1 at the left edge. Last power form, infinity to the zero: its exponent is infinity over infinity. One round sends the exponent to zero, so this power also tends to 1. It peaks just above 1.4, then sinks very slowly back toward 1. Three power disguises, one single machine, and not one new idea.

Before you touch the pen, ask which tool is the cheapest. The pencil is substitution from chapter three: cheapest for products and quotients. The marker is L'Hôpital: honest but slow, and it checks everything again every round. The printer you rent is Taylor's formula, next episode, built for sums. Here is where the pencil breaks. Swap each term for x inside the sum, and you get zero. That is wrong. The true value is one half, and L'Hôpital needs three rounds to get there. Both terms look like x, so the difference hides one level deeper. That is the debt chapter three left open, and next episode's Taylor pays it. Taylor is substitution done completely, keeping the terms that survive. Pencil first, the marker when you must, the printer for sums that cancel.

One habit before any adapter: make sure the form is genuinely indeterminate. This one is genuine: the base heads to 1 while the exponent blows up. Its exponent behaves like x, which dies away, so the limit is 1. Now a lookalike: here the base just sits at 2. That is no disguise at all: two to a huge power simply runs off to infinity. Run the machine blindly, differentiate anyway, and it claims the answer is 1. A wrong answer, and worse, the divergence hides behind a tidy number. Only the five pairings on the map are disguises, so check before you plug in.

4 · Outlook

First use: data science's entropy is built from exactly this kind of product. An outcome with zero probability contributes zero, and our first limit is the reason. Second use: savings interest compounded more and more often within a year. Same disguise; the machine gives e raised to the rate. At five percent a year, compounding never pushes you past about 5.13 percent.

Later on, this power disguise is everyday food in probability. Whenever a base creeps toward 1 while the exponent grows, use the same machine. Five disguises, one adapter each, and only two honest sockets. Check the form, rewrite it first, and only then differentiate.
