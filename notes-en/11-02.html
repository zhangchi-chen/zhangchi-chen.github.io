11-02 Singular integrals: scan for the crash point first, then integrate

Singular integrals: scan for the crash point first, then integrate

1 · Motivation

Today the trouble hides at one single point, not far away at infinity. Imagine an app that works on every input, except one that makes it crash. Some functions we integrate behave just like that app. Near one point, the values climb higher than any bound. The course text calls such a point a singular point. Away from that point, the function is integrable as usual. So here is the habit: find the singular points before you compute anything.

Here is an exam problem that catches many students. A student takes the antiderivative and plugs in both endpoints. Out comes negative two, and the student hands it in. But look at the graph. The function never dips below zero. Like a cafeteria receipt where every item costs money, yet the total is negative. A function that is never negative cannot give a negative integral. Here is the bug. At zero, the integrand has no bound. Chapter nine's Newton Leibniz formula needs an integrable function on the whole interval. And the antiderivative snaps apart at zero, so it cannot bridge the gap. The formula from chapter nine was used outside its conditions.

Now the honest rerun. Cut the interval at the singular point. On the right piece, stop short at epsilon and integrate up to 1. That piece is an ordinary integral, and its value is easy to write down. Now slide epsilon toward zero, and watch the value blow past every bound. So the right piece diverges, and the whole integral diverges with it. Negative two was never an answer. It was a bug report.

The course text turns that honest rerun into a definition. Definition two: integrate up to a wall, then let the wall approach a. So a is a singular point, and the integral is a singular integral. Clause one: however close you look to the right of a, the function has no bound. Clause two: on every closed piece that avoids a, the function is integrable. So each piece is an ordinary integral, and its value depends on u. Then let u slide toward a, from the right side. If that limit exists, the integral converges, and the limit is its value. If there is no limit, the integral diverges, and no value is assigned.

This is last episode's ritual again: an ordinary integral first, then a limit. Only the direction changes. The wall now closes in from inside the interval. If the singular point is the right end, the wall approaches it from the left. Either way, the limit is taken from one side only.

And what if the singular point sits inside, like zero in our accident? The whole converges exactly when both pieces converge. If both endpoints are singular points, the same rule applies: split inside. Each piece gets its own wall, moving on its own, with its own limit. Back in the accident, one divergent piece was already enough.

3 · Method

So does an unbounded integrand always mean the integral diverges? Take this function. As x heads toward 1, it shoots off to infinity. Stop at a wall below 1, and the arc sine gives the area exactly. Slide the wall toward 1, and the area settles down to half of pi. The function blows up, and yet the area under it stays finite. Being unbounded decides nothing. How fast it blows up decides everything.

How fast is too fast? Let's test a whole family of powers. Below 1 it converges; from 1 upward it diverges. When q is not 1, the piece from u to 1 has a neat formula. As u slides to zero, only one term is moving, and it decides everything. Its fate depends on one thing: is the exponent positive or negative? If q is below 1, that term fades away, and the integral converges. If q is above 1, that term explodes, and the integral diverges. And q equal to 1 gives a logarithm, which also climbs without end. So the dividing line is exactly 1, and 1 itself falls on the divergent side.

Now place last episode's family right beside this one. Sweep the exponent upward, and watch both verdicts flip at the same place. Out at infinity, the power must be bigger than 1. Near a singular point, smaller. It's one ruler, read in opposite directions, and exactly 1 fails both times. Near zero, a smaller power blows up more gently, leaving less area.

One last puzzle: an integral with trouble at both of its ends. Split at 1. You get a singular integral and an infinite integral. The singular piece converges only when p is below 1. The infinite piece converges only when p is above 1. No power meets both demands at once, so it diverges for every choice. Whatever power you pick, at least one end gives way, and at 1, both do.

4 · Outlook

Where does this matter? First, the length of a quarter circle. The arc length formula from chapter ten hands us exactly that integral. At the end the tangent stands straight up, yet the length is half of pi. Second, a weight on a spring, timed from the middle out to its farthest point. Time adds up distance over speed, and the speed hits zero at the end. It's the same singular integral, so a quarter of the swing takes half of pi.

This debt runs back to chapter nine: check the conditions before using the formula. Last episode gave us the twin ritual and the mirror image of today's dividing line. Two kinds of improper integral, one idea underneath: a limit of ordinary integrals. When direct computing stalls, both kinds need measuring sticks, and that comes next. Next, one comparison principle and two benchmark families will settle both kinds. Later on, integrals that depend on a parameter get the same scan first habit. So find the singular points first, and only then integrate.
