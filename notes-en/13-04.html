13-04 The sum function’s three questions: continuous, integrable, differentiable

The sum function’s three questions: continuous, integrable, differentiable

1 · Motivation

One sum function, three questions, real stakes. A partial sum only ever adds finitely many terms. So it is continuous, and you can integrate or differentiate it one term at a time. Now the limit takes over the class, like a stand in teacher.

First: does it show up every day? That is, is the sum continuous? Second: does it grade the same way? Can we integrate one term at a time? Third: can it learn new material? Can we differentiate one term at a time?

First, a warning sign: triangles that keep getting taller and thinner. Student A says: at every point the heights die out, so the integral is zero. Student B says: every triangle has area one half, so the answer is one half. Same functions, same interval, and yet two different answers. So trading a limit for an integral is not automatic. One condition settles all three questions: uniform convergence. And the M test from the last episode is how we check it.

3 · Method

Why does continuity survive the limit? That is our goal. Step one: freeze a single term, close to the limit everywhere at once. Step two: that one term is continuous, so near the point it barely moves. Step three: three small pieces add up to less than epsilon. Done. The key: one single term serves both points at once. That is uniformity. With pointwise convergence alone, the index we need explodes near 1. No single term serves every point, so the limit is free to break. In short: under uniformity, the two limits may trade places.

Integrals are easier: inside a thin band, the areas can only differ a little. Shrink epsilon and the gap shrinks with it. The swap is safe. Back to the triangles, now with a height we can choose. Any point right of zero is soon left behind for good. Each area is half of base times height. Let height equal the index: area one half, always. Think of moving out of the dorm: each day, more floor is clear. But the same half box stays by the door, piled higher and higher. The limit is an empty room. Its integral is zero. One half against zero. Here the swap simply fails. Which hypothesis died? Uniformity: the peaks climb without bound.

Question three hides a subtler trap. Watch this family. They converge uniformly to the line: the gap never exceeds one over the index. Their derivatives are perfectly continuous too. But at the right end, every single derivative is zero. So the derivatives tend to 1 everywhere, except at the end, where they give zero. Yet the limit line has slope 1 there. One is not zero. Which hypothesis died? The derivatives do not converge uniformly. Theorem 13.9 read backwards: a limit that jumps cannot be uniform. The functions converged uniformly. The derivatives did not. Stay away from the end, the derivatives behave, and the swap holds. Remember it: the most missed hypothesis of the chapter is about derivatives.

Now change the triangles: every peak has height 1. Still not uniform: the peak never comes down. Yet the integrals shrink to zero, and the conclusion holds anyway. Uniform convergence is sufficient, but not necessary. On exams: check the hypotheses, cite the theorem, and never claim the converse. Results in the other direction need extra conditions, Dini’s theorem for one.

Recall: the geometric series is not uniform on the whole interval. But on every closed piece inside, the M test works. The corollaries ask for less: uniform on every closed subinterval. That is enough, because continuity and derivatives only look nearby. The course text’s showcase: the zeta function, for x beyond 1. It is not uniform on the whole half line: near 1, the tails blow up. But on each closed piece, every derivative series has a convergent dominant series. So the zeta function has continuous derivatives of every order. We never computed the sum. The series alone told us.

4 · Outlook

First use: integrate a series whose sum we never write down. Term by term, the area comes out near 2.10. Second use: with cubes downstairs, we may differentiate term by term. Its derivative is a cosine series, continuous on the whole line.

A debt paid: the jump from the start of this chapter is now explained. And the M test from last time finally shows what it was for. As with the Newton Leibniz formula, these swaps rest on theorems, not hope. Left for later: power series, handled term by term. And a later episode on special values will lean on this smoothness.

Pointwise convergence: nothing is guaranteed to survive. Uniform convergence: the swaps, once their hypotheses are met. The M test: the tool that checks uniformity. And today: the payoff, read straight off the series. Later, real analysis rebuilds these swaps with dominated convergence, at a cheaper price. And existence proofs for differential equations rest on the uniform metric. With the right uniformity in place, the stand in passes all three.
