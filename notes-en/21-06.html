21-06 Path independence: the force field that does not care which road you take

Path independence: the force field that does not care which road you take

1 · Motivation

Today: a force field that does not care which road you take. Two points, and three different ways between them. Here is the real question: does the road change the answer?

First field: a work integral, just like the ones in episode twenty oh two. On the straight road, twenty-five sixths. Along the parabola, ten thirds. Same start, same finish, and already two different answers. Along the broken line, three halves. Three roads, and three different answers.

Now a second field, on the very same three roads. Every road gives exactly 5. So why does one field care about the road, and the other not?

Before any integral, compare two partial derivatives. For the first field, the two derivatives disagree. For the second field, they match, both equal to 1. The test told the whole story before we integrated anything. Today we see why the test works, and where it quietly breaks.

2 · Statements

Theorem 21.12: on a simply connected D, four conditions are equivalent.
Simply connected: every closed curve in D shrinks to a point without leaving D.
Condition (i): around every closed curve in D, the integral is 0.
Condition (ii): the integral depends only on the start and end points.
Condition (iii): P dx + Q dy is the total differential du of some u.
Condition (iv): ∂P/∂y = ∂Q/∂x at every point of D.
(i)–(iii) speak about curves across D; (iv) only looks at single points.
Equivalent means: any one of them forces the other three.
The proof runs once around this cycle of four arrows.
Field one fails (iv) on the plane, so (ii) fails too: routes matter.
Field two passes (iv), so only A and B matter: the answer 5.

The theorem: in a region without holes, four conditions turn out equivalent. In plain words: no holes, so any loop can shrink to a point inside the region. Condition one: every loop in the region gives zero. Condition two: only where you start and where you finish matters. Condition three: the expression is the total differential of one function. Condition four: the two cross derivatives agree at every single point. The first three are about the whole region; the fourth only checks points one at a time.

Equivalent means: get any one, and you get all four. The proof is one lap around these four arrows. The first field fails the fourth condition, so the route matters. The second field passes, so only the two endpoints matter.

3 · Method

First arrow: if every loop gives zero, routes can be swapped. Take any two routes from the start to the finish. Go out along one, come back along the other: that is a loop. By condition one, this loop gives zero. So the two routes give the same value. Condition two.

Second arrow: from routes that do not matter to an actual function. Fix a home base, and let u be the integral from there to any point. Every route agrees, so each point gets exactly one value. Now take one small step straight to the right. Route through the old point, and the change is just the short step. The mean value theorem turns it into P at a nearby point, times the step. Shrink the step to nothing: continuity hands us exactly P. A step straight up gives the other partial in the same way. Both partials are continuous, so u has the total differential from episode seventeen oh one.

Third arrow: from a function back to the test. Those two derivatives are just the two mixed partials of u. Continuous mixed partials do not care about the order, so they agree. So the test holds at every point: condition four.

The last arrow brings us back to zero loops. Take a loop in the region; with no holes, everything it encloses stays inside. Green's formula, from episode twenty one oh five, turns the loop into a double integral. By condition four, there is nothing to integrate, so the loop gives zero. One lap done: the four conditions live or die together.

Such a function is called a potential function, the primitive of the expression. Any route at all: only the value at the finish and at the start counts. It is the Newton Leibniz ending from episode nine oh three, one dimension up.

An example from the course text: does this expression have a potential function? Test first: both sides come out the same. It passes. Here is a candidate potential. Check both partials: they give back exactly the two pieces. Over a winding road from the origin, the answer is simply 2.

But where did that function come from? We can build it. Like crossing campus: first along the street, then down one corridor. Integrate along the horizontal leg, then the vertical leg, and add. The street leg and the corridor leg each hand over one piece. Add the two legs, and out comes exactly the candidate. One condition: the broken line must stay inside the region.

Now the catch: the region must have no holes. Take this field. It lives everywhere except the origin. And it passes the test at every point where it is defined. Yet one lap around the unit circle gives two pi, as episode twenty one oh five found. The test passes, the loop fails: the hole has cut the chain. Condition two fails too: two half circles, same ends, opposite answers. Green needs the whole inside, and this circle wraps around the missing point. A loop that does not wind around the origin still gives zero. The test only sees each point; the whole chain needs a region with no holes.

4 · Outlook

In physics, a force with a potential function is called a conservative field. The work done depends only on the potential at the two ends. Potential energy runs the other way, so work equals the potential energy lost. Gravity near the ground is the classic example. Gravity only cares how far you dropped, never the path. Like a running app that only tells you the net climb from start to finish.

Back to the opening: the potential is just the product, so every road gives 5. Facing an ugly line integral? Run the test first. If it passes, find the potential and plug in the two ends. If it fails, choose a friendly route, or hand the loop to Green, from episode twenty one oh five.

In space the same four conditions hold, as episode twenty two oh four will state. There the single test becomes three matching pairs. Exact differential equations and thermodynamics reuse this same idea. Run the test, mind the holes, and the road stops mattering.
