Hello, everyone.Today: the intermediate value theorem.Plus its corollary, the root theorem.Your watch buzzes when the air crosses 20°C.Morning: 8°C. Afternoon: 32°C.The rise is continuous, with no sudden leaps.Must there be a moment at exactly 20°C?

Intuition says yes, but can we prove it?Imagine a glitch: a jump from 15°C to 25°C.Then 20°C is skipped, and the watch stays silent.So the promise needs continuity.Continuity welds the graph into one rope.The rope runs from one shore to the other.To pass a level line, the rope must touch it.

But a picture is belief, not proof.

Theorem: let f be continuous on [a, b].Assume f(a) ≠ f(b).Take any μ strictly between f(a) and f(b).Then there is at least one x₀ ∈ (a, b)such that f(x₀) = μ.Corollary: the root theorem.If f is continuous on [a, b] and f(a)·f(b) < 0,

then f(x₀) = 0 for at least one x₀ ∈ (a, b).The root theorem is just the case μ = 0.A negative product means opposite signs at the ends.So 0 sits strictly between f(a) and f(b).A sign change is μ = 0 in disguise.Draw the level line y = μ.The rope starts below it and ends above it.

Somewhere, it must touch the line.There may be several crossings: at least one.Neither endpoint hits μ, so x₀ ∈ (a, b).Add last episode's max–min theorem.The image is a full interval: f([a, b]) = [m, M].Why is it true? Let's go on border patrol.Say f(a) < μ < f(b).

The other case: use −f and −μ instead.Let E = {x ∈ [a, b] : f(x) < μ}.a ∈ E, so E is non-empty.And b is an upper bound for E.So E has a supremum: x₀ = sup E.x₀ is the border between below and not-below.Claim: the border point sits exactly on μ.

Suppose f(x₀) < μ.Then x₀ < b, since f(b) > μ.Continuity keeps f below μ on a whole neighborhood.So points just right of x₀ join E.That beats the supremum: absurd.Suppose f(x₀) > μ.Then x₀ > a, since f(a) < μ.

A whole neighborhood of x₀ lies above μ.So E stays left of x₀ − δ.A smaller upper bound appears: absurd again.Both escapes fail, so f(x₀) = μ.Chase the border; the border lands on μ.Supremum plus continuity did all the work.

Application: x³ − 2x + 2 = 0 has a real root.Try ±1 and ±2: none is a root, so no easy factoring.Let p(x) = x³ − 2x + 2; then p(−2) = −2 < 0.And p(−1) = 3 > 0.So a real root lies in (−2, −1).Bisection: halve the cage, keep the sign change.Midpoint −1.5: positive. Keep the left half.

Midpoint −1.75: positive again. Keep the left half.Midpoint −1.875: negative. Keep the right half.The cage shrinks; the root stays trapped inside.The root is about −1.769.Bisection is the computable face of the root theorem.Every odd-degree real polynomial has a real root.Divide by the leading coefficient; let n be odd.

Take R = 1 + |a₀| + ⋯ + |aₙ₋₁|.For |x| ≥ R, the term xⁿ outweighs all the rest.Odd n gives p(R) > 0 and p(−R) < 0.The root theorem on [−R, R] gives a real root.

Next: for a > 0, xⁿ = a has a unique positive root.Existence: let g(x) = xⁿ − a.Work on the interval [0, 1 + a].g(0) = −a < 0.g(1 + a) = (1 + a)ⁿ − a ≥ 1.Because (1 + a)ⁿ ≥ 1 + a.So a root sits in (0, 1 + a).

Uniqueness: take 0 < x < y and factor yⁿ − xⁿ.yⁿ − xⁿ = (y − x)(yⁿ⁻¹ + ⋯ + xⁿ⁻¹) > 0.So xⁿ strictly increases: only one positive root.Last: let f be continuous with f([a, b]) ⊆ [a, b].Claim: some x₀ ∈ [a, b] has f(x₀) = x₀.The graph lives in the square, so it meets the diagonal.Let F(x) = f(x) − x.

F(a) = f(a) − a ≥ 0 and F(b) = f(b) − b ≤ 0.If either one is 0, that endpoint is fixed.Otherwise F changes sign, and the root theorem finds x₀.Example: cos maps [0, 1] into itself; its fixed point ≈ 0.739.Values are attained, and in-between values are crossed.One question remains: is the wobble controlled at a uniform scale?That is uniform continuity, two episodes ahead.
