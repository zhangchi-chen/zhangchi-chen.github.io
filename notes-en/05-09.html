05-09 Higher-order differentials: from second order on, a change of variable adds a term

Higher-order differentials: from second order on, a change of variable adds a term

1 · Motivation

Higher order differentials, and the extra term a change of variable brings. Two classmates solve the same problem and get two different answers. The setup: a sine, whose input is itself a square. The task: find the second order differential.

Lin decides that x is the variable in charge. He differentiates twice, keeping the small step fixed. Lin's answer comes out short and clean. Two lines and done, and Lin closes the notebook.

Zhou plugs in first, so only one variable is left. Two derivatives in a row, and the product rule splits the result in two. Zhou's answer has two pieces, not one.

To compare, translate Lin's answer into the same variable. Now line the two answers up, piece by piece. One piece matches. The other has vanished from Lin's page. Same function, same problem. So who made the mistake? Somewhere a step was skipped, like an unticked box on a packing list.

2 · Statements

First: what is d²y when x is the independent variable?
The definition, in the course text's words.
Key clause: x and dx are independent of each other.
So dy = f′(x) dx varies with x alone; dx is a constant.
Differentiate it once more: d(dy) = f″(x) dx · dx.
Result: d²y = f″(x) dx², the second-order differential.
Careful: three look-alikes that must not be confused.
The course text's warning, word for word.
dx² means (dx)²: the square of one small step.
d²x: the step of x never changes, so d²x = 0.
d(x²) = 2x dx: the L-shaped strip from 05-08.
A square, a zero, a strip: three different things.
But d²x = 0 only because x is the independent variable.
Keep going: dⁿy = d(dⁿ⁻¹y) = f⁽ⁿ⁾(x) dxⁿ.
The course text: orders n ≥ 2 are higher-order differentials.
Divide by dxⁿ: the familiar dⁿy/dxⁿ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(x).
This quotient is honest — but only while x is independent.
Lin used exactly this rule.
But in the homework x = t², so x was never independent.

First, what does the second differential mean in the plain case? Here is the definition, word for word. The key clause: the point and the step are chosen separately. So the first differential changes only with the point. Differentiate once more, and the step comes along as a factor. That is the second order differential.

Careful: three look alike symbols, easy to mix up. Here is the textbook's own warning. The first one is just a small step, squared. The second one is zero: the step itself never changes. The third is the L shaped strip from the last episode. A square, a zero, and a strip. Three different things. But remember: that zero belongs to the independent variable only.

Keep going, and every order adds one more factor of the step. From order two on, they are called higher order differentials. Divide out the steps, and the familiar derivative notation appears. That quotient is honest, but only under one condition. Lin used exactly this rule. But in the homework it was never free: it depended on another variable.

3 · Method

Here is the mechanism. Start with equal steps of the outer variable. Send each point through the squaring map. Now the steps are no longer equal. They keep growing. Each gap beats the one before by exactly zero point five. That growth of the step itself is the second differential of x. Here it is twice the squared step, matching the picture, not zero. Only with equal steps from the start would that growth vanish. Lin quietly assumed equal steps. The homework has growing ones.

Now redo the second differential, and let the step move too. The first differential is a product, and both factors are moving. The product rule hands back two pieces instead of one. The first piece is exactly Lin's whole answer. The second piece is the box Lin never ticked. When the step never changes, that piece is zero and quietly drops out.

Back to the homework, with both ingredients in hand. Multiply them, and out comes exactly the missing piece. Put it back on Lin's page, and the two answers agree. Lin's arithmetic was fine. The formula was simply used in the wrong place. Zhou's answer is the true second differential in the outer variable.

Here is the general statement, word for word. At first order, the formula survives the change of variable. At second order, the same form breaks. It is one piece short. This settles the promise from the last episode: invariance stops at first order.

First use: the second derivative of a curve given by a parameter. Recall: the first derivative was a ratio of two rates. The tempting guess: just square the bottom. It treats the notation as a fraction, and forgets the hidden piece. The right reading: differentiate the first derivative once more. Use the quotient rule, then divide by the horizontal rate once more. The result: a cube downstairs, and two pieces upstairs. That extra piece upstairs is the forgotten item again. Test both on the unit circle, at the forty five degree point. The right formula gives minus two root two; the guess, only minus root two. Differentiate the upper half circle directly, and the correct formula wins. That answers the question left open two episodes ago.

4 · Outlook

Second use: a better approximation. Add half of the second differential to the linear guess. Try it on the square root of one point one. The tangent line says one point zero five. The parabola trims a little and lands on 1.04875. The true value is 1.04881, so the error shrinks about twentyfold. In chapter six, Taylor's formula will make this exact, with a remainder.

Third use: whether the second differential is positive or negative. The squared step is positive, so the sign comes from the second derivative. Positive means the graph sits above its tangent line, near the point. At a flat point, positive means a minimum, negative a maximum. A bonus: at a flat point, the troublesome extra piece is zero anyway. So at a flat point, Lin's short form is right in any variable. If it is zero there, this test says nothing at all. The proofs come in chapter six, for extrema and for convexity.

One last rule: the fraction reading is safe only for the independent variable. Otherwise, differentiate one honest step at a time. And Lin's checklist finally has every box ticked.

Looking ahead: with two variables, the second differential has three pieces. In linear algebra, this shape is called a quadratic form. Always positive or always negative: that will decide minimum or maximum. Today's lesson carries over: from second order on, count every piece. Two routes, one answer, once nothing is left off the list.
