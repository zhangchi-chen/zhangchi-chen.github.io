10-02 Formula self-check: let the circle and the sphere catch your mistakes

Formula self-check: let the circle and the sphere catch your mistakes

1 · Motivation

Today the circle and the sphere become our mistake detectors. It is exam eve, and two sets of notes disagree on the same formula. One version carries an extra root factor, the other leaves it out. This chapter hands us a long card of formulas, all with friendly little coefficients. And the wrong versions look every bit as believable.

When a receipt looks odd, you compare it with prices you already know. Our price list was printed years ago, in school geometry class. Circle, sphere and cone give six answers we can recite in our sleep. Method one, build the formula from slices, then hold it against school geometry. Method two, recall the formula, test its units, then compare with school.

Each line is a thin slice added up, built in the element method episode. Those friendly coefficients come from circles and triangles. The root factor measures how much each tiny chord leans.

The course text issues a warning about one tempting shortcut. The cylinder band stands straight, the frustum band leans with the curve. Today a real sphere settles which notebook is right. Lengths, areas and volumes carry one, two and three powers of length. A slope divides a length by a length, so it is a pure number.

3 · Method

Test one, the distance around a circle, a price we know. Send a point once around the circle, and watch it go. Its speed never changes, it is always the radius. Add up the steps and the price tag comes back exactly.

Test two, the area of the circle, and we compute it twice on purpose. Route one fans thin sectors out from the center. A full turn of sectors gives exactly the school answer. Route two cuts the disk into upright strips, while the point walks the rim. The strips land on the very same answer. Two independent routes agreeing is the strongest check there is. Where its absolute value comes from is told in the very next episode.

Now the main event, the skin of a sphere, whose price we know. Spin the upper half of a circle around its diameter. The first notebook slices the sphere into thin frustum bands. Here comes the key move, taming the root factor. Multiply by the height and everything cancels, leaving only the radius. A surprise, every slice of the same thickness has the same area. Notebook A lands exactly on the school answer. The second notebook uses straight cylinder bands instead. Its integral is just the half disk area, scaled up. The answer comes up more than a fifth short. The receipt is wrong, and yet it looks perfectly innocent.

Check the units, and both answers are honest areas. Checking units is like running a spelling checker. It flags gibberish, but happily waves through the wrong synonym. The wrong answer is spelled perfectly, it just means the wrong thing. The missing factor is a pure number, so the units never notice it leave. Only the school answer catches this one.

The two bands agree only at the equator, where the curve is flat. Near the poles the bands lean hard, and the cylinder shortcut loses the most. On a cylinder the curve is flat everywhere, so both bands agree. That is why the wrong formula feels familiar, it survives the easiest case.

Method two, you only half remember the volume of a spun solid. Was it the circle around, or the disk inside? Ask the units first. A volume needs three powers of length, and the thickness supplies one. So the slice must contribute an area, the disk. The other choice is just the distance around, so it integrates to an area. Out at a glance, a volume can never be an area. Now the price check, and the sphere comes out exactly right. And the cone also lands on its school answer. Units choose the shape of the formula, then a known answer fixes the numbers.

Last test, the slanted skin of a cone, another price we know. Here l is the slant height of the cone. The slant never changes, so the root factor is one fixed ratio. Integrate, and the cone lands exactly on its price tag. One factor comes from the radius, the slant comes from the tilted element. Drop it, and you get the classic school slip, height in place of slant.

So what can the units catch, and what slips right past them? It catches mixing kinds, like a misremembered root in the arc formula. That adds a plain number to an area, which is nonsense. It also catches an area pretending to be a volume. It misses wrong coefficients, with or without the root, both are areas. So check in two layers, units first, known answers second.

4 · Outlook

First use, the area of a cap sliced off a sphere. Because the integrand is constant, the cap area comes out in one line. Let the cap cover the whole sphere, and the full price returns. Second use, mid exam, you suddenly doubt that half in the polar formula. Units shrug, but one circle shows that dropping the half doubles the area.

Every formula we checked today was built in the element method episode. Today was the quality check at the end of that assembly line. The physics episode will put its answers through the same two checks. A total pressure must come out as a force, and work as force times distance.

Physicists sketch a law from its units before running any experiment. Numerical courses use it to sniff out impossible computer output. Today it caught a broken root, and admitted what it cannot see. Units first, and then the circle and the sphere have the final say.
