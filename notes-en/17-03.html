17-03 The multivariable chain rule: draw the variable tree

The multivariable chain rule: draw the variable tree

1 · Motivation

Today, one small drawing will keep a whole computation honest. A homework problem with two layers of variables. We want its rate as only the first variable moves.

One classmate plugs everything in first, and gets four. Another reaches for the old one-variable chain rule, and gets one. Four against one, for the very same question. B's answer is too small. Something got left out — but what?

Nudge the first variable, and both middle variables react. B counted one route and lost the other. The forgotten route contributes three. Add the two routes, and the answers finally agree.

It is like a campus map with two ways from the dorm to class. Each leg rescales the nudge, and the two routes' shares add up at the end. Which ones multiply, which ones add? Let's draw it properly.

2 · Statements

Put z on top, with its variables x and y below it.
Then hang s and t under each of x and y.
x, y are intermediate variables; s, t are independent variables.
Each edge carries a partial derivative: upper variable over lower.
Along one path from z down to s, multiply the edges.
Across all paths that reach s, add the products.
Two paths from z to s: exactly two terms.
Theorem 17.5 says exactly this, hypotheses first.
First hypothesis: φ and ψ are differentiable at (s, t).
Second hypothesis: f is differentiable at (x, y) — not merely partial derivatives.
Conclusion: the composite function is differentiable at (s, t).
And the formula is the tree read aloud: two paths, two terms.
For ∂z/∂t, follow the two paths ending at t instead.
The general form: m intermediate variables, n independent ones.
Each uₖ opens one path to xᵢ, so the sum has m terms.
Here f must be differentiable; the uₖ need only partial derivatives.
Theorem 17.5 asks more below, because it also promises a differentiable composite.
With a single path, it is the one-variable chain rule of 05-04.

Put the final quantity on top, and hang its two variables below. Then each middle variable sprouts its own two branches. The middle layer holds the intermediate variables; the bottom holds the independent ones. Write a partial derivative on every edge, the upper one over the lower one. Walk one route down to the bottom, multiplying as you go. Then add up every route that ends at the same leaf. Two routes to the same leaf means exactly two terms.

Here is the theorem: hypotheses first, then the formula. First, the lower layer must be differentiable at the point. Second, the top layer must be differentiable too, and we will see why. Then the composite function is differentiable as well. And the formula is just the tree, read aloud: two routes, two terms. For the second variable, walk the two routes to the other leaf.

The general version: any number of middle and bottom variables. Every middle variable opens one route, and each route is one term. The top must be differentiable; below, partial derivatives are enough. The theorem asks more of the lower layer because it promises more: a differentiable composite. With a single route, we are back to the one-variable chain rule.

3 · Method

Why does the tree work? Go back to the total differential. The key move: write each middle change through the bottom changes. Regroup, and the number in front of the first change is our two-route sum. Plug a linear rule into a linear rule, and you still get a linear rule. And the leftovers die out, because the lower layer is continuous. Remember that first line: it only exists if the top is differentiable.

Take the course text's first example: a logarithm of two middle variables. The letters are new, but the tree has the same shape. The classic slip: write the first route and stop. It is like a step counter that skipped one leg of your walk. The fix: one term for each route, painted in the route's color. Blue route plus amber route: now the derivative is complete. A good habit: count the routes before you write a single term. The other partial works the same way, through the other leaf.

Now the risky hypothesis: what if the top only has partial derivatives? The course text answers with a warning example. Along both axes the function is flat zero, so both partial slopes are zero. So along the diagonal, the chain rule predicts a rate of zero. Now actually take the walk, and watch the height. The height is exactly half of the walk, so the true rate is one half. One half against zero. The rule has broken down. The reason: the top function is not differentiable at the origin. The only possible tangent plane is flat, yet the surface pulls away far too fast. Same lesson as before: two slopes along the axes do not make a tangent plane. That hypothesis is exactly the piece this example is missing.

Example three: one variable sits both inside and outside. In the tree, one branch skips the middle and drops straight down. Three routes reach the bottom, so the answer has three terms. The shortcut branch needs no multiplying at all. Their sum is called the total derivative. Careful: the partial derivative here covers only the shortcut branch. At the start: the total is two, the partial is one.

Remember the one-variable version: the differential keeps its shape. Now the two-variable version, as the course text states it. Independent or intermediate, the formula wears the same shape. You never have to decide who is independent. Example seven: an exponential times a sine. Peel the outer layer first, treating the middle variables as free. It is like unboxing a parcel, one layer at a time, with no map in hand. Peel the next layer, and swap in the bottom changes. Collect the terms, and the two partial derivatives sit there as coefficients. We never drew a tree, and never asked who was independent.

4 · Outlook

First application: switching to polar coordinates. Same tree as always, with radius and angle at the bottom. Two routes reach the radius, giving two terms. Two routes reach the angle as well. At each point, the radial and turning directions are just the axes, rotated. Square and add: the cross terms cancel, and what is left matches exactly. Radius and angle just repackage the same slope information.

The promise made back in chapter five is now kept, for many variables. In chapter eighteen, implicit differentiation runs on this very rule. In chapter twenty-one, changing variables in double integrals needs these partials.

Looking ahead: the tree is secretly a matrix multiplication. Row meets column: multiply in pairs, then add. Same rule as the tree. In chapter twenty-three, that becomes one clean sentence about matrices.

So: draw the tree, multiply along each route, add the routes. And always check that the top is truly differentiable. Then the tree will never let you forget a route.
