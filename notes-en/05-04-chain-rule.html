Motivation

  1. Chain rule and inverse functions: nested dolls, opened from the outside in.
  2. Late night, problem set due: differentiate sin(x²).
  3. Classmate A writes cos(x²): sine becomes cosine, done.
  4. Classmate B writes 2x·cos(x²): the same, plus an extra factor.
  5. Same function, two answers. At most one is right.
  6. Ask the graph: y = sin(x²), with a point at x = 1.
  7. Classmate A's slope, about 0.54: the line cuts across the curve.
  8. Classmate B's slope, about 1.08: the line hugs the curve.
  9. B is right. A lost the factor 2x. Where does it come from?
  10. sin(x²) is a doll inside a doll: x² sits inside the sine.
  11. Each layer changes at its own rate, and the rates multiply.
  12. Today: why rates multiply, and where the obvious proof breaks.
  13. And a bonus: run a function backwards, and its slope flips over.

What the statement says

  1. Notation alert: here the course text calls the inner function φ, the outer f.
  2. In episode 04-08 it was the other way round: f inside, g outside.
  3. Here is the chain rule, Theorem 5.8, exactly as the text states it.
  4. Clause one: φ is differentiable at x₀; the inner layer has a rate.
  5. Clause two: f is differentiable at u₀ = φ(x₀), where the inner lands.
  6. The outer function never sees x₀; it only sees u₀.
  7. Conclusion: the composite's rate is f′(u₀) times φ′(x₀).
  8. With numbers at x₀ = 1: squaring stretches a tiny piece by about 2.
  9. Then sine scales it again, by about cos 1 ≈ 0.54.
  10. Overall about 2 × 0.54 ≈ 1.08: exactly classmate B's slope.
  11. Now the picture to remember: rates as gears.
  12. The small gear spins twice per big turn: du/dx = 2.
  13. Add a third gear, 1.5 times faster again: dy/du = 1.5.
  14. Per pedal turn, the last gear spins 2 × 1.5 = 3 times.
  15. Rates compose by multiplication: dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx).

Method / proof core

  1. The tempting proof: split the difference quotient through Δu.
  2. As Δx → 0, each factor seems to settle on a derivative.
  3. Look at the denominator Δu. What if Δu = 0?
  4. Δu = 0 whenever the inner function returns to its value u₀.
  5. Here φ(x) = x² sin(1/x) returns to 0 infinitely often near 0.
  6. No Δx is small enough to dodge it: the proof divides by zero.
  7. Extreme case: φ is constant, so Δu = 0 for every Δx.
  8. Yet f(φ(x)) is constant too, and the rule holds: 0 = f′(u₀)·0.
  9. The theorem is fine; only the proof is broken.
  10. Same trap as 04-08: an inner function parked on one value.
  11. The honest fix: multiply, never divide.
  12. Let H(u) be the difference quotient of f; it has a hole at u₀.
  13. The text's lemma fills the hole with f′(u₀): now H is continuous.
  14. So the outer increment is f(u) − f(u₀) = H(u)·(u − u₀).
  15. At u = u₀ both sides are 0: nothing is divided by zero.
  16. Same patch for the inner: φ(x) − φ(x₀) = G(x)·(x − x₀).
  17. Put u = φ(x): the outer patch times the inner patch.
  18. H(φ(x)) is continuous at x₀: Theorem 4.5 from 04-08.
  19. At x₀ the factor equals f′(u₀)φ′(x₀): the chain rule.
  20. No division by Δu anywhere, so a constant inner is harmless.
  21. A three-layer doll: y = e^(sin(x²)).
  22. Outer shell: y = eᵘ, whose derivative is eᵘ.
  23. Middle shell: u = sin v, whose derivative is cos v.
  24. Inner doll: v = x², whose derivative is 2x.
  25. Multiply along the rail: y′ = e^(sin(x²))·cos(x²)·2x.
  26. Stop one layer early, and the answer ends at cos(x²): 2x lost.
  27. That was classmate A's slip at the start, one layer down.
  28. Rule: one factor per layer. Count the dolls, count the factors.
  29. The course text lists both rules in Leibniz notation.
  30. Here the du's seem to cancel, like a fraction.
  31. It is legal, but not as arithmetic: the theorem makes it true.
  32. dy/du is one derivative, not a ratio; cancelling is a memory aid.
  33. Mismatched middles never cancel: dy/dt · dz/dx is a different thing.
  34. Matching letters link like a chain: dy/du · du/dv · dv/dx = dy/dx.
  35. Last tool: inverse functions, the gear train run backwards.
  36. Theorem 5.7, the inverse function rule, exactly as the text states it.
  37. Example: x = sin y on [−π/2, π/2], reversed, is y = arcsin x.
  38. Forward slope at y₀ = π/6: cos(π/6) ≈ 0.87.
  39. Backward slope at x₀ = 1/2: about 1.15, the reciprocal.
  40. Swapping the axes swaps rise and run: dy/dx = 1/(dx/dy).
  41. Why φ′(y₀) ≠ 0? At y₀ = π/2 the forward tangent is flat.
  42. Mirrored, flat turns vertical: slope 1/0, no derivative at x = 1.
  43. Why strictly monotone? Otherwise one x has two y's: no way back.
  44. So Δx and Δy are nonzero together, and shrink to 0 together.
  45. Back to arcsin: its slope is 1/cos y.
  46. On (−π/2, π/2), cos y > 0, so cos y = √(1 − x²).
  47. So (arcsin x)′ = 1/√(1 − x²); at x = 1/2, about 1.15. Match.

Applications and outlook

  1. Use one: spilled coffee spreads in a circle, radius growing 0.5 cm/s.
  2. Area A = πr², so dA/dt = 2πr · dr/dt: time is the inner layer.
  3. At r = 4 cm: 2π·4·0.5 = 4π ≈ 12.6 cm² per second.
  4. Use two: in 05-05, chain plus inverse grow three mother formulas into the table.
  5. For instance, ln x reverses eˣ, so its derivative is 1/eʸ = 1/x.
  6. A debt to 05-07: parametric slopes are chain plus inverse, used honestly.
  7. Later, implicit differentiation pushes both sides of an equation through this rule.
  8. In several variables it becomes a tree: one term per path.
  9. So: peel from the outside, one factor per layer, never divide by Δu.
  10. Forward, rates multiply; backward, they flip.
