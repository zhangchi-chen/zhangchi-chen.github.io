06-03 Monotonicity and inequalities: turn the inequality into a function

Monotonicity and inequalities: turn the inequality into a function

1 · Motivation

Today we see how a graph's direction can prove an inequality. Homework: show the exponential beats one plus x, away from zero. The first thing anyone tries is plugging in numbers. Close to zero, the two sides almost touch. But three checks can never cover every number.

Let's draw both sides and look. The picture agrees, but a picture proves nothing. Here is the key move: watch the gap, not the two sides. Give the gap a name, capital F. Now the homework asks one thing: does F stay above zero? And rising or falling is exactly what the derivative records. So today we read slope signs, and turn them into proofs.

3 · Method

First, a warm-up straight from the course text. Differentiate once to get the slope. Factor it, to read the sign piece by piece. The sign can only change at plus or minus one over root three. Read it like a monthly budget: stretches in surplus, stretches in deficit. One test point in each band tells you its sign. So the graph climbs, dips, and climbs again. On both outer bands the function rises. In the middle band it falls. Careful, rising on each band does not mean rising on both together.

Back to our homework, with a recipe card from the shared kitchen. Step one: put everything on one side. Call it F; we want it positive everywhere except at zero. Step two: differentiate, and look at the sign. The derivative is negative left of zero, positive right of zero. And the anchor: F is zero at zero. Step three: right of zero, it climbs away from zero, so it's positive. On the left, it slides down to zero, so it was positive before. Positive on both sides, and the homework is done. This is exactly Example 5 in the course text. One small debt: we started at an endpoint where the slope is flat.

Some problems already hand you a difference of two values. The reflex fires: trade the difference for a slope times a length. A replay from last episode: the arctangent estimate. Arctangent's slope never exceeds one. Multiply by the positive length, and the estimate is ours. Second example: compare two exponentials with their inputs. The reflex again: the slope at the hidden point is above one. Multiply by the positive length, and it is proved. And the same fact also fits our recipe card. G climbs on the positive side, so its later value is bigger. Two recipes on one shelf; the problem says which to grab. Before drawing a sign chart, cut the domain at the bad points.

Now the footnote: a nonnegative slope only buys plain increasing. First case: a function that pauses flat in the middle. The slope is never negative, but it sits at zero along a stretch. So the second clause fails: it rises, but not strictly. Second case: x cubed, perfectly flat for an instant at the origin. Yet it is strictly increasing, since the slope is zero at one point only. Even infinitely many isolated flat points are allowed. The second clause only bans a solid stretch of zeros. So a positive slope everywhere is sufficient, but not necessary. With continuity at the endpoint, the endpoint joins in; that is how we started at zero.

4 · Outlook

First use: counting how many roots an equation has. The slope is always positive, so it crosses zero at most once. It starts below zero and ends above, so exactly one root sits between. Second use, a classic puzzle: which of two famous powers is bigger? Take logarithms, and it becomes a question about one function. Past e the slope turns negative, so the function falls from there on. Pi lies past e, so its value is smaller. Rearrange, and e to the pi comes out on top.

Last episode's mean value theorem was one of today's two engines. Later this chapter, the same chart becomes the first test for extrema. In the convexity episode, the same dictionary is applied to the slope itself. Later, comparing how fast functions grow is this recipe, run at infinity. In optimization courses, a gradient that keeps increasing means exactly convexity. So: name the function, read its slope's sign, conclude, and mind the footnote. Turn the inequality into a function, and let the slope do the work.
