11-03 Convergence tests: the p-integral as the measuring stick

Convergence tests: the p-integral as the measuring stick

1 · Motivation

Today we judge endless areas without ever finding them. Tonight's homework asks whether this endless area is finite. First instinct: find the area formula, then let the wall run away. Dead end: chapter eight already warned us no such formula exists. Second instinct: the curve dies out, so surely the area is finite? But one over x dies out too, and its area is infinite.

Dying out is not enough; how fast it dies out is everything. And the limit in the definition is usually out of reach. The goal: decide from the shape of the curve alone. Ruler one, for curves above the axis: compare sizes. Ruler two, for curves that swing across the axis: watch them cancel. The ruler in our drawer: the power family measured two episodes ago. The watershed sits exactly at one: steeper converges, flatter diverges.

2 · Statements

Theorem 11.1, the Cauchy criterion, the engine under every test.
Plain words: past some G, every slab from u₁ to u₂ is tiny.
Name any tolerance ε > 0; some starting point G answers it.
Small slabs, not small values: that is the whole criterion.
For 1/x, the slab from u to 2u is always ln 2.
What survives: if ∫ converges and lim f exists, that limit is 0.
For f ≥ 0, the running area F(u) only grows as u moves right.
So a ceiling suffices: increasing and bounded means convergent.
Theorem 11.2, the comparison test: the larger integral controls the smaller.
Picture f tucked under g, both above the axis.
If g's area is finite, f's area beneath it is trapped.
Read backwards: if f's area is infinite, so is g's.
Careful: under a divergent g, f may converge or diverge.
In practice, g is a ruler from the drawer: c/xᵖ.
Corollary 3, the limit form: compare by a limit, not an inequality.
xᵖ f(x) → λ says: far out, f looks like λ/xᵖ.
p > 1 with a finite λ, even λ = 0: the integral converges.
p ≤ 1 with a positive λ, even λ = +∞: the integral diverges.
λ = 0: f is smaller than the ruler; λ = +∞: f is bigger.
Other combinations give no conclusion; slide p and look again.

The Cauchy criterion, the engine under every test today. In plain words: far enough out, every slice of area is tiny. Name any tolerance, however strict, and a starting point answers it. It asks for small slices of area, not small heights. For one over x, the slice out to double distance never shrinks. Only this survives: if the curve has a limit, a finite area forces zero. When the curve stays above the axis, the area only grows as the wall moves. So a ceiling is enough, the monotone bounded key from chapter seven.

The comparison test: the bigger area controls the smaller one. Picture the smaller curve tucked under the bigger one. If the top area is finite, the area beneath it is trapped. Read backwards: an infinite area underneath makes the top one infinite. Careful: a divergent top curve tells us nothing about the one below. In practice, the top curve is a ruler from the drawer.

The limit form: compare with a limit instead of an inequality. Far out, the curve behaves like a scaled copy of the ruler. A steep ruler and a finite ratio, even zero, mean convergence. A flat ruler and a positive ratio, even infinite, mean divergence. A zero ratio means even smaller than the ruler; an infinite one, even bigger. Any other pairing says nothing; just slide the ruler and look again.

3 · Method

First example: any power times a decaying exponential. Multiply by the square: the exponential crushes every power anyway. A steep ruler and a zero ratio: convergent, for every power. Second example: the top grows nearly as fast as the bottom. Multiply by the square root: the ratio settles at one. A flat ruler and a ratio of one: divergent, and no antiderivative needed.

When the curve changes sign, compare its absolute value instead. On every slice, cancelling only shrinks the area, so the signed one follows. This is absolute convergence; the signed total never beats the unsigned one. Signed area settles, unsigned area blows up: that is conditional convergence. Think of pacing the dorm corridor during a phone call. Your position settles down, yet the step counter never stops climbing. Conditional convergence lives entirely on the cancelling signs.

The Dirichlet test, built for exactly this kind of cancellation. First: the running area of one factor never leaves a fixed band. Second: the other factor drifts one way only, and fades to zero. With both in place, the product's area converges. The Abel test hands the two jobs out another way. Here one area already settles; the other factor drifts one way, within bounds.

Back to the homework, and to its whole family at once. Steep powers are easy: the ruler caps it, so it converges absolutely. Flatter powers: the running area of sine stays within two. And the power fades steadily to zero, so Dirichlet says it converges. Is it absolute? To prove it is not, we need a floor beneath it. The floor: use the power one, square the sine; both only shrink it. Split it: a divergent piece minus a convergent one. The floor's area is infinite, so the unsigned area is too: conditional convergence. The whole family in one picture: steep is absolute, flat is conditional. Two arguments meet: one proves it converges, one proves it is not absolute.

Division of labour again, the group project from integration by parts. Its engine is the second mean value theorem, promised in chapter nine. It lets the fading factor speak only at the two ends of the slice. Each piece is capped by the band, so the slice is capped by the fading factor. As the factor fades, the slice gets tiny, and the Cauchy criterion finishes. Notice: we never needed a formula for the product's area, only estimates. Abel follows at once: subtract the limit of the tame factor.

4 · Outlook

At a singular point the same tools work, against last episode's power family. The watershed flips: near the point, slower blow-ups are the ones that converge. Both tests carry over too, with the singular point playing infinity.

First use: the bell curve has no area formula, yet its tail converges at once. So the normalization promised two episodes ago rests on solid ground. Second use: an integral from the optics of light bending around an edge. Substitute the square, and the Dirichlet test says it converges. Yet the curve never dies out: the area converges without small heights.

The series chapter replays this whole story, with sums in place of areas. Same skeleton, same comparison, and the same two tests, by the same names. And there, cancellation hides a real paradox: shuffle the order, and the total moves. So, before you compute anything, hold the tail against a ruler.
