12-06 Absolute and conditional: split the positives from the negatives

Absolute and conditional: split the positives from the negatives

1 · Motivation

Absolute and conditional convergence: we split the positives from the negatives. Last episode, Leibniz showed the alternating harmonic series settles down. But add up the sizes of its terms, and they grow forever. The series converges, yet its sizes pile up to infinity.

Laundry day. Lights in one bag, darks in the other, each on its own scale. Do the same with a series: positive terms one way, negative terms the other. For this series of squares, both bags settle at a finite weight. And the sum is just one weight minus the other. Now sort the alternating harmonic series the same way. Both bags break the scale. Each total grows without end. Infinity minus infinity, and still the series has a sum. So which series can be weighed bag by bag, and which only as a mixture?

Every series comes with a companion, the series of its absolute values. The definition: if the series of absolute values converges, the series is absolutely convergent. Conditionally convergent: the series converges, but its absolute values do not. The alternating harmonic series: the emblem of conditional convergence, as promised last episode. And the alternating series of squares is absolutely convergent. One corner stays empty forever, and that is Theorem twelve point twelve. Theorem twelve point twelve: an absolutely convergent series converges. Walk back and forth, and you never end up farther away than the distance you walked.

Each term splits into two pieces: its positive part and its negative part. A positive term keeps itself as its positive part; the negative part is zero. A negative term: positive part zero, negative part its size. Either way, both parts sit between zero and the size of the term. Add the two parts and you get the size; subtract them and you get the term back. The key fact: if a series converges only conditionally, both parts diverge. Each part alone runs off to positive infinity; only the mixture settles down.

3 · Method

Recall the Cauchy criterion from episode one: far out, every block is thinner than epsilon. Absolute convergence hands us exactly this for the sizes: a far block weighs almost nothing. The triangle inequality: a signed block can never outgrow the sum of its sizes. So the signed blocks are tiny as well; the signs even cancel. So by the Cauchy criterion, the series itself converges. One inequality, and we are done.

Draw each term as a bar: positive ones point up, negative ones point down. The positive part: press every negative bar flat to zero. The negative part: flatten the positives, flip the negatives up. Add the two rows together, and you get the sizes. Subtract the second row from the first, and the original terms come back. Every identity in the decomposition is simply this picture, read aloud.

First case: the sizes add up finitely. Then each part sits underneath the sizes, term by term. So by the comparison test from episode two, both parts converge. The sum is a difference of two finite totals. And it runs backwards: two finite parts add up to finite sizes.

Now the dangerous case: the series converges, but not absolutely. Suppose, just to test it, the positive part converged. Then linearity forces the negative part to converge as well. And then the sizes, the two parts added, converge too. But the sizes diverge. Contradiction. The same three steps rule out the negative part. Watch it happen on the alternating harmonic series. Conclusion: when convergence is only conditional, both parts climb to positive infinity. Say it twice: each part alone is infinite, and only the mixture converges. Keep these two infinite parts: next episode builds a machine out of them.

To classify a series, check the sizes first, then the series itself. Drill one: classify this series for every value of p. Pause here. The extra exponent fades, so the sizes act like a p series. When p is bigger than one, the sizes converge: absolutely convergent. For p between zero and one, the sizes diverge, but Leibniz still works on the tail: conditionally convergent. And when p is zero or below, the terms never shrink to zero: divergent.

Drill two, with sine of two over n. Pause and classify. The terms shrink steadily to zero, so the Leibniz test gives convergence. But the sizes hug a harmonic tail, so it is conditionally convergent.

Drill three, with factorials below, for any real x. Pause. Each size is at most one over n factorial, and those add up by the ratio test. So it is absolutely convergent, whatever x is.

Now read the parts: both finite means absolutely convergent. Exactly one of them infinite: the series diverges. Both infinite: it could be conditional or divergent, so ask the series itself.

Trap one: convergent means absolutely convergent. False. The alternating harmonic series breaks it. Trap two: a divergent series has divergent sizes. That one is true. But useless: it is Theorem twelve point twelve, read backwards. Trap one is the one that costs exam marks, and the whole point of the split.

Multiplying two series means adding up a whole table of products. Cauchy's theorem: if both converge absolutely, every order of the table gives the product of the sums. Adding along the diagonals gives the Cauchy product. Square the geometric series, one diagonal at a time. Each diagonal holds one more copy of the next power. For ratios below one in size, the total is one over the square of one minus the ratio. Why demand absolute convergence here? Because the order of adding must not matter. A table has no single natural order, and next episode gives the full answer.

4 · Outlook

First use: two classmates split a long sum, one part each. Subtracting their totals works exactly when the series converges absolutely. Under conditional convergence, neither classmate ever finishes.

Second use: in chapter fourteen, Cauchy products multiply exponential series. Each diagonal collapses by the binomial theorem, and the exponents simply add. Debts settled: last episode's cliffhanger now has a name, conditional convergence. And the two infinite parts are packed and ready for next episode. A glimpse ahead: an expectation is defined by exactly this split, positive minus negative. Both parts must be finite, and measure theory calls that integrability. Split the positives from the negatives, and see which bags can be weighed.
