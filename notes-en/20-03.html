20-03 The two types of line integral: two faces of one object

The two types of line integral: two faces of one object

1 · Motivation

Two types of line integral. Are they really two different things? Every evening you ride the same winding road to the library. First question: how far is the ride? Second question: how much did the wind help you?

The length is an integral of the first type. Direction plays no part. The wind's work is an integral of the second type, and direction matters. Two episodes ago we weighed a curve by length; last time we measured work by signed steps. One curve, two ways of keeping the books. Must they stay strangers?

And last episode left a puzzle. Ride the route backwards and the work changes sign. Yet a piece of arc length is never negative. So if the work can go negative, where is the minus sign hiding? Here is the claim of this episode: work is secretly an integral of the first type too. And what gets weighed? Only the part of the force that points along the road.

First ingredient: measure position by the distance already ridden. It is simply how far you have ridden from the start, along the road itself. Mark equal steps of distance, and every piece of road is equally long, bend or no bend.

At every point, the tangent direction points the way the distance grows. Picture it as the arrow in your map app, turning with every bend. Zoom in, and one tiny step splits into a sideways part and an upward part. The sideways share of the step is the cosine of the angle with the horizontal axis. The upward share is the cosine of the angle with the vertical axis.

In the course text's words, these are the direction cosines of the tangent. Check it: the tiny triangle has a right angle, so Pythagoras applies. Divide through, and the squares of the two cosines add up to exactly one. Let the arrow ride along. It keeps turning, yet its length stays one.

Now the key move. Pull the length of the step out of the displacement. Substitute both, and the length of the step stands outside, all by itself. This is the relation between the two types of line integral, in the course text's words. On the left, an integral of the second type. On the right, one of the first type. The bracket is just the dot product of the force with the tangent direction. The arrow has length one, so this is the force's tangential component.

Split the force at a point: one part along the road, one part straight across it. The part across the road only pushes you sideways. It does no work at all. Only the part along the road earns any work. Ride along, and that forward part grows, shrinks, and on some stretches even turns negative. Tailwind or headwind, the work adds up distance times the forward share of the wind.

In space, just add one more component and one more cosine. And here is the space version, in the course text's words. And again, the three squared cosines add up to exactly one.

3 · Method

Back to the puzzle. Each piece of length is positive, so where did the minus sign go? Ride it backwards: same road, but distance now counts from the library. The tangent direction flips. The arrow simply turns around. Both direction cosines change sign together. But the length of each step is untouched. Distance does not care which way you ride. So the minus sign moves into the integrand, carried by the cosines. The first type side now carries the sign, and the relation holds as before. The tailwind on the way out is a headwind on the way home. Only the arrow changed. Direction is stored in the tangent, never in the length.

A test ride: the straight segment from the origin to one, one. It meets both axes at forty five degrees, so both cosines are equal. So on this segment it becomes a first type integral of the sum, times that cosine. Try a simple force, with one parameter running both coordinates. Computed directly, it is the area under a straight line: one and a half. Now take the first type route. The length factor and the cosine cancel exactly. The same integral, the same one and a half. Both faces agree. It works backwards too: any first type integral here becomes a second type one. Check with the constant one: root two, exactly the segment's length. On a straight road the cosines never change, so switching faces costs nothing.

4 · Outlook

Now a harder ride: the circle where the unit sphere cuts a slanted plane. We want the work of this field once around the circle. The direction: counterclockwise, as seen looking in from the positive x axis. Writing down a parametrization of this tilted circle is a real chore. The tangent stands at right angles to the radius and to the normal of the plane.

Their cross product points straight along the tangent. On this circle the mixed products cancel out, so the length is root two. Divide by root two, and we know the direction cosines everywhere, no parametrization needed. Multiply out, and the whole numerator collapses to minus one. The tangential component is the same negative number everywhere: a steady headwind. For the radius, Pythagoras again: the radius squared comes out to two thirds. Length times that constant: about minus three point six three. No parametrization, no grinding. The direction cosines did all the work.

A second use: the forward part of a force never exceeds the whole force. So the work never exceeds the strongest force times the length of the road. Estimates like this need the first type face, where every length is positive.

The chapter's closing table. The first type weighs mass; the second measures work. What gets integrated: a plain number valued function, versus the force's tangential component. Does direction matter? No for the first type, yes for the second. And the last row ties them together: the relation between the two types. One object with two faces. The last two episodes were twins all along.

This tangent direction is an old friend: it built tangent lines to space curves. Green's theorem, later in the next chapter, runs on the oriented face. Yet its geometric reasoning keeps slipping through the face without direction.

One dimension up, the same split repeats. Surface integrals of the first type weigh. Surface integrals of the second type measure how much flows through. And the relation between them is carried by the normal, not the tangent. Green opens the family album, and Stokes' theorem closes it. One road, two questions, and in the end one integral.
