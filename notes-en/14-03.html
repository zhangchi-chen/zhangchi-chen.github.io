14-03 Summing power series: differentiate and integrate, back and forth

Summing power series: differentiate and integrate, back and forth

1 · Motivation

Today we learn to sum power series by differentiating and integrating, back and forth. We own exactly one closed form, the geometric series and its simple fraction. But tonight's problem set asks for three sums we have never seen.

Take the first one: its partial sums creep up toward two. It looks like exactly two, yet nothing we own can produce it. The trouble is the extra factor n sitting in every term.

Differentiate a power, and watch its exponent drop down in front. Integrate instead, and you divide by one more than the exponent. So the real question: can an infinite sum be differentiated one term at a time?

Picture the series as a photo that you keep editing, again and again. Integrating is a softening edit, and every coefficient gets divided down. Differentiating is the undo button, and each coefficient gets multiplied back. Which edits are safe, and where is it safe to make them? That is today's question.

3 · Method

The root test explains it in one line: under the root, that growing factor melts to one. So the limit superior never moves, and last episode's Cauchy Hadamard formula returns the same radius. Dividing changes nothing either: under the root, it creeps back up to one.

The course text's Example six starts from the one sum we own, the geometric series. The trunk is the geometric series, valid strictly inside the walls. Slide down one rung, and the derivative rail hands us its closed form. Climb up one rung, and a logarithm appears out of nowhere. Two rails from a single engine, and we never had to sum anything new. Check it by eye: the partial sums hug the curve between the walls.

Now the exam favourite, Example seven: find the sum function of this alternating series. The plan: climb two rungs, and each climb strips away one factor n. First move: pull one x out front, so integrating cancels exactly one factor. Climb once: integrate, divide out that x, and one factor is gone. Look at the ladder: we're halfway up, with just one factor left to strip. Climb again, and look who's waiting at the top: a geometric series we own. Its sum is one we know: the geometric series with the signs flipping. We've reached the top with a closed form in hand. Now press undo, twice.

Undo once by differentiating, and the middle rung gets its own closed form. Multiply that x back in, then press undo one more time. Notice that every single move happened strictly between the walls. Multiply the last x back in, and the answer is finally out of the oven. A sanity check at one tenth: the partial sums and the formula agree to four places. Goal, key move, result: read every problem of this kind in those three beats.

But the course text adds a careful note about the endpoints. Same radius, yes, but the domains are free to differ right at the walls. Sliding down can lose an endpoint, and climbing up can gain one. Think of filters that only kick in on export, right at the studio door.

A concrete case: the geometric series fails at both walls, its terms never die. Climb one rung, and at the left wall it now converges: a gain. At that wall the terms alternate and shrink: Leibniz, from twelve oh five, steps in. At the right wall we get the harmonic series, divergent as ever. Slide back down, and that hard won left endpoint is lost again. The note, in action. Trap defused: only the open interval between the walls is guaranteed to survive.

4 · Outlook

Payoff time: plug numbers into our identities, but only at safe points. Back to the first puzzle: put one half into the derivative rail. The rail gives four, and halving it gives exactly two. Mystery solved. Fun fact: that two is the average number of coin flips until the first head. Now Example seven at minus one half: the formula returns minus six. Unwrap the signs, and a second sum falls out for free: exactly six.

Now the wall itself, allowed only because the course text says the formula reaches it. Put in minus one, and the alternating series lands on a logarithm. Flip every sign: the alternating harmonic series is the log of two. So the constant A, carried through twelve oh five and twelve oh seven, is log two.

The closing card: the ladder runs both ways, but only strictly inside the radius. At the walls, every single step gets checked again by hand, the habit from fourteen oh one. Fourteen oh four turns the ladder around, starting from a function and finding its series. And fourteen oh five gives the endpoint fact we used quietly today its own theorem card.

A glimpse ahead: computer algebra tools work with series representations in exactly this way. And in later courses, special functions, the error function among them, are defined by such series. Inside the walls, edit and undo as freely as you like; on the walls themselves, check by hand.
