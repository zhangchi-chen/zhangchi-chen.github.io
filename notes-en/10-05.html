10-05 Physics applications: work, pressure, and force as integrals

Physics applications: work, pressure, and force as integrals

1 · Motivation

Today the integral goes to work on pressure, pumping, pulling, and power. Your physics homework has one rule: force is pressure times area. But the gate stands upright, and deeper water pushes harder. Plug in the top and you get nothing; plug in the bottom and it is way too big.

Emptying a tank is worse: every layer has its own climb to the top. A thin rod pulls on a small mass, and every piece tugs in its own direction. Even the 220 volts on the wall socket hides a strange kind of average. Four different jobs, one tool from the element method episode.

2 · Statements

Here is the work order we will run four times today.
Step one: name the quantity Φ spread along [a, b].
Step two: check that Φ is additive over subintervals.
Step three: on a thin sliver, freeze the law: dΦ = f(x)dx.
Step four: audit — the error is o(Δx), and the units match.
Step five: integrate. One card, four jobs.
Job one: a circular gate, radius 3 m, water up to its centre.
Here is the course text's statement.
First clause: equal depth, equal pressure, namely ρgx.
So slice horizontally: depth is constant along each strip.
A strip at depth x has half-width √(9 − x²).
Pressure ρgx times strip area 2√(9 − x²)dx: that is dP.
Audit: treating the strip as one depth costs only o(Δx).
Units check: pressure times area is force, in newtons.
Stack the strips from x = 0 down to x = 3.

Step three: on one thin slice the law holds still, so write the element. Step four: the error must shrink faster than the slice, and the units must agree. Step five: integrate. The same card serves all four jobs.

Job one: a round gate, three metres in radius, with water right up to its middle. Here is how the course text puts it. First idea: water at the same depth pushes equally hard. So we slice sideways, because along each strip the depth stays put. Pythagoras tells us how wide the strip is at that depth. Pressure times the strip's area: that is the strip's share of the force. Audit: pretending the strip sits at one depth costs a vanishingly small error. Units check: pressure times area really is a force. Now stack every strip, from the waterline down to the bottom. One substitution, and out comes a clean eighteen rho g.

3 · Method

Job two: pump a cone shaped pool completely dry. Picture a tall building, and every floor's water must be carried to the roof. Going down, the radius shrinks steadily, from fifteen metres to zero. Here is how the course text states it for the cone. Volume gives the weight, and that layer's climb turns it into work. Audit: from the top of a thin layer to its bottom, the climb barely changes. The same small move from the element method episode. And the units land on joules, just as work should. Now add up every floor, from the rim down to the tip. Expand, integrate, and the whole pumping bill arrives in one line.

Job three: a thin rod with its mass spread evenly. A small mass waits right above the middle, at a fixed height. Newton's law of gravitation tells how hard each little piece pulls. The tempting shortcut: just add up all those sizes. And it is wrong, because every pull points a different way.

Move in day at the dorm: two friends pull one box, at right angles. The box feels five, not seven: pulls add as arrows, not as numbers. So the sizes do not add up, and step two of our card fails.

The fix: break each pull into a sideways part and a downward part. Mirror pieces pull sideways equally hard in opposite ways, so those cancel. The downward parts all point the same way, so these really do add. Integrate the downward parts, and the course text's answer appears. On a test rod, the shortcut overshoots by about eleven percent.

Job four: the wall voltage swings back and forth across a resistor. At each instant, power is voltage squared over resistance, and never negative. Energy piles up moment by moment, so a full cycle's work is an integral. Shave the humps into the troughs, and the wave levels off at one half. So a full cycle's work has a neat closed form. Spread that work evenly over the cycle, and you get the average power. And here is the course text's own statement. Average power is the mean value we met before, wearing a physics name. The effective value is the steady voltage that would heat the resistor just as much. It has to be a squared average, because the plain average of the voltage is zero. So the 220 on the socket is an effective value; the real peaks run higher.

4 · Outlook

One last habit: never ship an answer without a sanity check. The gate holds back as much as eighteen cubic metres of water weighs. And it sits safely between zero and the deepest case guess. Shrink the rod to a dot, and Newton's point mass law comes back. And peaks near 311 volts sit perfectly well with a 220 volt label.

Beyond today: stretching a spring, the work grows with the square of the stretch. The 1500 watts on your dorm kettle is an average power too. The element method ran all four jobs, and the units check watched every element. And the integral mean value theorem came back as average power.

Next chapter, the interval runs off to infinity, and the bookkeeping stays the same. Later, the same card weighs a flat plate whose mass spreads over a region. But what if all you have is a measured curve, and no formula at all? Or the antiderivative exists but cannot be written with familiar functions. Then the last section of the course text helps, with trapezoids and parabolas. Numerical analysis will later tell exactly how far off they can be. Over regions and surfaces, balance points and moments of inertia run the same card. Slice it, freeze the law, check it, add it up: integrals doing physics.
