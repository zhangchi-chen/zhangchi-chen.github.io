Continuous functions on a closed region: old theorems in a new home

1 · Motivation

Continuous functions on a closed region, where old theorems move into a new home. On a closed interval, a continuous function always reaches its highest point. A classmate asks where the first coordinate is largest on the open unit square. Every point you pick is beaten by one a little further to the right. The right edge would win, but the edge is not part of the set. Close the square, and the maximum value one sits right on that edge.

Back in four oh six, closed intervals came with four theorems. Today is moving day, and all four pieces of furniture go into the plane. But a new room can hide new traps. The answer comes down to two phrases: bounded closed, and connected.

3 · Method

Who does the heavy lifting? The completeness theorems of the plane, from sixteen oh one. Frame one: if it were unbounded, pick points where it climbs ever higher. Frame two: the points stay bounded, so the accumulation theorem gives a convergent subsequence. Frame three: the region is closed, so the limit point cannot escape it. Frame four: continuity at the limit keeps the values tame, yet they blow up. Four frames, four tools: picking points, the accumulation theorem, closedness, and continuity.

Bounded, yes. But is the very top value actually reached? Suppose it is never reached: every value stays strictly below. Build the reciprocal of the gap: its denominator stays positive, so it is continuous. By part one, this continuous new function must be bounded. But the values creep up to the top, so the reciprocal explodes. Contradiction. So some point really does reach the top. It is the old trick from the line, the one in seven oh three, played again. For the minimum, flip the function and run it again.

Uniform continuity means one ruler for the whole region. We met this one ruler idea in four oh five, and again in thirteen oh two. Then sixteen oh two, for every path, and today, for every pair of points. Stop four. Thirteen oh two had one index for every point; here one radius serves every pair. Proof skeleton: suppose not, so one fixed tolerance defeats every candidate radius. So we get pairs of points, closer and closer, whose values stay apart. A subsequence of the first points converges, and closedness keeps its limit inside. Each partner is dragged along to the very same limit, by the triangle inequality. Continuity at that one point pulls both values together, and the gap collapses.

The intermediate value theorem needs a road from the low point to the high point. The course text walks a polygonal line inside the region. On one segment the values cross the target, and the one variable theorem finishes the job.

Now take the two open quadrants from sixteen oh one. Let the function be the sign of the first coordinate: one up here, minus one down there. It is continuous on the set, because near each point it is simply constant. It takes one and minus one, yet never the zero in between. Two campuses with no bridge, so the rumor simply cannot cross. Connectedness is a load bearing wall, and the course text says so plainly.

Here is the trap card: drop one word, and you lose one theorem. Bounded but not closed: the first coordinate, on the open unit square. Its supremum, one, is never attained: a trophy shelf with no wall to lean on. Theorem 16.8 fails here, because closedness was holding it up. Not connected: the sign exhibit, and Theorem 16.10 falls. Closing the set will not save it: two separate closed disks fail the same way.

One last trap, and it points forward: continuity in each variable separately. Let the function be zero on both axes and one everywhere else. Along the horizontal axis it is always zero, so it is continuous in that variable. The same holds along the vertical axis. But walk in along the diagonal: it stays one, yet the origin holds zero. So, as a function of the point, it is not continuous at the origin. Continuity in each variable separately is not continuity. In seventeen oh one, partial derivatives will walk exactly this fault line.

Use one: on a bounded closed set, some point is nearest to your dorm. Distance to the dorm is continuous, so Theorem 16.8 hands us the minimum. Use two, photo zoom: a single pixel size works for the whole picture. Take the radius from Theorem 16.9; small enough cells keep every error under tolerance. The debts are paid: four oh six stated them, seven oh three proved them on the line. Today the completeness tools of sixteen oh one paid for the move. In any number of dimensions, all four theorems move in word for word. In metric spaces, bounded closed is not enough; it must be upgraded to compact. Looking ahead: continuous on compact is among the most reused theorems in mathematics. Existence results in optimization quote it, and so does the topology of metric spaces. So does the analysis of loss landscapes in machine learning. Same furniture, new home: three theorems lean on bounded closed, one leans on connectedness.
