Motivation

  1. Sum, product, quotient: the extra little patch of area.
  2. Tonight's homework: differentiate f(x) = (x²+1)(x³−2x).
  3. The tempting move: differentiate each factor, then multiply.
  4. At x = 1 the shortcut gives 2 · (3 − 2) = 2.
  5. But look at the graph: at x = 1 the tangent is flat.
  6. A slope of 2 would cut steeply through the curve.
  7. Safe road: multiply out first, f(x) = x⁵ − x³ − 2x.
  8. Then f′(x) = 5x⁴ − 3x² − 2, so f′(1) = 0.
  9. Zero, not two: the tempting move is simply wrong.
  10. And for x sin x, multiplying out is not even possible.
  11. We need a rule for products themselves.
  12. A dorm room's rent is split by floor area: 4 m by 5 m.
  13. Re-measured, each side turns out 0.1 m longer.
  14. Quick guess: the extra is 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01 m².
  15. Truth: 4.1 × 5.1 = 20.91, so 0.91 m² extra — 91 times more.
  16. Where did the missing 0.9 m² hide? That is our question.

What the statement says

  1. Theorem 5.4: derivatives of sums and differences add and subtract.
  2. In plain words: the change of a sum is the sum of the changes.
  3. Theorem 5.5: in a product, the factors take turns being differentiated.
  4. One term lets only u change; the other lets only v change.
  5. Theorem 5.6, the quotient rule — it needs v(x₀) ≠ 0.
  6. Its minus sign and its v² look arbitrary; we will derive both.
  7. The course text's list puts all the rules in one place.
  8. Two special cases hide inside: a constant factor, and 1/v.
  9. Each rule is a statement about a single point x₀.
  10. The hypothesis: u and v are differentiable at that point.
  11. The quotient rule adds one more: the denominator is non-zero there.

Method / proof core

  1. Sum rule: split the difference quotient; limit arithmetic (03-04) does the rest.
  2. Sums were the warm-up. Products are the real story.
  3. At x, the room is u(x) by v(x); its area is u(x)v(x).
  4. Step from x to x + Δx: the sides grow by Δu and Δv.
  5. All the extra area lives in this L-shaped border.
  6. One long strip: Δu times v.
  7. A second strip: u times Δv.
  8. And one tiny corner tile: Δu·Δv.
  9. So the extra area is: strip, plus strip, plus corner.
  10. Divide by Δx: each strip turns into a rate.
  11. Here is the dangerous tile: does the corner really vanish?
  12. Δv → 0, because differentiable v is continuous: Theorem 5.1, from 05-02.
  13. So the corner is o(Δx): it shrinks faster than Δx itself.
  14. Let Δx → 0: two strips survive, (uv)′ = u′v + uv′.
  15. Your extra rent is two strips; the corner tile is pocket change.
  16. Forget the formula? Draw the rectangle and read it off.
  17. Now the classic mistake: (uv)′ = u′v′.
  18. It's the roommate who pays for the corner tile and skips both strips.
  19. Test u = v = x: (x²)′ at x = 1 is 2.
  20. The mistake gives 1 · 1 = 1. Half the answer is missing.
  21. The missing half: the square's two strips, x·Δx each.
  22. Second test: x sin x at x = π. The product rule says −π.
  23. The mistake gives 1 · cos π = −1. Wrong again.
  24. The graph settles it: near π the curve drops at slope −π.
  25. The mistake forgets both strips, and the strips are the whole answer.
  26. Quotients next, derived not memorized: write u/v = u · (1/v).
  27. Then only one new piece is missing: the derivative of 1/v.
  28. When v grows, 1/v shrinks: that is where the minus sign comes from.
  29. Over a common denominator, the minus sign appears by itself.
  30. By continuity, the bottom tends to v(x₀)², safely non-zero.
  31. Reciprocal rule: (1/v)′ = −v′/v².
  32. Check with v = x: −1/x², exactly what the power rule says.
  33. Now the product rule stitches the two pieces together.
  34. Common denominator v², and out comes (u′v − uv′)/v².
  35. The minus in the numerator is the reciprocal's minus, not magic.
  36. So the minus always sits on the term holding v′.
  37. Two steps, one new idea, nothing memorized twice.
  38. Back to tonight's homework, now with the product rule.
  39. Two terms, one per strip, and no degree-five expansion.
  40. At x = 1 it's −2 + 2 = 0: the safe road's zero.
  41. Second exhibit: tan x, which nobody can multiply out.
  42. Quotient rule on sin x / cos x: numerator cos²x + sin²x.
  43. That numerator is 1, so tan′x = 1/cos²x.
  44. The graph agrees: slope 1 at 0, steeper toward the edges.
  45. The unexpandable x sin x? One step: sin x + x cos x.
  46. Constants ride along: (cu)′ = cu′, since a fixed side adds no strip.
  47. Habit: pull the constant out first; it saves a line every time.

Applications and outlook

  1. Use one: the class T-shirt order, a 60 setup fee plus 8 per shirt.
  2. The average cost per shirt, A(q) = C(q)/q, is a quotient.
  3. Quotient rule: A′(q) = (8q − C(q))/q² = −60/q², always negative.
  4. In the picture, the average is the chord's slope, and it keeps flattening.
  5. Each extra shirt costs 8, below the average, so the average drops.
  6. Use two: paying in another currency, cost = price × exchange rate.
  7. Both drift, so the cost changes by two strips: p′r + pr′.
  8. Price up 2%, rate up 1%: cost up about 3%.
  9. Exactly 3.02%: the extra 0.02% is the corner tile.
  10. Remember: the corner vanished thanks to 05-02's continuity.
  11. Next, 05-04 adds the chain and inverse rules, completing the toolbox.
  12. Then in 05-05, the derivative table becomes pure lookup.
  13. The product rule's n-fold echo returns as the Leibniz formula in 05-06.
  14. This two-strip picture is the last time a rule is this visual.
  15. From the chain rule on, the pictures become gears and trees.
  16. With partial derivatives later, the product rule survives unchanged.
  17. Why? Along one direction, it is the same increment algebra.
  18. Remember the room: two strips count, the corner vanishes.
