15-06 From zeta(2) to L-functions: a gallery of special values

From zeta(2) to L-functions: a gallery of special values

1 · Motivation

From zeta of two to L functions, a gallery of special values. Our dorm corridor has a wall where numbers hang in frames. Four of the frames came from the last episode. Each little tag says which auxiliary function made it.

Then a friend asks the obvious thing: why not cube it and get zeta of three? The series comes out fine, yet zeta of three never appears. Meanwhile, four frames on the wall are still empty. Today three of them get filled, and one stays empty, with an honest label.

We need a second production line: the Parseval equality, which squares coefficients. Squaring reaches fourth powers, which no single point of that series can. So, which line makes which value, and why does zeta of three have no seat?

Here is the Parseval equality, as the course text states it in an exercise. The left side measures how big f is, over one whole period. The right side squares every coefficient and adds them all up. One size, two ways to measure it, like Pythagoras with endless directions. Careful here: the constant term carries its own special weight.

Here are the gallery values, in full. Lambda keeps only odd denominators, and beta also flips the signs. Zeta of two and four come from the course text; beta of three and the chi three value are companions.

And here is the auxiliary function that will make beta of three. Between zero and pi it is a parabola arch, zero at both ends. Only odd harmonics appear, each shrinking like one over its cube. At the midpoint, those sines take turns between plus one and minus one. Three empty frames, and three routes: square, design, and sieve.

3 · Method

First frame: zeta of four, using nothing but the coefficients we already have. The last episode already worked out every coefficient. The left side is one clean integral: two fifths of pi to the fourth. Now comes the dangerous step: the constant term. The right weight gives two ninths of pi to the fourth, not one ninth. Squaring wipes out every sign; all the terms turn positive. Put the two sides together, and sixteen zeta of four appears. Subtract, divide, and zeta of four is pi to the fourth over ninety. The first new frame goes up, exactly the course text exercise. A quick check: the partial sums creep right up to it.

Second frame, beta of three, and this time we start from the series. We want odd harmonics only, shrinking like one over the cube. If the function is zero at both ends, the slowest part dies. And a constant second derivative leaves exactly the cube term. The arch is symmetric about its middle, so every even harmonic cancels. Extend it as an odd function, and this is the quoted sine series. Plug in the midpoint: the arch's peak height. The sines alternate, and beta of three is pi cubed over thirty two. And the point is safe: no jump there, and the series converges absolutely. Second frame up: the old recipe, run in reverse.

The last frame needs a pattern of badges first. At the door, each guest gets a badge in turn: plus, minus, blank, and again. That pattern is called chi three: plus one, minus one, zero, over and over. Chi four cycles every four guests: plus one, zero, minus one, zero. Chi four is simply the sine, sampled at quarter turns. Both are Dirichlet characters, for three and for four. Careful: not every rotation qualifies; a character must also respect products. Its L function weights each badge by its guest's reciprocal, and adds. And beta was an L function all along, for chi four.

The tool for the job is the sawtooth series. It's the old x series, shifted, and its convergence was vetted in twelve oh eight. Hop around the circle a third of a turn at a time, and watch the heights. That's chi three again, just scaled. At that point, the sawtooth stands at pi over six. Divide out the scale, and the third frame's value lands. No jump at that point, and the value is about 0.6046. The same sawtooth at a quarter turn gives back beta of one. Third frame up: the old sieve now has a proper name.

Now the real question: why does zeta of three get no seat? Let's draw the seating chart, one column for each production line. On the left, even functions with cosines, and even powers below. On the right, odd functions with sines, and odd powers below. Parseval squares things, and a power times itself is always even. Zeta of three needs an odd power, with every sign a plus. Cosines give all pluses at zero, but the powers are even. Mismatch. Sines have the right powers, but at zero every term vanishes. Anywhere else, sooner or later a sine turns negative. So zeta of three stands in the aisle, between the columns. Careful: we have ruled out these lines, not every formula there could be.

4 · Outlook

Zeta of three was proved irrational in 1978, so it is not for lack of trying, yet no odd zeta value has a known closed form, even today. Here's the finished wall, each frame tagged with its production line. All of it ran on episodes fifteen oh five and fifteen oh four. And zeta itself, continuous and differentiable, was vetted in thirteen oh four.

First use: zeta of four is a faster recipe for pi. Ten terms, times ninety, a fourth root: already 3.1414. The zeta of two route, after ten terms, is still stuck at 3.049. Second use: Parseval is an energy budget, split across harmonics. For x squared, the constant and one harmonic already carry 96.6 percent. From constants to sound: the chapter finale takes it from here.

Looking ahead, this wall is the ground floor of analytic number theory. Up there, L functions generalize, gain Euler products, and encode the primes. And today's values are the display pieces on that ground floor. The sieve went full time: Dirichlet proved these L values are never zero, so every progression with coprime start and step holds infinitely many primes.
