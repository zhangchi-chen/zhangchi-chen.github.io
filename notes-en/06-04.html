06-04 Cauchy's mean value theorem: two processes, one shared instant

Cauchy's mean value theorem: two processes, one shared instant

1 · Motivation

Cauchy's mean value theorem, two processes and one shared instant. Two roommates start their downloads at eight and both stop at nine. A downloads three gigabytes at a steady pace; B starts slow, then speeds up. In total, A got exactly three times as much as B. Was there a single moment when A was going exactly three times as fast as B?

Let's draw how A's speed compared with B's changes over the hour. The ratio keeps falling, and at half past eight it is exactly three.

Lagrange's theorem only ever looks at one roommate at a time. The tempting move is to use it once for each roommate, then divide. But that gives you two separate instants, not one. Today's theorem promises a single instant that both of them share.

Here is the theorem, exactly as the course text states it. Clause one: both graphs run unbroken over the whole interval, ends included. Clause two: inside the interval, both have a tangent line everywhere. Clause three: one may pause, but never both at the same moment. Clause four: B's start and end values differ, so B really gets somewhere. Then, at some instant inside, the ratio of speeds matches the ratio of totals. The left side lives at one instant; the right side covers the whole window. And it is the same instant on top and on the bottom. That is the whole point.

Now track both at once: one roommate across, the other up, moment by moment. The moving point traces a parametric curve, with time as the hidden parameter. The total ratio is the slope of the chord joining the two ends. The speed ratio is the slope of the tangent at that moment. 3 · Method

Here is the proof idea: push the picture down until the chord lies flat. That push is the auxiliary function: A's progress minus a fixed multiple of B's. At both ends, the auxiliary function is exactly zero. Equal heights at the ends, smooth in between: that is Rolle's home turf. Rolle gives an inside point where its tangent is flat. So at that instant, A's speed is a fixed multiple of B's. Here comes the dangerous step: we want to divide, so B's speed must not be zero. If B were frozen at that instant, the equation would freeze A as well. Both frozen at the same moment is exactly what clause three rules out. So B's speed is not zero there, we may divide, and the theorem drops out. Same chord shift as in Lagrange's proof; only the thing we subtract has changed. Later in this chapter, this move becomes the first template for building auxiliary functions.

Now let's test that tempting shortcut on a square and a cube, from zero to one. For the square, Lagrange's instant lands at one half. For the cube, the instant lands at about 0.577. Put them on one timeline, and the two cursors drift apart. It's like comparing two friends' progress from screenshots taken at different moments. Divide them, and the top and bottom come from two different instants. And its value always matches the chord ratio, which is why this trap is so quiet. But no single instant produced it, and a single instant is the whole claim. Cauchy's own instant for this pair is two thirds. Three different instants: a half, about 0.577, and two thirds. Never merge them.

Why clause four? If B ends where it started, the total ratio divides by zero. The extreme case, where B never moves at all, is ruled out right here. Why clause three? Square and cube again, now from minus one to one. At zero both speeds vanish, and the moving point stalls at a sharp corner. The chord is flat, yet no tangent anywhere is flat, so no instant works. Rolle's point lands exactly on the stall, where division is impossible.

4 · Outlook

Now an example from the course text, a mean value formula with a logarithm. Reading skill: look at what sits beside the change in f. That logarithm is really a change in a known function, the log itself. Make the log the second function; all four clauses then hold. Cauchy now trades the ratio for a single instant's speed. Clear the fraction, and that is exactly the example's formula. The habit: a known function's increment shows up, so invite it in.

A second use: walk along an arc of the unit circle. Cauchy promises some tangent parallel to the chord. By symmetry, the tangent touches exactly at the middle angle. And out falls a neat trig identity, no guessing needed.

This theorem is the engine that drives the next episode. L'Hôpital's rule is proved from exactly this theorem. Whenever two quantities are driven by one hidden parameter, like time, temperature or sample size, at some instant their rates compare exactly as their totals do. L'Hôpital is the limit version of this sentence, and statistics meets the same shape as relative risk. Two processes, one shared instant. That is Cauchy's mean value theorem.
