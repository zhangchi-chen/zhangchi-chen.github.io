05-06 Higher derivatives: differentiate, then queue again

Higher derivatives: differentiate, then queue again

1 · Motivation

Higher derivatives: take the result, and line up once more.

The homework wants the hundredth derivative of one small product. By hand, that means a hundred rounds of the product rule. Round one: the exponential stays put. Two more rounds, and the bracket keeps changing. Read down the columns, and a pattern starts to peek out. But guessing the hundredth row is a gamble.

Puzzle two: a wiggle that gets squeezed flat near zero. It has a derivative everywhere, yet no second derivative at zero. Puzzle three: a curve that looks like one smooth S. Its first derivative is fine, but at zero the second is missing. So today's question: what does it take to differentiate again, and again?

Picture differentiation as one cafeteria counter. A function goes in on a tray; its derivative comes out. For the second derivative, take what came out and line up again. Each pass works only on whatever came out last. So the nth derivative is the same counter, passed n times.

Here is how the course text defines the second derivative. And each higher order is built from the one just below it.

First clause: the derivative itself must be differentiable at that point. Take the sine curve: its slopes trace the cosine. The second derivative is the slope of that lower graph. A slope of slopes, and here it is minus the sine. Second clause: the same old quotient, only now built from the derivative. Hidden demand: the derivative must exist all around the point, not just at it.

Third clause: each order is defined from the one before. The tall notation means: differentiate, n times. Read it as one symbol, not as a fraction. The second differential is another object, saved for a later episode.

Now send three classics through the counter. The cube shrinks pass by pass, down to a constant, then to zero. Each pass brings one exponent down front, and they multiply to six. In general you reach n factorial, then one more pass gives zero. The sine turns a quarter of a dial on each pass. Cosine, minus sine, minus cosine, and after four passes it is home. Why a quarter turn? The cosine is the sine slid left by a quarter period. So the sine's derivatives are just quarter turns, counted. The cosine rides the same dial, one quarter ahead. The exponential is the fixed point: no pass changes it at all. The course text records all three, one line each.

3 · Method

Back to products: what do many passes do to a product? One pass: each new term differentiates exactly one factor. Two passes: four paths, each one choosing which factor to hit, twice. The two mixed paths land on the same term, so it counts twice. Three passes: eight paths, merging into one, three, three, one. To land a given term, just choose which passes hit the second factor. The number of such choices is exactly the coefficient.

Now put the binomial theorem right above it. Same shape: powers have become orders of derivatives. Expanding a power picks one of the two letters from every bracket. Differentiating picks one of the two factors at every pass. Same counting, so the very same coefficients. And this is the Leibniz formula. Careful: zero passes leave the factor itself, not one. Check four passes: one, four, six, four, one.

Back to the homework product, now at any order. Give the square the second slot: from the third pass on, it is zero. The exponential takes the first slot and never changes. In the full sum, only the first three terms survive. The three survivors add up to one neat line. Check: the first three orders give back our three rounds. And the hundredth derivative fits in one line. Tip: give the polynomial the slot that dies, and the sum stays short.

Now we pay episode two's old debt: puzzle two. Fill the hole at zero with the value zero. The graph is trapped between two parabolas. At zero the quotient gets squeezed to zero, so the slope there is zero. Away from zero, the product and chain rules give the derivative. The first term fades away; the second keeps swinging forever. Arbitrarily close to zero, it still hits both one and minus one. So the derivative has no limit at zero: it breaks there. If a second derivative existed, the derivative would have to be continuous. It isn't, so there is no second derivative at zero. Two roommates: one always calm, one wild only at midnight. The calm one tames the wild one for one pass, but not for two.

Puzzle three: a parabola on the right, an upside-down one on the left. First order, straight from the definition: the slope at zero is zero. Altogether the derivative is a V shape with a corner at zero. Second order: back to the definition, this time with the derivative. The quotient is two on the right, and minus two on the left. Two different one-sided limits, so there is no second derivative at zero. The blind shortcut: differentiate the right-hand formula twice and get two. That two is only the right-hand half of the story. Rule: at a join, every new order goes back to the definition. Each step uses quotients of the previous derivative.

4 · Outlook

Where it shows up: the dorm lift pulling away from the ground floor. The first derivative is its speed, the second its acceleration. Your stomach feels the second derivative, not the speed. Back to the dial: two quarter turns flip the sign. The simplest spring obeys exactly that rule, and the sine fits it. Next chapter's Taylor formula is built from exactly these values.

Counting how many passes survive sorts functions into classes. Puzzle three is in the first class, not the second. Puzzle two is differentiable, yet not even in the first class. Later, in function series: may we differentiate an infinite sum, again and again?

One counter, queue again: that is every higher derivative. Leibniz's coefficients count which passes hit which factor. And at a join, every order goes back to the definition.
