Motivation

  1. The derivative: speed at this instant, slope at this point.
  2. Your takeout is on its way, and the app shows a live speed.
  3. The average over the whole trip is easy: distance over time.
  4. But right now the app says 21.6. Measured over what time?
  5. At one instant, the rider moves zero distance in zero time.
  6. Zero over zero tells us nothing at all.
  7. So shrink the window instead: one minute, ten seconds, one second.
  8. The averages settle on 21.6, and that is the speed right now.
  9. So speed right now secretly means a limit of averages.
  10. Second puzzle: what exactly is a tangent line?
  11. School answer: a line meeting the curve at exactly one point.
  12. But the line x = 1 meets y = x² once, and is no tangent.
  13. And y = x³ crosses its own tangent at the origin.
  14. Counting meeting points fails both ways. We need a better idea.
  15. Idea: take a nearby point Q and draw the secant PQ.
  16. Slide Q toward P, and the secant swings toward one position.
  17. That limiting position is the tangent. Again, a limit.

What the statement says

  1. The course text makes the speed story precise.
  2. On the distance–time graph, average speed is a secant's slope.
  3. Let t approach t₀ from either side: the averages close in.
  4. If the limit exists, it is the instantaneous velocity at t₀.
  5. The tangent is defined the same way, as a limiting position.
  6. P(x₀, y₀) stays fixed while Q runs along the curve toward it.
  7. The secant PQ swings; its limiting position PT is the tangent.
  8. So the tangent's slope is the limit of the secant slopes.
  9. Now put the two formulas side by side.
  10. Rename t as x and s as f: it is the same formula.
  11. Two doors, one room: output change over input change, then a limit.
  12. Peel off both stories, and what remains is the definition.
  13. First, f is defined on a whole neighbourhood of x₀.
  14. So x can come from either side, as close as we like.
  15. Second: the limit of the quotient must exist…
  16. …then f is differentiable at x₀, and the limit is f′(x₀).
  17. Increment form: Δx = x − x₀, and Δy follows.
  18. The derivative is the limit of Δy/Δx as Δx shrinks to 0.
  19. Δy/Δx is the average rate of change: the difference quotient.
  20. The derivative is the rate of change of f at x₀ itself.
  21. One sentence to keep: output change over input change, then limit.

Method / proof core

  1. Let's compute one from scratch: f(x) = x² at x₀ = 1.
  2. Take a step Δx and land on Q, back on the curve.
  3. The difference quotient is ((1 + Δx)² − 1)/Δx.
  4. Expand and cancel Δx, which is never 0: the quotient is 2 + Δx.
  5. As Q slides in, the secant slope 2 + Δx closes in on 2.
  6. So f′(1) = 2: computed from the limit, not guessed.
  7. Through (1, 1) with slope 2, the tangent is y = 2x − 1.
  8. Check: the parabola stays above this line, touching only at P.
  9. Now look at the quotient itself, as a function of x.
  10. Here it equals x + 1, with a hole at x = 1.
  11. At x = 1 it reads 0/0, and that point never enters.
  12. The limit only looks nearby, and the hole sits at height 2.
  13. It's the function limit of 03-04; every chapter 3 instinct applies.
  14. A tempting shortcut: look at x₀ + h and x₀ − h together.
  15. This symmetric quotient divides by the full width 2h.
  16. When f is differentiable, it does tend to f′(x₀).
  17. The trap is the converse: it can exist when f′(x₀) does not.
  18. Example 2 in the course text: f(x) = |x| at x₀ = 0.
  19. The quotient |x|/x is 1 on the right and −1 on the left.
  20. Two values, no single limit: f′(0) does not exist.
  21. Now the symmetric quotient: its top |h| − |−h| is always 0.
  22. So its limit is 0, and everything looks calm.
  23. Yet Example 2 says f′(0) does not exist. The shortcut fooled us.
  24. The symmetric secant hops over the corner and never looks at it.
  25. Like a classmate averaging quizzes 1 and 3, never noticing quiz 2 is missing.
  26. The derivative anchors at the point and checks each side separately.
  27. Lesson: a symmetric limit existing does not make f differentiable.
  28. Differentiability is decided one point at a time.
  29. A strange example: f(x) = x²·D(x), D the Dirichlet function.
  30. On rationals f equals x²; on irrationals it equals 0.
  31. At 0 the quotient is x·D(x), squeezed between −|x| and |x|.
  32. The wedge closes, so the limit is 0: f′(0) = 0.
  33. At any other x₀, f keeps jumping between about x₀² and 0.
  34. The top won't shrink while the bottom does: the quotient blows up.
  35. So f′ exists at exactly one point: x = 0.
  36. f′ lives only where the limit exists: for |x|, at every x ≠ 0.
  37. Easiest case of all: a constant function f ≡ C.
  38. The top is always C − C = 0, so f′(x) = 0 everywhere.
  39. That is the first entry of a derivative table we will build later.
  40. Notation: f′(x₀), y′ at x₀, or dy/dx at x₀.
  41. Read dy/dx as one symbol for now; 05-08 makes it a real quotient.

Applications and outlook

  1. The same quotient keeps coming back in other clothes.
  2. A rod whose first x cm weigh x² g: density 2 g/cm at 1 cm.
  3. Printing class notes: the slope of the cost curve is the marginal cost.
  4. At 500 copies it gives 2 yuan, about the cost of one more copy.
  5. Current and reaction rate too: every rate is this one sentence.
  6. In later courses, the objects become vectors and matrices.
  7. But the sentence never changes: output change over input change, then limit.
  8. Two questions stay open for episode 05-02.
  9. Differentiable at a point: must f be continuous there?
  10. And at a corner like |x|, what if we look from one side only?
  11. Speed now, slope here: one quotient, one limit.
