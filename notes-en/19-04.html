19-04 Gamma at one half: the pi inside the normal distribution

Gamma at one half: the pi inside the normal distribution

1 · Motivation

Meet the most famous curve in all of statistics: the bell. Its whole formula fits on a fingernail. Smooth, always positive, the same on both sides. Its tails vanish faster than any exponential ever could.

So how much area hides under the whole bell? Start with the right half, from zero out to infinity. The old plan from chapter nine: find an antiderivative, plug in the ends. But the bell has no elementary antiderivative, and that is proven. Times a constant, its antiderivative even has a name: the error function. It flattens out at some height. But at which number? Last episode left us owing one value: gamma at one half. And yet that value and this area are secretly the same problem.

3 · Method

The front door is locked, so let's walk around the building. The trick: take a second copy, in a new variable, and multiply. Squared, the area becomes a volume; this is the step we borrow. Here's that volume: the bell, spun round into a smooth hill. The height only cares how far you are from the origin. A round hill calls for round coordinates: polar coordinates.

Slice the quarter disk into thin curved strips, like tree rings. Each tiny patch is bigger the farther out it sits. That extra r is our old friend from chapter eighteen: the Jacobian. The integral splits cleanly into an angle part and a radius part. The angle part is nothing but a quarter turn. On its own, the radius bell would be just as locked. With that r attached, the door simply swings open. Push R outward, and the radius area fills up toward one half. Multiply the two parts, and the quarter disk is done. Send the radius to infinity: the quadrant gives pi over four. So the square is pi over four, if our borrowed step holds.

To play it safe, keep every region finite, and squeeze from both sides. Inside the square sits the quarter disk of radius a. And the square fits inside a bigger quarter disk that touches its corner. Picture the hill as a photo, and crop it three ways. A bigger frame always holds more of the photo's brightness. On the square, the volume is just the single variable area, squared. Now let all three frames grow at once. Both round totals close in on pi over four, and the square is trapped. Same photo, round album or square album: in the end the totals agree.

So the half area, squared, is pi over four; take the positive root. Double it, and gamma at one half is the square root of pi. Careful: pi over four is the square, not the area itself. And the pi? It came from the quarter turn we swept around. To be honest, chapter twenty one proves these steps; until then, we owe it.

4 · Outlook

With gamma at one half in hand, the recurrence starts hopping up. One hop up: multiply by one half. Next hop: multiply by three halves. Every value is a plain fraction times the same constant.

Here's an exercise from the course text that uses them right away. The curve is symmetric, so double the right half. Match it with the alternative form, and the power fixes s. So the right half is half of gamma at three halves; double it, and the exercise is done.

Next tool: squeeze the bell sideways with a positive dial. A rescaling stretches the squeezed bell back into the standard one. The squeezed area is the old one times a simple factor. Turn the dial down: the bell spreads out, and its area grows.

Example seven in the course text hides a cosine wave inside the bell. Step one: differentiate right under the integral sign. Allowed: the M-test gives uniform convergence, two episodes back, doing real work. Step two: integrate by parts, and the boundary term drops away. What's left is the original integral: a differential equation. Step three: solve from today's value, and out comes another bell, only wider. The pattern to keep: differentiate under the integral, get an equation, solve.

A glimpse ahead: set the dial to one half. Over the whole line, this wider bell's area is pinned down exactly. Divide by that, and the normal distribution's density has total area one. As the course text says, probability theory uses it all the time. So the pi inside every bell curve is the shadow of gamma at one half. One substitution, one extra dimension, one circle: that's the whole story.
