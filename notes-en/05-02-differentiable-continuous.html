Motivation

  1. Differentiable and continuous: joining up, but maybe not smoothly.
  2. Problem set, question 3: is this function differentiable at 0?
  3. The two pieces meet at the origin, with no gap.
  4. A classmate writes: continuous at 0, therefore differentiable.
  5. Look closer: the left piece arrives climbing, the right leaves flat.
  6. Joined up, yes. Joined smoothly? That is the real question.
  7. Between these two words run two arrows, one each way.
  8. Last episode left this open: does differentiable force continuous?
  9. The classmate used the reverse arrow: continuous, so differentiable.
  10. Spoiler: the first arrow holds, and the proof is one line.
  11. The reverse arrow breaks, and in three different ways.
  12. One-sided derivatives will let us name each failure.

What the statement says

  1. Start with the finite increment formula from the course text.
  2. Read it like a receipt that itemizes the change Δy.
  3. Line one, f′(x₀)Δx: what the tangent line predicts.
  4. Line two, o(Δx): a leftover tiny even next to Δx.
  5. At Δx = 0 the receipt still balances: 0 = 0 + 0.
  6. Definition in disguise: Δy/Δx = f′(x₀) + α, α → 0; times Δx.
  7. Theorem 5.1: differentiable at x₀ implies continuous at x₀.
  8. Continuous at x₀ means: as Δx → 0, also Δy → 0.
  9. Differentiable asks more: Δy/Δx must settle to a limit.
  10. So differentiable is the stronger promise, sitting inside continuous.
  11. The course text adds: differentiability is only sufficient for continuity.
  12. The witness is |x|: continuous at 0, but with a sharp corner.
  13. Definition 2 splits the limit into a right and a left version.
  14. Right derivative f′₊(x₀): Δx shrinks through positive values only.
  15. Left derivative f′₋(x₀): Δx shrinks through negative values only.
  16. Theorem 5.2 ties the two halves back together.
  17. The derivative exists exactly when both sides exist and agree.

Method / proof core

  1. Now the one-line proof: take the receipt and send Δx → 0.
  2. Both lines die: f′(x₀)·Δx → 0, and o(Δx) → 0 even faster.
  3. So Δy → 0, which is exactly continuity at x₀.
  4. Bonus: Δy dies at the pace of Δx, trapped inside a wedge.
  5. Continuity promises only Δy → 0; it says nothing about the rate.
  6. Δy may die too slowly, like √Δx: slower than any line.
  7. Or too violently, swinging between ±Δx forever.
  8. Or with a different slope on each side, like |Δx|.
  9. Three ways to be continuous but not differentiable.
  10. Failure one, the corner: f(x) = |x| at 0.
  11. Like a corridor's sharp turn: you walk in one way, out another.
  12. From the right, every secant has slope |h|/h = 1: f′₊(0) = 1.
  13. From the left, slope −1 every time: f′₋(0) = −1.
  14. Both exist, but 1 ≠ −1, so by Theorem 5.2, no f′(0).
  15. Failure two, the vertical tangent: f(x) = √x at 0.
  16. It is continuous at 0: √h → 0 as h → 0⁺.
  17. But the quotient is 1/√h → +∞: secants stand up vertical.
  18. Like a ladder too steep to walk: a tangent, but no finite slope.
  19. Note: 0 is an endpoint here, so only the right-hand quotient exists.
  20. Failure three, oscillation: x·sin(1/x), with f(0) = 0.
  21. Continuous at 0: it is squeezed between −|x| and |x|.
  22. Its difference quotient is just sin(1/h).
  23. Like a washer on spin cycle, it rattles in [−1, 1] and never settles.
  24. It hits 1 and −1 again and again near 0: no limit, no derivative.
  25. Three portraits: sides disagree, quotient explodes, quotient never settles.
  26. In all three the function is continuous: continuity is not enough.
  27. Back to question 3: piecewise joins are Theorem 5.2's home ground.
  28. Step one, continuity: both pieces tend to f(0) = 0.
  29. Why first? Not continuous would mean not differentiable, by Theorem 5.1.
  30. Step two, right side: (1 − cos h)/h → 0, so f′₊(0) = 0.
  31. Step three, left side: (h − 0)/h = 1, so f′₋(0) = 1.
  32. Step four, compare: 0 ≠ 1, so not differentiable, though continuous.
  33. So the classmate loses the marks: a corner hides at 0.
  34. The habit: continuity, right quotient, left quotient, compare.
  35. Careful: f′₊(x₀) is not lim f′(x) as x → x₀⁺; they can differ.
