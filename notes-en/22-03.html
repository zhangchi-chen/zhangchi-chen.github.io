22-03 Gauss's formula: flux, divergence, and the sources inside

Gauss's formula: flux, divergence, and the sources inside

1 · Motivation

Gauss's formula, and the sources hiding inside. Somewhere in your dorm room, a diffuser is quietly running. Wrap it in an imaginary bubble, and count only what slips across. Here's the question: can the skin alone reveal what's inside?

We've met this trick before: Green's formula, chapter 21, episode 5. One lap around the edge, and the whole region was known. Now raise the whole picture by one dimension. The boundary curve becomes a closed surface, a skin. Line integrals become flux through the skin. And Green's derivative combination becomes a new one: the divergence. And the slogan survives untouched: the boundary reads the interior.

2 · Statements

Let the solid V be bounded by a piecewise smooth closed two-sided surface S.
If P, Q, R have continuous first-order partial derivatives on V,
then ∭_V (∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z) dxdydz = ∯_S P dydz + Q dzdx + R dxdy,
with S taken on the outer side.
Left side: something summed over every point inside the solid.
Right side: only the skin of the solid is used.
Outer side: every normal points out, the convention of 22-02.
Piecewise smooth allows edges and corners, like a cube's.
Continuous first partials make the derivative safe to integrate.
D(x,y,z) = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z is called the divergence of the vector function
A = (P,Q,R), written div A.
So the formula reads: divergence inside equals flux outward.
Quick check: the field (x, y, z) on the unit ball.
Inside: divergence 3 times volume 4π/3 gives 4π.
On the sphere: strength 1 times area 4π, also 4π.

Theorem 22.5: take a solid wrapped in a piecewise smooth, closed, two sided surface. and the field's first order partial derivatives are continuous on it, then summing the derivatives inside equals the integral over the skin, with the surface taken on its outer side.

On the left, every single point inside gets counted. On the right, only the skin matters. Outer side means every arrow points outward, as chapter 22, episode 2 agreed. Piecewise smooth means edges and corners are fine; a cube is welcome. Continuous first derivatives keep the integrand well behaved.

This derivative sum has a name: it is called the divergence of the vector function of this field, and we write it div A. So the formula says: add up divergence inside, and get the outward flux. Test drive: the position field itself, on the unit ball. Inside, the divergence is 3 everywhere; times the volume, that's 4 pi. On the skin, strength 1 over area 4 pi: again 4 pi.

3 · Method

Why should this be true? Let's follow just one third of it. Picture a solid sandwiched between a floor surface and a ceiling surface. Shine a light from above: its shadow on the floor is the region D. Now pull out one thin vertical column. Just as in chapter 21, episode 4, integrate along the column first. The Newton Leibniz engine of chapter 9, episode 3 kicks in: top minus bottom. Now add up every column standing on the shadow. The first piece is exactly the flux through the top face, upper side. The bottom face looks downward, so its flux arrives with a minus sign. The side wall stands straight up, casts no shadow, and adds nothing. Top, bottom, and wall together make the whole closed surface. The other two terms repeat the trick sideways; add all three, and Gauss stands.

Now a real number: the flux of this field out of a cube of side a. Head on, that means six faces and six separate integrals. Gauss offers a shortcut: the divergence is tiny. And all six faces fold into a single integral over the cube. By symmetry the two pieces match, and together they make a to the fourth. Symmetry did half the job, and one integral replaced six. Grinding through all six faces lands on the same number.

So what is divergence really measuring at one point? Back to the diffuser: wrap a tiny ball around that point. By Gauss, what leaves the ball equals the divergence summed inside. The mean value theorem squeezes it into one value times the volume. Divide by the volume, and let the ball shrink onto the point. In the limit, the outflow per unit volume is the divergence at that point. The course text calls this its second definition; no coordinates needed. Divergence is a density, like the mass density in chapter 21, episode 7. For the position field, the ratio is 3 no matter how small the ball.

Positive divergence marks a source, where new fluid is born. Negative divergence marks a sink, like a shower drain swallowing water. Zero divergence everywhere gives a source free field. Then any closed bubble you draw inside has zero net flux. Count what leaves the room, and you've counted the diffusers inside.

Now feed the formula the plainest field of all: position itself. The divergence is 3, so three times the volume equals a surface integral. So the volume is one third of that surface integral. On the unit ball, a third of 4 pi gives four thirds pi. Its younger sibling lives in chapter 21, episode 5: Green's area formula. A half in the plane, a third in space: count the dimensions. Both say one thing: the boundary measures what it encloses.

4 · Outlook

A bonus: Maxwell's equations stand on a divergence pillar and a curl pillar; we built one.

Now a trap: the position field through an upper hemisphere, a dome. Quote Gauss straight away? The dome is open; it wraps up nothing. The fix: put a lid on it, like covering a cup before weighing what spilled. Closed now, Gauss applies: three times the half ball's volume. The lid lies flat on the floor, and nothing crosses it. Lift the lid off again: the dome keeps every bit of it.

Second trap: choose the inner side, and every arrow turns around. By the sign flip from chapter 22, episode 2, the unit ball gives negative 4 pi. Gauss only speaks for the outer side; the inner side costs a minus sign.

Chapter 22, episode 4 brings in Stokes and the curl, and the family is complete. The same theorem works in any dimension, and even on curved manifolds. Every conservation law says it: what changes inside is what crossed the boundary. It's the master pattern of field theory: the boundary reads the interior.
