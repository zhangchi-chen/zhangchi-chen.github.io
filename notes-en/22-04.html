Stokes's formula: the whole family in one frame

1 · Motivation

Stokes's formula, and the whole family in one frame. Here is the homework: a loop of six edges, sliced through a cube. The task: integrate these three terms once around the loop. Six edges, three terms each: eighteen little integrals to grind through. Before grinding, ask a better question: have we seen this before?

A group project: three earlier episodes each handed in one row. Row one, from chapter nine, episode three: Newton Leibniz on an interval. Integrate the derivative across the interval; only the two endpoints answer. Row two, from chapter twenty one, episode five: Green, on a plane region. Integrate over the region, and one lap around its edge gives the answer. Row three, from chapter twenty two, episode three: Gauss, on a solid. Add up divergence inside; the flux out through its skin answers. Notice the pattern: each boundary is exactly one dimension lower. Row four is still empty: a curved surface and the curve around its rim. A family photo, everyone with the same nose: one sentence, four costumes. Integrate a derivative over a body, and the boundary answers. Today the fourth relative walks into the photo: Stokes.

Here is the body, a curved surface, and its boundary, the rim. Two choices must agree: which side of the surface, which way round the rim. The course text settles it with one rule, the right-hand rule. Stand on the chosen side, head up, and walk: the surface stays on your left. Like a bottle cap: twist it the way the rim runs, and it moves toward the chosen side. Choose the upper side, look down from above, and the rim runs counterclockwise.

Here is Theorem twenty two point six, Stokes's formula, word for word. First the ground rules: the surface is smooth, its rim smooth in pieces. And the field must be smooth enough: continuous first partials, right up to the rim. On the left, an integral over the surface, taken on the chosen side. The surfaces and their sides were prepared in this chapter's first two episodes. On the right, a line integral, once around the rim. Those three brackets are the parts of one object: the curl of the field. In field language: the flux of the curl through the surface equals the circulation around the rim. Chapter twenty, episode three built the bridge: circulation in either kind of line integral. The course text also packs it into a determinant, easy to remember. Expand it along the top row, and the three brackets fall right out. And the fourth row is complete: a surface, its rim curve, and the curl.

A test drive: a swirling field, and the upper half of the unit sphere. Its curl is constant: straight up, size two. Pair the upper side with a counterclockwise rim: both sides come out two pi. Mismatch: lower side, same rim, and the surface says minus two pi. Same magnitude, opposite sign: a bug in the convention, not the arithmetic. Any cap on the same rim gives two pi, even the flat disc: only the rim matters. So when a surface integral looks hard, walk the rim instead.

3 · Method

Why is it true? Take the surface to be a graph over a plane region. Handle the P part alone first; the other two repeat the same move. Project down: the rim lands on the edge of the flat region. On the rim the height is fixed, so the integral moves down to the shadow. Green's formula, from chapter twenty one, episode five, takes over on the flat region. There is only one new ingredient: as you move in y, the height changes. The chain rule hands us one extra term, carried by the slope. And the normal on the upper side carries that same slope, so the two sides agree. Stokes is just Green in a curved costume, and the chain rule carries the curve. The other two parts repeat the same move; add the three pieces.

With Stokes in our pocket, back to the homework hexagon. For the surface, take the flat hexagon itself, tilted equally toward all three axes. The right-hand rule: seen from the tip of the normal, the loop runs counterclockwise. The key move: on this plane, the curl against the normal is one constant. With a constant integrand, the flux is just that constant times the area. It is a regular hexagon through six edge midpoints. And the loop integral is minus nine halves a cubed. Eighteen little edge integrals, traded for a single multiplication.

4 · Outlook

A promise from chapter ten, episode three, kept: the area formula's last stop. Take a flat loop floating in space, lying in a tilted plane. The goal: integrate this determinant once around the loop. The key move: the hidden field has curl exactly twice the normal. So the loop integral is twice the area the loop encloses. Now lay the plane flat, normal straight up. And it collapses to the old friend: area as half a loop integral. Met in chapter ten, proved by Green in chapter twenty one, generalized today. In vector form, its third component is exactly our formula. Its length is the area, and it points along the normal. The area formula from chapter ten, now in its most general shape.

Chapter twenty one, episode six moves into space as Theorem twenty two point seven. With a potential in space, work depends only on where you start and stop. The gradient: integrate it along any path, and only the two ends matter. The divergence: Gauss adds it up over a solid. The curl: Stokes adds it up over a surface. Three kinds of derivative, four formulas, and the chapter was one sentence all along.

Later, differential forms turn the four into literally one theorem. The derivative integrated over a body equals the integral over its boundary. On manifolds it is the generalized Stokes formula, the door to differential geometry. One sentence, four costumes: the whole family, finally in one frame.
