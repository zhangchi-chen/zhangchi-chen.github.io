21-01 The double integral: the definite integral moves onto the plane

The double integral: the definite integral moves onto the plane

1 · Motivation

The double integral: the definite integral moves onto the plane. How much lasagna sits on this tray, when the top is all bumps? Base times height breaks down: which height would you even use? We have met this puzzle before, one dimension down.

There we cut the base, added up rectangles, and took a limit. Let the longest piece shrink to nothing, and the limit is the definite integral. Today the same steps run on a tray instead of a strip.

Step one: lay a net of straight lines across the base. Just like slicing the lasagna into square portions. Each little cell has its own area, and we pick any point inside it. Step two: over each cell, swap the bumpy column for a flat-topped one. The bumpy column holds about height times base. Add up every flat column, and the total is the integral sum. Step three: refine the net until the cells shrink to nothing. The mesh is the widest cell's diameter, not a head count of cells. Thin strips alone fail: add as many as you like, they stay just as tall. In the limit, the sums give exactly the volume of the solid.

Here is the definition, word for word, from the course text. It starts from a closed, bounded region whose area we can measure. J is one fixed number, our candidate answer. However narrow the band you draw, a matching fineness must exist. Then every partition finer than that must behave. And however the sample points are chosen: every sum, not just one. Every one of those sums lands inside the band around J. Then the function is integrable, and J is its double integral. We write it with two integral signs, one for each dimension.

Side by side with episode nine one: the interval becomes a region. Little lengths along a line become little areas on the plane. And the largest piece must shrink, in both stories. Everything else, the band, the fineness, the free sample points, carries over. Only one thing is new: the region needs an area before anything starts.

An interval comes with its size built in; a plane set does not. The integral sum is built from areas, so areas had better exist. Lay a square net over the region; the squares fully inside give the inner area. Now count every square that even touches the region: the outer area. Refine the net: the inner area never drops, the outer area never climbs. The region has an area exactly when the inner and outer areas agree. Put another way: the boundary band can be squeezed to zero area. Any region fenced by smooth pieces, corners allowed, has an area. So every region in this course is safe.

First reading: for a nonnegative function, it is the volume under the roof. Second reading: set the function to one, and the roof flattens to height one. and its volume is just the area of the base. Area is no longer a separate build; it is one special integral.

3 · Method

Which functions are integrable? We quote two theorems and take them on trust. Theorem 21.6: continuity on such a region guarantees integrability. Theorem 21.7: a bounded function whose breaks fill zero area is still integrable. Say f is one below a parabola and two above it. It jumps all along that curve, so cover the curve with thin boxes. With more boxes the total area keeps halving, all the way to zero. In fact the graph of any continuous function has zero area. So finitely many jump curves do no harm: the function stays integrable.

Careful now: let f be one where both coordinates are rational, zero elsewhere. Every cell has both kinds of points, so a sum can come out one or zero. No single number can sit close to both, so this function is not integrable. That is the Dirichlet failure from episode nine two, one floor up. And yet it is bounded: its breaks fill the whole square, so boundedness alone fails.

The properties move over wholesale, starting with linearity. Additivity: split the region in two, and the two integrals add up. Monotonicity: a function that sits lower never has a larger integral. Absolute values integrate too, and cancellation can only lose size. The estimate: the solid sits between a low flat slab and a high flat slab. Why does it all transfer? The upper and lower sums from episode nine two run unchanged.

Property 7, the mean value formula, in the course text's own words. Think of a fluffy duvet in a box, patted flat without losing any volume. The bumpy top re-pours into one flat top, same base, same volume. By the estimate, the flat height lands between the lowest and the highest point. Because f is continuous on a connected region, some point hits that height exactly. Divide by the area, and that value is the average height over the region.

4 · Outlook

First number: integrate the product of the coordinates over the unit square, by definition. It is continuous, so it is integrable, and one family of grids is enough to find the value. Cut it into an even grid, and sample each cell at its upper right corner. Each cell adds a tiny product, and the whole double sum folds into one neat square. Double the grid again and again, and the sums slide toward one quarter. The limit is one quarter, and so is the double integral. Notice: one quarter is the one-variable answer, one half, squared. Why it splits like that is a story for episode twenty-one two.

Second number: we never compute it, we just trap it between two numbers. The region is a diamond with diagonals of twenty, so its area is two hundred. The plan: find how low and how high the integrand goes, then use the estimate. Each cosine squared stays between zero and one, so the integrand is pinned in a narrow window. Multiply by the area, and the integral is trapped between about 1.96 and 2. A window about two percent wide, and not one antiderivative needed.

So far we can define and estimate, but we cannot compute in general. Iterated integrals, in the next episode, will settle that debt. Looking ahead: the same three steps over a solid give the triple integral. A starred section of the course text pushes on to any number of dimensions. Later, measure theory turns zero-area sets into sets of measure zero. and skips the detour through area altogether. Cut, approximate, take the limit: the definite integral has moved onto the plane.
