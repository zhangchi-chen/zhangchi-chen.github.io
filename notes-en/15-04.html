15-04 The period need not be minimal: the surplus is a string of zeros

The period need not be minimal: the surplus is a string of zeros

1 · Motivation

The period need not be the smallest one; the extra is only a string of zeros. Two roommates set out to expand the absolute value of sine. One picks period pi, the other picks two pi. Their two tables of coefficients look quite different. In one of them, every second entry is zero. So which roommate got it wrong? Neither of them. Both answers describe the same series. The extra entries hold nothing but zeros.

Here is today's claim: a period that is too big does no harm. A preview: treat cosine as if it only repeated every four pi. Out comes a column of zeros, with one honest number in it. Picture a budget sheet in half weeks, for a lunch bill you pay once a week. Every other entry is zero, and that pattern gives away the weekly rhythm. So when is the bigger period safe, and what are the zeros telling us?

Stretch the period k times, and the frequency steps get that much finer. Every in between frequency gets a zero coefficient, automatically.

Rule two: if f repeats every half period, every odd coefficient vanishes. The even ones are just the half period coefficients, renumbered. Rule two is simply rule one with k equal to two.

Rule three runs backwards, and it leans on the convergence theorem. Odd coefficients all zero, plus convergence, and the period halves. At a jump, the function counts as its midpoint, as in episode fifteen two.

The course text's anchor: the absolute value of sine. Only the even harmonics survive. Three rules, then: enlarge freely, halving makes zeros, and zeros can be read backwards.

3 · Method

Demo one: take cosine, and pretend its period is four pi. With four pi as the period, the frequencies climb in half steps. Let's check one half frequency coefficient, the first. Look closely: the second half is the first half turned upside down. The areas cancel; that coefficient is zero. Every half frequency coefficient vanishes in exactly this way. The whole frequency cells hold the old answers for period two pi. Only one cell survives, the one for cosine itself, and it equals one. Wasted cells, but the right answer.

Now the general reason, done carefully for k equal to two. Suppose the function repeats every half period, and pick one coefficient. Key move: cut the integral at the halfway point. In the second half, slide everything back by half a period. The function doesn't notice the shift, but the cosine flips when n is odd. When n is odd, the two halves cancel exactly. When n is even, the halves add up to the half period coefficient. Odd entries vanish, even entries survive, and that's the whole mechanism. The sine coefficients follow the very same split. For general k the pattern is the same; here it is stated, and proved only for two.

Demo two: absolute sine, with period two pi. This is exactly the worked example in the course text. It's even, so only cosines appear, as in episode fifteen three. The key move: split the product into two sines. The result: zero at every odd n, and a negative number at every even one. On the chart, every odd harmonic is simply missing. The zeros give it away: the true period is pi. Just like the lunch sheet, the empty entries reveal the rhythm. So a period that's too big is a cheap way to find the smallest one. Once more: the extra zeros are information, not waste.

Now go backwards. If the odd coefficients all vanish, what follows? Step one: every term left in the series repeats every half period. So the sum of the series also repeats every half period. Step two needs the convergence theorem: the series really returns to the function. Only then does the function itself inherit the half period. Without it, which step breaks? The one from the series back to the function. Coefficients are integrals, and an integral can't see a single point. So the series might be describing a different function. The convergence theorem settles it: at points of continuity, the series gives the function.

Careful, though. This door only opens outward. Making the period bigger is always safe, as long as it's a true period. Shrinking is not safe: pi is simply not a period of cosine. Force it anyway, and the formula only reads a window of length pi. What it builds is the series of that one window, copied over and over. This window quietly builds absolute cosine, whose average is not zero. Slide the window over, and the average changes to zero. Two windows, two answers. The formula has stopped talking about cosine. With a true period, rule one gives the same answer from every window. Keeping only the even entries describes yet another function: f averaged with its half shift. For cosine, that average is zero everywhere; nothing survives. Shrinking swaps in a different function. Only enlarging is safe.

4 · Outlook

Think back to episode fifteen three, where we chose an extension's symmetry. Today we chose the period instead, the same kind of freedom. The coefficient formulas don't care how we dress the function up. But our bookkeeping leaves fingerprints, written in the pattern of zeros. First use: sine squared shows only even harmonics, so its period is pi. Second use: to add a two pi signal and a four pi signal, compute both with four pi. Next episode, today's block for k equal to three becomes a sieve.

A glimpse ahead: in signal processing, this is called oversampling. Record at a surplus rate, and the zeros reveal the true bandwidth. Surplus data buys certainty. Enlarge the period freely. The surplus is only zeros, and the zeros talk.
