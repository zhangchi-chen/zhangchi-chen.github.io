12-03 Ratio and root tests: how much of the term survives

Ratio and root tests: how much of the term survives

1 · Motivation

Two tests, one question: how much of each term survives? Homework hands you a cube over a power of two. Last episode taught us to find a benchmark and compare. But which one? The top and the bottom are in a tug of war. New idea: let each term face its own neighbour. Divide a term by the one before, and the mess cancels down. Far out, each term keeps about half of the one before.

Think of a phone battery that loses half its charge every hour. After a few terms, a geometric series holds it down from above. So it converges, and nobody had to guess a benchmark. That trick has a name, the ratio test, and it has a sibling, the root test. Today we find out why they work, where they go quiet, and which one wins.

2 · Statements

Theorem 12.7, the ratio test, also called d'Alembert's test.
First, a positive-term series: every term is above zero.
Past N₀, every ratio stays at or below one fixed q < 1.
The ceiling q is fixed; it may not creep up toward 1.
Then the series converges.
But if every ratio past N₀ is at least 1, terms never shrink.
Then the series diverges.
Corollary 1, the limit form: compute one limit q, then read.
Below 1 converges; above 1, or +∞, diverges.
Exactly 1? The corollary says nothing at all.
Theorem 12.8, the root test, also called Cauchy's test.
The n-th root is the average factor per step: uₙ = rⁿ.
Roots past N₀ at most some l < 1: converges.
Roots at least 1 mean terms at least 1: it diverges.
Corollary 2 needs no limit, only the limit superior.
The limit superior is the top level the roots keep returning to.
Limit superior below 1: converges, even while the roots keep jumping.
Limit superior above 1: infinitely many terms exceed 1, so it diverges.
And a remark that ranks the two tests.
A ratio limit q forces the root limit to be q too.
Whenever the ratio limit decides, the root decides too, and more.

Here it is in full: the ratio test, also named after d'Alembert. First, we only deal with positive terms. After some index, every ratio stays under one fixed ceiling below one. That ceiling is nailed in place; it is not allowed to creep up toward one. Then the series converges. But if no ratio ever dips below one, the terms never shrink. Then the series diverges.

The limit form: work out one limit, then just read the answer. Under one, it converges; over one, even infinity, it diverges. And exactly one? The corollary stays completely silent.

Now the root test, also named after Cauchy. The nth root tells you the average factor each step multiplies in. Roots held under a fixed level below one: it converges. Roots of at least one mean terms of at least one, so it diverges.

Corollary two drops the limit and keeps only the limit superior. The limit superior is the highest level the roots keep coming back to. If that top level is below one, it converges, jumps and all. Above one, infinitely many terms are bigger than one, so it diverges.

And a remark that settles which test is stronger. Whenever the ratio has a limit, the root has the very same limit. Whenever the ratio limit settles a series, the root settles it too.

3 · Method

Why does it work? Pull off the mask, and it is the comparison principle. First throw away the early terms; a finite handful never matters. Chain the steps: each one multiplies in one more factor of q. Result: every term sits under a geometric ceiling. Smaller than something convergent, so the comparison principle finishes the job. The root test unmasks even faster: a small root means a term under a geometric one. Both are last episode's comparison, aimed at a geometric series. So the promise from last episode is kept: geometric comparisons now take one line.

In everyday terms, q is the share of charge your battery keeps each hour. Keep less than all of it every hour, and the charge fades away geometrically. Keep all of it or more, and the charge never drops; the terms never die. That divergence half is just the necessary condition from the partial sums episode.

Careful: a ratio under one at every single step is not enough. For the harmonic series, the share it keeps climbs toward a full hundred percent. And the harmonic series still diverges: the ceiling must be fixed, and below one.

Now two famous series, and both tests go quiet. Their ratios both creep up to exactly one. Roots too: both drift up to one as well. Same readings, opposite endings: one converges, the other diverges. When the limit is exactly one, the limit forms cannot decide. Why? Plot on a log scale, and every geometric decay becomes a straight line. The two curves flatten out, and sooner or later rise above every such line. They fade too slowly; no geometric series can hold them down. At one, the geometric lens is too coarse: the whole p family piles up there.

Now the course text's own showdown series, with a sign that flips. Odd terms carry a one on top, even terms a three: a zigzag. The ratios take turns: three halves, then one sixth, again and again. No limit, no fixed ceiling under one: the ratio test is stuck. Roots: the root of the top, a one or a three, drifts to one. So the root heads to one half, below one: it converges. Why the root wins: it averages the zigzag factors into one half per step. That is the remark at work: a ratio limit forces a root limit, not the other way round.

Another course text series, where the ratio keeps swinging back and forth. Each step multiplies by one factor, then the other, taking turns. Think of your weekly commute: the worst delay and the best one keep coming back. Those are the shadow lines from chapter seven: the limit superior and the limit inferior. If even the worst ratio is below one, it converges. If even the best ratio is above one, it diverges. But if the two lines straddle one, the ratio test cannot decide. Remember this band; in chapter fourteen the limit superior gets a whole theorem.

Here is a pocket card: three questions, in this order. Factorials or powers like three to the n? Reach for the ratio test. Is the whole term raised to the nth power? The root test peels it right off. And if either test lands on one, step outside the geometric world. That is where the next episode takes over, with p series and Raabe's test.

Your turn: pause the video and decide these two. First one: factorials, so the ratio; it lands on a quarter, so it converges. Second one: an nth power downstairs, so the root; it lands on one half, converges.

4 · Outlook

First use: prove a sequence dies out by adding it up. Factorials against powers: the ratio lands on one over e, below one. So it converges, and for free, factorials lose to powers in the limit. Second use: a whole family with a parameter; the ratio sinks to zero. However large you make it, the factorial wins and the series converges. Later, in numerical analysis, q comes back in fixed point iteration. Each round, the error keeps the same fraction of itself; it is the very same picture.

Both tests ask the same thing: how much of each term survives. Under one it fades, over one it never lets go, and at exactly one the tests fall silent. When the ratio wobbles, the root test can still see the way through.
