07-01 Completeness of the reals: six keys, one door

Completeness of the reals: six keys, one door

1 · Motivation

Six keys, one door, and behind it a line with no holes. Six roommates hold six different keys, yet every key opens the same dorm door. The door is the completeness of the reals, and the keys are six theorems.

Back in chapter two, a bounded sequence that only climbs was promised a limit. Try the decimals of root two, cut off one digit further each time. Every term is a fraction, they never go down, and they all stay under two. Where does it land? Among the real numbers, right on root two. But root two is no fraction, so among the rationals the sequence lands nowhere. Same sequence, same conditions, and yet two different answers. The difference is not in the sequence; it is in the line underneath.

Picture the rationals as a long street where one house number is missing. The courier gets closer and closer, but that door simply does not exist. The reals leave no gap anywhere, and that is what completeness means. No gaps is only a picture; the course text makes it precise in six ways.

2 · Statements

Key 1: the Supremum Principle, Theorem 1.1.
Upper bounds come in crowds, and among them there is a smallest one.
That smallest upper bound is the supremum, sup S; the set leans on it.
Mirror image: a lower bound guarantees a greatest lower bound, the infimum.
Key 2: the Monotone Convergence Theorem, Theorem 2.9.
A walk that only climbs, inside a bounded corridor, must settle down.
Rising or falling, both work: bounded and monotone means convergent.
Its first words matter: “in the real number system.”
Key 3: the Nested Interval Theorem, Definition 1 and Theorem 7.1.
Closed intervals, each inside the last, with lengths shrinking to zero.
Such a sequence traps exactly one real point, ξ.
Left and right endpoints both converge to that same point ξ.
Drop “closed”: the open intervals (0, 1/n) shrink, yet trap no point.
Key 4: the Heine–Borel Finite Covering Theorem, Theorem 7.3.
An open cover: open intervals, with every point of S inside at least one.
The cover may contain infinitely many intervals.
Over a closed interval [a, b], finitely many of them already do the job.
From infinitely many local pieces to a finite list: that is its power.
Key 5, first half: the Weierstrass Cluster Point Theorem, Theorem 7.2.
A cluster point: every neighbourhood of it holds infinitely many points of S.
Any bounded infinite set has one: a crowd in a small room must bunch up.
Key 5, second half: the Bolzano–Weierstrass compactness theorem, Theorem 2.10.
A bounded sequence may never settle, yet some subsequence always converges.
Same crowding idea as the cluster point, now told with sequences.
Key 6: the Cauchy Convergence Criterion, Theorem 2.11.
Cauchy sequence: far enough out, any two terms lie within ε of each other.
Shrink ε: the later terms still huddle, and no limit is ever mentioned.
So: a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy; no target needed.
Six statements, six pictures, one fact: no holes in the line.

Key one: the Supremum Principle, Theorem one point one. Upper bounds come in crowds, and among them one is the smallest. That smallest upper bound is the supremum, and the set leans right against it. Flip it over: a floor under the set guarantees a highest floor, the infimum.

Key two: the Monotone Convergence Theorem, Theorem two point nine. A walk that only climbs, trapped in a corridor, has to settle somewhere. Climbing or falling makes no difference: bounded and one way means it converges. Notice the opening words: in the real number system.

Key three: the Nested Interval Theorem, Theorem seven point one. Closed intervals, each tucked inside the last, their widths shrinking to nothing. A sequence like that traps exactly one real point. The left ends climb, the right ends fall, and they meet at that point. Make them open, from zero to one over n, and no point survives in all of them.

Key four: the Heine Borel Finite Covering Theorem, Theorem seven point three. An open cover is a pile of open intervals, with every point of the set inside one. Think of a blanket stitched from infinitely many patches. Over a closed interval, a finite handful of them already does the whole job. From endlessly many local pieces down to a short finite list: that is its power.

Key five, first half: the Weierstrass Cluster Point Theorem, Theorem seven point two. Shrink the neighbourhood as much as you like; it still holds endlessly many points. Pack infinitely many points into a bounded stretch, and somewhere they bunch up. Key five, second half: the Bolzano Weierstrass compactness theorem. A bounded sequence may bounce forever, yet some thread of its terms always settles. It is the same crowding idea as before, retold in the language of sequences.

Key six: the Cauchy Convergence Criterion, Theorem two point one one. A Cauchy sequence: far enough along, any two terms sit within epsilon of each other. Make epsilon smaller, the later terms still huddle, and no target is ever named. So converging and being Cauchy are the same, and no target is needed. Six statements, six pictures, and underneath them one fact: the line has no holes.

3 · Method

Which key fits which job? This table is the takeaway of the whole episode. Need to show a supremum or infimum exists? Reach for the first key. A sequence built step by step that only moves one way? Second key, as in episode two oh five. Hunting for a special point by halving again and again? The third key. You know something near every point and need one finite argument? The fourth key. Remember episode four oh three: closed and bounded leaves no escape route. Same instinct. Pulling a settling thread out of a bounded sequence? Key five, as in episode two oh six. Need convergence but cannot guess the limit? Key six, as in episode two oh seven. Keys two, five and six arrived back in chapter two, their proofs held over for this chapter. Rule of thumb: let the shape of the task choose the key.

Now take all six keys down the rationals street, with its hole at root two. Key one: the fractions whose square is below two, bounded above by two. But no fraction is the smallest upper bound; the true supremum, root two, is missing. Key two: our climbing decimals are bounded, yet among the fractions they cannot land. Key three: the shrinking fraction intervals trap just one point, root two, no fraction. Key four: open intervals that stop just short of root two still cover every fraction from one to two. Yet any finite handful leaves some fractions uncovered right beside the hole. Key five: those decimals form a bounded infinite crowd that gathers only at root two. So no fraction is a cluster point, and no thread of terms settles on a fraction. Key six: the decimals agree further and further, so they are Cauchy, yet they reach no fraction. Six keys, six failures, one cause: the missing point at root two. Every one of them fails over the fractions; completeness is what makes them true.

Fine print: these open intervals, creeping toward zero, together cover zero to one. But any finite handful leaves a bare strip beside zero, so there is no finite subcover. So closedness carries weight: the theorem speaks of closed intervals, not open ones.

4 · Outlook

First job: each term built from the last. Does it settle? It only climbs and never passes two, so the second key guarantees a limit. Once the limit is known to exist, one small equation pins it at two. Second job: a running sum of shrinking sine terms, limit hard to guess. Key six needs no guess: the tail terms huddle ever closer, so it converges.

Next, episode seven oh two proves the six are one fact in six costumes. Then episode seven oh three pays the proofs owed since chapters two and four. Later, in real analysis, this whole chapter turns into a definition. A space earns the name complete when one of these properties holds. Fill in every hole of the rationals, and you get one rigorous way to build the reals. Six keys, one door, and behind it a line without a single hole.
