08-03 The second substitution: a trigonometric function hides inside the radical

The second substitution: a trigonometric function hides inside the radical

1 · Motivation

Today, a trig function is hiding inside a square root. Here is an integral that looks perfectly harmless. Its graph is the top half of a circle. First try the trick from the last episode: glue the differential. But gluing needs a spare x outside the root, and there is none.

So flip the plan: last time we read the integrand first, then named the new variable. Now we decide what x is made of, and undo it at the end. The root is a lock, and a trig identity is the key. Three shapes, three keys, like a shortcut sheet on your desk.

2 · Statements

Theorem 8.5, the second substitution, as the course text states it.
First clause: φ maps the t-interval J onto the whole x-interval I.
And it can be undone: x = φ(t) has an inverse function.
The original integral must already exist on I.
Then compute in t instead; suppose that gives G(t).
Send t back through the inverse: G(φ⁻¹(x)) + C answers on I.
The convenient form, also called substitution by replacement.
It is the same identity as 08-02, read in the opposite direction.

Here is the theorem, exactly as the course text says it. First, the new interval is carried onto the whole old interval. And the map can be run backwards: it has an inverse function. And the original integral must already exist. Then do the work in the new variable and find an answer there. Feed that answer back through the inverse, and the original integral is done. Plug in, integrate, plug back: also called substitution by replacement. It is the very same identity as last time, read backwards.

3 · Method

Arrow one: when a square loses something under the root, the sine key fits. Let the sine play x, and the root collapses. So we fence the sine into a stretch where it only climbs. Every height is hit exactly once, so the inverse is the arcsine. Bonus: the cosine is never negative here, so the root comes out clean.

Substitute, and the root is simply gone. A double angle formula finishes it, but in the wrong language. To translate back, sketch a little triangle in the margin. Pythagoras fills in the last side, and the cosine reads off. Translate back, and the answer is finally in x. Differentiate to check: the integrand comes right back.

Arrow two: the subtraction flips sides, and the secant takes over. Start with the right side: let the secant play x. The tangent is positive here, so the root comes out clean. The tangents cancel, and the gluing from last episode finishes the secant. A new sketch: the secant fixes two sides, and the tangent reads off. Translate back, and a stray constant melts into the new one. On the far left, use the other branch; the tangent turns negative. After the dust settles, the very same formula comes out.

Arrow three: a sum of squares, and the tangent identity fits. Try a squared sum; the tangent sweeps every real number exactly once. Everything simplifies to the same cosine square as arrow one. Sketch the triangle: the tangent fixes the legs, Pythagoras the rest. Multiply sine by cosine, and the roots multiply away. Translate back, and the answer is back in x.

Now the whole map, all in one picture. Square minus square: the sine key. Square plus square: the tangent key. Subtraction the other way round: the secant key. And with no root at all, no key is needed. Tape it above your desk: see the shape, grab the key.

Not every root comes from a square: here a square root meets a cube root. Take the least common multiple of the two degrees, which is 6. For positive x, both roots vanish at once. What remains is a rational function, finished in the partial fractions episode.

But why does the theorem insist the original integral exists? Because the proof borrows that antiderivative and runs the theorem from last episode. Two antiderivatives differ by a constant, so undoing the map recovers the original.

The course text says it plainly: this condition is not optional. The counterexample: a function that jumps right at zero. Substitute with the cube: smooth, invertible, covering the whole interval. On the new side, everything computes happily. Translating back predicts slope one at zero, but the function says zero. Every candidate is forced into that shape, so no antiderivative exists. The new side worked perfectly; the old side was empty all along.

The inverse matters too: let the sine run a full lap, and heights repeat. And where the cosine is negative, the root quietly flips sign. That is why every key is cut to a branch that only climbs.

4 · Outlook

Use one: the sine key explains a formula from the table. Use two: spot a sum of squares, reach for the tangent, done in seconds. Use three: later, the length of a parabola brings this shape back.

The two substitutions are one coin: glue in one direction, replace in the other. With definite integrals later, the limits will do the translating for you. These keys open exactly three locks; rational functions need a systematic pipeline. After that, the toolbox is in principle complete.
