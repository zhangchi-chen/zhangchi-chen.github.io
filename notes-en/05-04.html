Chain rule and inverse functions: differentiate nested dolls from the outside in

1 · Motivation

Chain rule and inverse functions: nested dolls, opened from the outside in. Late night, deadline close: a sine wrapped around a square. Classmate A says: sine turns into cosine, and that's it. Classmate B has the same thing, but with an extra factor in front. Same function, two answers, and at most one of them can be right.

Let the graph decide: the curve, and one point on it. Classmate A's slope, about 0.54, cuts straight across the curve. Classmate B's slope, about 1.08, hugs the curve perfectly. B wins. But where did that extra factor come from?

This is a nested doll: a square hiding inside a sine. Each layer has its own speed of change, and the speeds multiply. Today: why the speeds multiply, and where the obvious proof breaks. And a bonus: run a function backwards, and its slope turns upside down.

2 · Statements

Notation alert: here the course text calls the inner function φ, the outer f.
In episode 04-08 it was the other way round: f inside, g outside.
Here is the chain rule, Theorem 5.8, exactly as the text states it.
Clause one: φ is differentiable at x₀; the inner layer has a rate.
Clause two: f is differentiable at u₀ = φ(x₀), where the inner lands.
The outer function never sees x₀; it only sees u₀.
Conclusion: the composite's rate is f′(u₀) times φ′(x₀).
With numbers at x₀ = 1: squaring stretches a tiny piece by about 2.
Then sine scales it again, by about cos 1 ≈ 0.54.
Overall about 2 × 0.54 ≈ 1.08: exactly classmate B's slope.
Now the picture to remember: rates as gears.
The small gear spins twice per big turn: du/dx = 2.
Add a third gear, 1.5 times faster again: dy/du = 1.5.
Per pedal turn, the last gear spins 2 × 1.5 = 3 times.
Rates compose by multiplication: dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx).

Heads up: in this chapter the book calls the inner layer phi. Back in the composition episode, the letters were swapped. Here is the chain rule, word for word from the course text.

First: the inner layer must have a speed at the start. Second: the outer layer needs a speed exactly where the inner one lands. The outer layer never sees the start, only the landing spot. Conclusion: outer rate times inner rate.

Now with numbers: squaring stretches a tiny piece near one by about 2. Then the sine scales it again, by about 0.54. Overall, about 1.08: classmate B's slope, exactly as the graph said.

Here's the picture to keep: rates as bicycle gears. Each turn of the big gear spins the small one twice. Add a third gear, half again as fast. One pedal turn, and the last gear spins three times. Rates compose by multiplying. That is the whole chain rule.

3 · Method

Here comes the tempting short proof. Shrink the step, and each factor settles on its derivative. Done? Now look at that new denominator. What if it is zero?

It's zero whenever the inner layer comes back to the same value. And it can happen again and again, as close to the point as you like. No step is small enough to dodge it. The proof divides by zero.

The extreme case: a constant inner layer. The denominator is always zero. Yet the composite is constant too, and the rule gives zero, correctly. The theorem is fine. It's the proof that's broken. It's the same trap from the composition episode: an inner layer parked on one value.

The honest fix: multiply, and never divide. Take the secant slope of the outer layer. At the centre, it has a hole. Fill the hole with the derivative, and the patched slope is continuous. So the outer change is the patched slope times the inner change. Even at the centre both sides are zero. No division anywhere.

The inner layer gets the same patch, with its own patched slope. Plug the inner into the outer, and the two patches simply multiply. Continuity passes through composites, as the composition episode showed. At the point, the factor is outer slope times inner slope. Nothing is ever divided by the inner change, so a constant inner layer is harmless.

Now a doll with three layers. Outer shell first: an exponential, its own rate. Next shell: a sine, whose rate is a cosine. Innermost: a square, with rate two x. Then multiply along the rail, all three factors, none skipped. Stop one layer too early, and the last factor goes missing. That's exactly classmate A's slip from the start. One factor per layer: count the dolls, count the factors.

The text writes both rules in Leibniz notation, which looks like fractions. Here the middle letters seem to cancel, like a fraction. It works, but not because of arithmetic. The theorem makes it true. Each symbol is one derivative, not a ratio. Cancelling just helps you remember. When the middle letters don't match, there's nothing to cancel. When each letter hooks into the next, the whole rail collapses to one.

One last tool: inverse functions, the gear train run backwards. Here is the inverse function rule, word for word. Example: the sine, on its rising stretch, run backwards is the arcsine. Going forward, the tangent's slope is about 0.87. Going backward, the slope is about 1.15: flipped upside down. Swap the axes, and rise and run trade places. Why must the forward slope be nonzero? At the top, the tangent lies flat. In the mirror, flat becomes vertical, and its slope would need dividing by zero.

Why strictly monotone? So that every input has exactly one way back. So neither step can vanish alone, and both shrink together. Back to arcsine: one over the cosine. The cosine is positive on that stretch, so we take the positive root. At one half, it gives about 1.15, matching the mirrored tangent.

4 · Outlook

In the cafeteria, a coffee spill spreads, its radius growing half a centimetre each second. Area depends on radius, radius on time: multiply the two rates. At four centimetres, about 12.6 square centimetres per second.

Use two: next episode, these two rules grow three mother formulas into the whole table. For instance, the logarithm reverses the exponential, so its slope is one over x. A promise for episode seven: parametric slopes will use both rules, without cancelling shortcuts.

Later, implicit differentiation pushes both sides of an equation through this rule. With several variables, it becomes a tree, one term for each path. So: open from the outside, one factor per layer, and never divide by the inner change. Forward, rates multiply. Backward, they flip.
