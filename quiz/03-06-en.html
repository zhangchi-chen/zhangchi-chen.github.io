As x → 0, o(x²)·O(1/x) simplifies to:

A. o(x)B. O(x)C. o(x³)

A. by o(f)·O(g) = o(fg) with f = x², g = 1/x: o(x²·1/x) = o(x).

As x → 0, x·o(1/x²) equals:

A. o(x)B. o(1/x)C. O(1/x²)

B. by x·o(f(x)) = o(x·f(x)): o(x·1/x²) = o(1/x).

The equals sign in “o(f) + o(f) = o(f)” means:

A. the two classes are mutually equalB. the two o(f)'s are the same functionC. containment ⊆: the sum of any two members of o(f) stays in o(f)

C. function-class equations read left to right as membership/containment, not symmetric equality.

In general, equivalent substitution is legitimate…

A. inside products and quotientsB. inside sums and differencesC. anywhere in an expression

A. the correction factor 1 + o(1) passes through products and quotients; in differences the leading parts may cancel.

Why does naive substitution in (tan x − sin x)/x³ give the wrong answer 0?

A. tan x is not equivalent to xB. the leading parts cancel, leaving o(x), and o(x)/x³ is undeterminedC. the limit does not exist

B. tan x − sin x is only known to lie in o(x); the remainder decides, and the true limit is 1/2.

For u = x·sin(1/x) and v = x as x → 0, which conclusion holds?

A. u ~ vB. u ∈ O(v), even though lim u/v does not existC. u is not an infinitesimal

B. |u| ≤ |v| already gives u ∈ O(v) with no ratio limit needed — exactly where the function-class language is more robust.
