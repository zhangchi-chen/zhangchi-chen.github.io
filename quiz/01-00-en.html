To compare aₙ = 1 + (−1)ⁿ/n with bₙ = 1, why does “comparing sizes” fail?

A. Every term of aₙ is greater than 1.B. Term by term, the inequality flips back and forth.C. aₙ has no limit.

B. a₁ = 0 < 1, a₂ = 1.5 > 1, a₃ = 2/3 < 1 … so instead one compares the distance |aₙ − 1| = 1/n and the long-run trend.

“Approximate inputs give approximate outputs” names which concept?

A. ContinuityB. The derivativeC. Completeness

A. Continuity is the hope that limits pass through functions: under x², the inputs 2.9, 2.99, 2.999 give 8.41, 8.9401, 8.994001.

f(t) = 100 + t versus g(t) = t²: in the long run, who leads is decided by…

A. their values at t = 10.B. which formula is shorter.C. their growth rates, i.e. the derivatives.

C. At t = 10, f still leads (110 versus 100); but from 10 to 11, g gains 21 while f gains only 1 — from t = 11 on, g leads (121 versus 111).

At x = 0.1, what does the fit 1 + x + x² give for 1/(1 − x) = 1.1111…?

A. 1.11, with remainder 0.00111…B. 1.1, with no remainderC. 1.111, exact equality

A. 1 + 0.1 + 0.01 = 1.11; the gap 1.1111… − 1.11 = 0.00111… is exactly the remainder.

Why does the sequence 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, … need the completeness of the reals?

A. Every term is irrational.B. Its limit √2 is not rational and has nowhere to land in ℚ.C. It diverges to ∞.

B. Every term is a finite decimal, hence rational, but the target √2 is not; completeness guarantees that ℝ has no such hole.

For aₙ = (−1)ⁿ·n/(n + 1), the upper limit is

A. 0B. 4/5C. 1

C. The even terms 2/3, 4/5, 6/7, … tend to 1 (never exceeding it), while the odd terms tend to −1; so limsup = 1 and liminf = −1.
