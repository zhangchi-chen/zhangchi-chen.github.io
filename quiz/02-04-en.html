Given 0 ≤ cₙ ≤ 1 + 3/n, what can you conclude about cₙ?

A. cₙ → 1B. nothing about the limitC. cₙ → 0

B. The two bounds tend to 0 and 1 respectively; different limits, so squeezing yields no conclusion.

Together with the lower bound 1 − 1/n² ≤ cₙ, is the upper bound 1 + 3/n good enough?

A. yes: both bounds tend to 1B. no: it is far cruder than the lower boundC. one must replace 3/n by 3/n²

A. The two bounds may differ in precision; only their limits must agree.

Why can't the product rule give (−1)ⁿ · n/(n²+1) → 0?

A. n/(n²+1) has no limitB. the product is unboundedC. (−1)ⁿ has no limit

C. The product rule needs both factors convergent; (−1)ⁿ oscillates. Squeeze instead with |cₙ| ≤ 1/n.

Which two bounds squeeze xₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ 1/√(n²+i)?

A. n/√(n²+n) and n/√(n²+1)B. 1/√(n²+n) and 1/√(n²+1)C. 0 and 1

A. Replace every term by the smallest one, then by the largest; both bounds tend to 1.

The key inequality behind [nα]/n → α is?

A. [nα] ≥ nαB. nα − 1 < [nα] ≤ nαC. [nα] ≤ nα − 1

B. Divide nα − 1 < [nα] ≤ nα by n; the two resulting bounds both tend to α.

Which of these limits genuinely requires squeezing?

A. (2n+1)/(n+3)B. (n+1)/nC. sin n / n

C. sin n has no limit, so the arithmetic rules fail; |sin n / n| ≤ 1/n squeezes it to 0.
